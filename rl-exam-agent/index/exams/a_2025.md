## Q1 (30 pts) — Optimal stopping on an urn: a martingale betting game
**Topics:** MDP formalism, optimal stopping, Q-values of a fixed policy, martingale / fair game, optimal policy | **Pillar:** Planning | **Difficulty:** 4
**Maps to:** lecture_03, lecture_04
**Statement (English translation):**
A sequential decision problem. An urn holds $g$ green balls and $b$ blue balls. At each step $t$, as long as the urn has at least two balls, a ball is drawn uniformly at random from the urn. The agent then has two actions:
- **stop**: the agent earns $1$ if the drawn ball is blue and $0$ if it is green; the game then ends.
- **continue**: the agent earns $0$ and observes the color of the drawn ball; that ball leaves the urn and play continues without it.

If the urn has only a single ball, the agent earns $1$ if that ball is blue and $0$ if it is green (forced). The agent's objective is the sum of rewards until the game ends.

a. Write the MDP of the problem formally.

b. Let $\pi_{\text{cont}}$ be the policy that always plays **continue**. Compute its Q-values from the initial state. (Recall the initial state has $g$ green and $b$ blue balls.)

c. What is the optimal value function when the urn has $g'$ green and $b'$ blue balls? Hint: the recursion $f(x,y)=\tfrac{y}{x+y}f(x,y-1)+\tfrac{x}{x+y}f(x-1,y)$ with boundary $f(n,0)=0,\ f(0,n)=1$ has solution $f(x,y)=\tfrac{x}{x+y}$; the state $(b',g')$ has $b'$ blue and $g'$ green.

d. Change the **continue** action so the agent earns $q>0$ (instead of $0$). What is the optimal policy now?

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) The MDP of the urn game**

**What is asked.** Translate the urn game into the components of an MDP: states, actions, rewards, transitions, initial state and horizon. The answer is a definition, not a number.

**Tool and why.** Modelling a story as an MDP (cheat sheet §12): the state is whatever we must know at decision time to predict the future. Here the future depends only on how many balls of each colour are still in the urn, not on the order in which they left, so the two counts are the state. The game ends after finitely many steps, so no discount is needed (cheat sheet §3).

**Definitions.** We write a state as $(b',g')$: $b'$ blue balls and $g'$ green balls are left (blue first, the same order the statement uses in part (c)). Let $n=b'+g'$ be the number of balls left. We add an absorbing terminal state $\bot$, so that "the game ends" is an ordinary transition.

**Solution.**

Step 1, states: all pairs $(b',g')$ with $b'\in\{0,\dots,b\}$ and $g'\in\{0,\dots,g\}$ and at least one ball, $n\ge1$, plus $\bot$. The empty urn is never reached, because the game ends when one ball is left.

Step 2, initial state: $s_0=(b,g)$, the full urn. We assume $b+g\ge2$; otherwise there is no decision at all.

Step 3, actions: when $n\ge2$ the actions are $\text{stop}$ and $\text{continue}$. When $n=1$ there is no choice: the last ball pays, so we model a single forced action.

Step 4, rewards and transitions, by the rules of the game. The agent chooses before it sees the drawn ball, so the reward of $\text{stop}$ is the expected payoff, the probability that a uniform draw is blue:

| state and action | probability | next state | reward |
|---|---|---|---|
| stop at $(b',g')$ | $1$ | $\bot$ | $\tfrac{b'}{n}$ |
| continue, a blue ball leaves | $\tfrac{b'}{n}$ | $(b'-1,g')$ | $0$ |
| continue, a green ball leaves | $\tfrac{g'}{n}$ | $(b',g'-1)$ | $0$ |
| forced, when $n=1$ | $1$ | $\bot$ | $b'$ |

In $\bot$ the agent stays forever with reward $0$. All rewards are non-negative.

Step 5, horizon: every $\text{continue}$ removes one ball, so there are at most $b+g-1$ continues and the game ends after at most $b+g$ decisions. The horizon is finite and $\gamma=1$.

**In words.** Only what is left in the urn matters for the future, and the only reward arrives at the moment the game ends.

**Sanity check.** With one blue and one green ball, stopping pays $\tfrac12$ in expectation. Continuing leaves one ball, which is blue with probability $\tfrac12$, so it also pays $\tfrac12$ on average. The model reproduces what the story says.

**What to write in the exam.**

- States: $(b',g')$, the blue and green counts, plus a terminal $\bot$; start at $s_0=(b,g)$.
- Actions: stop or continue when $n\ge2$; at $n=1$ a forced payoff $b'$.
- Stop: reward $\tfrac{b'}{n}$, go to $\bot$.
- Continue: reward $0$; go to $(b'-1,g')$ with probability $\tfrac{b'}{n}$ and to $(b',g'-1)$ with probability $\tfrac{g'}{n}$.
- Horizon: finite, $\gamma=1$.

*Verified: the MDP coded in Python with exact fractions reproduces every value in parts (b)–(d).*

**(b) Q-values of the always-continue policy**

**What is asked.** Two numbers for the policy $\pi_{\text{cont}}$: the value of stopping at $s_0$ and the value of continuing at $s_0$ (and then always continuing). The answer is two formulas in $b$ and $g$.

**Tool and why.** Bellman equation of a fixed policy (cheat sheet §1 and §2): $Q^\pi(s,a)$ is the immediate reward plus the expected value of $\pi$ from the next state. So we need $V^{\pi_{\text{cont}}}$ on every state, which we get by induction on the number of balls, because each step removes exactly one ball.

**Definitions.** Let $W(b',g')$ be the value of $\pi_{\text{cont}}$ from state $(b',g')$, that is $W=V^{\pi_{\text{cont}}}$. We introduce it because $Q^{\pi_{\text{cont}}}(s_0,\text{continue})$ is the expected value of $W$ after the first removal.

**Solution.**

Step 1, the value of stopping: $\text{stop}$ ends the game at once and pays $\tfrac{b}{b+g}$ in expectation, by the table of part (a). What the policy would do later is irrelevant, so

$$Q^{\pi_{\text{cont}}}(s_0,\text{stop})=\tfrac{b}{b+g}.$$

Step 2, **Claim.** For every state with $n\ge1$ balls, $W(b',g')=\tfrac{b'}{n}$. The proof is by induction on $n$, upward from $1$.

**Base.** For $n=1$ the payoff is forced and equals $b'$, which is $\tfrac{b'}{1}$.

**Hypothesis.** For some $n\ge2$, every state $(b'',g'')$ with $n-1$ balls has $W(b'',g'')=\tfrac{b''}{n-1}$.

**Step.** Under $\pi_{\text{cont}}$ the reward is $0$ and one ball leaves, so by the Bellman equation of the policy, and then by the hypothesis in the second row:

$$\begin{aligned}
W(b',g')&=\tfrac{b'}{n}W(b'-1,g')+\tfrac{g'}{n}W(b',g'-1)\\
&=\tfrac{b'}{n}\cdot\tfrac{b'-1}{n-1}+\tfrac{g'}{n}\cdot\tfrac{b'}{n-1}\\
&=\tfrac{b'(b'-1+g')}{n(n-1)}\\
&=\tfrac{b'(n-1)}{n(n-1)}\\
&=\tfrac{b'}{n}.
\end{aligned}$$

The fourth row uses $b'+g'-1=n-1$. If $b'=0$ or $g'=0$ the matching term has weight $0$ and the computation still holds.

Step 3, the value of continuing: the first $\text{continue}$ pays $0$ and moves to a random next state, after which $\pi_{\text{cont}}$ keeps continuing. This is exactly $W(s_0)$, so by the claim with $n=b+g$:

$$\boxed{Q^{\pi_{\text{cont}}}(s_0,\text{stop})=\tfrac{b}{b+g}}$$

$$\boxed{Q^{\pi_{\text{cont}}}(s_0,\text{continue})=\tfrac{b}{b+g}}$$

**In words.** Removing balls uniformly at random until one is left makes the last ball a uniformly random ball of the original urn, by symmetry. So it is blue with probability $\tfrac{b}{b+g}$, exactly like the ball drawn right now.

**Sanity check.** Take $b=1$ and $g=2$. Continuing reaches $(1,1)$ with probability $\tfrac23$ (a green ball leaves) and then keeps the blue ball with probability $\tfrac12$; otherwise the blue ball is gone. The win probability is $\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac12$, which is $\tfrac13$, the same as $\tfrac{b}{b+g}$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Stop: the game ends with expected reward $\tfrac{b}{b+g}$.
- Claim: $V^{\pi_{\text{cont}}}(b',g')=\tfrac{b'}{n}$, by induction on $n$ with the one-step Bellman equation of the policy.
- Conclusion: both Q-values at $s_0$ equal $\tfrac{b}{b+g}$.

*Verified: exact-fraction evaluation of $\pi_{\text{cont}}$ for all $b,g\le8$ gives $W=\tfrac{b'}{n}$ in every state; the example $b=1$, $g=2$ gives $\tfrac13$.*

**(c) The optimal value function**

**What is asked.** The optimal value $V^*$ of a state with $b'$ blue and $g'$ green balls. The answer is a formula, with a proof.

**Tool and why.** Bellman optimality equation of an optimal-stopping problem (cheat sheet §2 and §4): at each state compare the value of stopping with the value of continuing. The horizon is finite, so we prove the formula by induction on the number of balls, the same induction as in part (b). The hint's recursion is exactly the continue branch.

**Definitions.** No new notation: $V^*(b',g')$ is the optimal value and $n=b'+g'$.

**Solution.**

Step 1, Bellman optimality: for $n\ge2$ the agent takes the better of the two actions,

$$V^*(b',g')=\max\Big\{\tfrac{b'}{n},\ \tfrac{b'}{n}V^*(b'-1,g')+\tfrac{g'}{n}V^*(b',g'-1)\Big\},$$

and for $n=1$ the value is the forced payoff $b'$.

Step 2, **Claim.** For every state, $V^*(b',g')=\tfrac{b'}{n}$, and when $n\ge2$ both actions attain it. The proof is by induction on $n$, upward from $1$.

**Base.** For $n=1$ the value is $b'$, which is $\tfrac{b'}{1}$.

**Hypothesis.** For some $n\ge2$, every state $(b'',g'')$ with $n-1$ balls has $V^*(b'',g'')=\tfrac{b''}{n-1}$.

**Step.** By the hypothesis, the continue branch is the same expression as in part (b), Step 2, so it equals $\tfrac{b'}{n}$. The stop branch is also $\tfrac{b'}{n}$. The maximum of two equal numbers is that number, so $V^*(b',g')=\tfrac{b'}{n}$, and both actions are optimal.

Step 3, link to the hint: with $x=b'$ and $y=g'$ the continue branch is the hint's recursion, and its solution $f(x,y)=\tfrac{x}{x+y}$ is our formula. **Remark.** The boundary values that fit this solution are $f(n,0)=1$ (only blue balls, a sure win) and $f(0,n)=0$ (no blue ball). The statement prints the two boundary values the other way round; the recursion and the solution formula are the consistent part, and they are what we used.

$$\boxed{V^*\big((b',g')\big)=\dfrac{b'}{b'+g'}}$$

**In words.** This is a fair game: the fraction of blue balls is a martingale, because after a uniform removal its expected value equals its current value (that is the computation in the step). So no stopping rule can do better or worse than stopping now; every policy has value $\tfrac{b'}{n}$.

**Sanity check.** If $b'=0$ the value is $0$: no blue ball can ever be drawn. If $g'=0$ the value is $1$: every draw is blue. For the state $(2,3)$ the value is $\tfrac25$, and a simulation of a policy that stops at random times gives $0.4006$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Bellman: $V^*$ is the larger of $\tfrac{b'}{n}$ and the continue value.
- Induction on $n$: if $V^*$ is the blue fraction one level down, the continue value is $\tfrac{b'}{n}$, the same as stopping.
- Answer: $V^*\big((b',g')\big)=\tfrac{b'}{b'+g'}$, and both actions are optimal everywhere (fair game).

*Verified: exact DP with fractions for all $b',g'\le8$ gives $V^*=\tfrac{b'}{n}$ with stop and continue equal in every state; a Monte-Carlo run of a random stopping rule from $(2,3)$ gives $0.4006$ against $0.4$.*

**(d) A paid continue action**

**What is asked.** The optimal policy when every $\text{continue}$ pays $q>0$. The answer is a policy, with its value and a proof.

**Tool and why.** Guess and verify by induction on $n$ (cheat sheet §2 and §4): guess the value of "always continue", then check that it satisfies the Bellman optimality equation and that continue wins the maximum. Induction fits because each step removes one ball.

**Definitions.** Let $V_q^*(b',g')$ be the optimal value of the modified game. The subscript $q$ separates it from the value of part (c).

**Solution.**

Step 1, the guess: always continuing collects $q$ on each of the $n-1$ continues, and the forced last payoff has mean $\tfrac{b'}{n}$ by part (b), because the payments $q$ do not change which balls leave. So we guess $V_q^*(b',g')=q(n-1)+\tfrac{b'}{n}$.

Step 2, **Claim.** For every state the guess is correct, and when $n\ge2$ continue is strictly better than stop. The proof is by induction on $n$, upward from $1$.

**Base.** For $n=1$ the value is the forced payoff $b'$, and the guess gives $q\cdot0+b'$.

**Hypothesis.** For some $n\ge2$ the guess holds on every state with $n-1$ balls, that is $V_q^*(b'',g'')=q(n-2)+\tfrac{b''}{n-1}$ there.

**Step.** The value of continuing is $q$ now plus the expected value of the next state, which we read from the hypothesis:

$$\begin{aligned}
Q_q^*(\text{continue})&=q+\tfrac{b'}{n}\Big(q(n-2)+\tfrac{b'-1}{n-1}\Big)\\
&\quad+\tfrac{g'}{n}\Big(q(n-2)+\tfrac{b'}{n-1}\Big)\\
&=q+q(n-2)+\tfrac{b'(b'-1+g')}{n(n-1)}\\
&=q(n-1)+\tfrac{b'}{n}.
\end{aligned}$$

The second equality uses $\tfrac{b'}{n}+\tfrac{g'}{n}=1$; the last uses the algebra of part (b). Stopping pays $\tfrac{b'}{n}$, so

$$Q_q^*(\text{continue})-Q_q^*(\text{stop})=q(n-1),$$

which is strictly positive because $q>0$ and $n\ge2$. Hence the maximum is the continue value and the guess holds at $n$.

Step 3, conclusion: in every state with at least two balls the optimal action is continue,

$$\boxed{\pi^*(b',g')=\text{continue}}$$

and the optimal value from the start is

$$V_q^*(s_0)=q(b+g-1)+\tfrac{b}{b+g}.$$

**In words.** The probability of winning the final bet is $\tfrac{b'}{n}$ whatever you do (part (c)), so stopping early gains nothing and gives up the payments $q$ that are still available.

**Sanity check.** For $q\to0$ the gap $q(n-1)$ vanishes and we are back to the tie of part (c). For $b'=1$, $g'=2$ and $q=0.3$:

$$V_q^*(1,2)=0.3\cdot2+\tfrac13=\tfrac{14}{15}\approx0.933,$$

against $\tfrac13$ for stopping now.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Guess: $V_q^*(b',g')=q(n-1)+\tfrac{b'}{n}$.
- Induction: continue gives $q+q(n-2)+\tfrac{b'}{n}$, stop gives $\tfrac{b'}{n}$, the gap is $q(n-1)$, positive.
- Answer: always continue while two or more balls remain; value $q(n-1)+\tfrac{b'}{n}$.

*Verified: exact DP with fractions for $q$ equal to $\tfrac{1}{1000}$, $\tfrac{1}{10}$, $\tfrac{3}{10}$ and $1$, and for all $b',g'\le8$ gives the formula and the gap $q(n-1)$; the example gives exactly $\tfrac{14}{15}$.*

**Common mistakes.**

- Setting the stop reward to $1$ or to the colour actually drawn: the agent decides before it sees the ball, so the reward is the expectation $\tfrac{b'}{n}$.
- Putting the history of draws in the state: only the counts matter, and a larger state makes the induction on $n$ much harder.
- In (d), stopping when the blue fraction is high: the final win probability is the same for every policy, so the extra $q$ always decides.

**💡 Trick.** Recognize the **martingale**: under uniform removal the probability "the ball I eventually bet on is blue" never changes — it is $\tfrac{b}{g+b}$ from the start. So with $q=0$ every policy (stop now, or drain the urn) has the *same* value $\tfrac{b}{g+b}$; the only thing that can break the tie is a per-step bonus $q>0$, which makes **continue** strictly best.

**⚠️ Watch out.** Blue is the paying color, so the value is $\tfrac{b}{g+b}$ (blue over total) — don't flip it to $\tfrac{g}{g+b}$. And in (d), "always continue" is optimal not because it changes the win-probability (it doesn't) but purely because it harvests the extra $q$ each step; the terminal term is unchanged.

## Q2 (30 pts) — Finite-horizon reward transforms: which preserve the optimal first action
**Topics:** finite-horizon MDP, optimal policy, reward scaling/shifting, horizon dependence, action-value ordering | **Pillar:** Planning | **Difficulty:** 4
**Maps to:** lecture_02, lecture_03
**Statement (English translation):**
Let $M=(s_0,S,A,R,P)$ be a finite-horizon MDP with horizon parameter $T$. All rewards are in $[-1,+1]$ (note they can be **negative**). Let $\pi^*$ be an optimal policy for $M$ and $a_0^*$ the action it takes at the initial state $s_0$. In each part we modify $M$ and ask: **must there still be an optimal policy that takes $a_0^*$ at $s_0$?** Answer true/false with a proof or a counterexample.

a. Multiply every reward by a constant $\alpha\in[-1,+1]$.

b. Add a constant $\beta\in[-1,+1]$ to every reward.

c. Change the horizon parameter $T$ to $T'=2T$.

d. In every state $s$ add a **reset** action with reward $0$, $r(s,\text{reset})=0$, whose next state is $s_0$.

e. In some state $s$ and action $a$ with $a\ne\pi^*(s)$ and $r(s,a)\ge0$, halve that reward: $r'(s,a)=r(s,a)/2$.

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) Multiplying every reward by α**

**What is asked.** Whether, for every $\alpha\in[-1,+1]$, some optimal policy of the scaled MDP still takes $a_0^*$ at $s_0$. The answer is a verdict with a proof or a counterexample.

**Tool and why.** Reward transformations (cheat sheet §5): multiplying by a positive constant keeps every arg-max, but a negative constant turns maximization into minimization. Because the question says "necessarily", one counterexample is enough for "false".

**Definitions.** Let $V^\pi_M$ be the expected sum of the $T$ rewards that policy $\pi$ collects in $M$ from $s_0$. Let $Q_T(s,a)$ be the optimal value with $T$ steps left when the first action at $s$ is $a$. We need them to compare policies and first actions.

**Solution.**

Verdict: **false.**

Step 1, where it does hold: by linearity of expectation $V^\pi_{\alpha M}=\alpha V^\pi_M$ for every $\pi$. For $\alpha>0$ the order of policies does not change, and for $\alpha=0$ every policy is optimal. So the only possible failure is $\alpha<0$.

Step 2, the counterexample: one state $s_0$ with a self-loop, two actions $a_1$ with reward $1$ and $a_2$ with reward $0$, and $T=1$. Then $Q_1(s_0,a_1)=1$ and $Q_1(s_0,a_2)=0$, so $a_0^*=a_1$, and it is the unique optimal first action.

Step 3, scale by $\alpha=-1$: the rewards become $-1$ and $0$, so $Q_1(s_0,a_1)=-1$ and $Q_1(s_0,a_2)=0$. Now $a_2$ is strictly better, and no optimal policy takes $a_1$.

**In words.** A negative factor turns the best action into the worst one; the interval $[-1,+1]$ allows such a factor.

**Sanity check.** With $\alpha=1$ we get $M$ back, and with $\alpha=0$ both actions tie at $0$; both agree with Step 1.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Verdict: false.
- Reason: $V^\pi_{\alpha M}=\alpha V^\pi_M$, and a negative $\alpha$ reverses the order.
- Counterexample: one state, rewards $1$ and $0$, $T=1$; after $\alpha=-1$ they are $-1$ and $0$, so $a_2$ is strictly better.

*Verified: finite-horizon DP on the counterexample gives first-step Q-values $(1,0)$ before and $(-1,0)$ after scaling.*

**(b) Adding β to every reward**

**What is asked.** Whether some optimal policy of the shifted MDP still takes $a_0^*$ at $s_0$. Verdict with a proof or a counterexample.

**Tool and why.** Reward transformations in a finite horizon (cheat sheet §5): every policy collects exactly $T$ rewards, so a constant shift moves every policy's value by the same amount.

**Definitions.** Let $M+\beta$ be the MDP with rewards $r+\beta$ and the same transitions. $V^\pi_M$ is as in part (a).

**Solution.**

Verdict: **true.**

Step 1, one trajectory: a run of $T$ steps in $M+\beta$ collects

$$\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(r_t+\beta)=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r_t+\beta T.$$

Step 2, one policy: the transitions did not change, so every policy (even a history-dependent one) induces the same distribution over trajectories in both MDPs. Taking expectations in Step 1 gives $V^\pi_{M+\beta}=V^\pi_M+\beta T$.

Step 3, optimality of $\pi^*$: for every policy $\pi$, using the optimality of $\pi^*$ in $M$ in the second row,

$$\begin{aligned}
V^{\pi^*}_{M+\beta}&=V^{\pi^*}_M+\beta T\\
&\ge V^\pi_M+\beta T\\
&=V^\pi_{M+\beta}.
\end{aligned}$$

So $\pi^*$ is still optimal, and it takes $a_0^*$ at $s_0$.

**In words.** The same constant $\beta T$ is added to every policy's score, so the ranking does not move. This needs every run to last exactly $T$ steps; if runs could end at different times, a shift would favour longer or shorter runs.

**Sanity check.** In the example of part (a) a shift gives first-step values $1+\beta$ and $\beta$, and $a_1$ is still the better one for every $\beta$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Verdict: true.
- Every policy collects exactly $T$ rewards, so $V^\pi_{M+\beta}=V^\pi_M+\beta T$.
- Same shift for all policies, same order, so $\pi^*$ stays optimal.

*Verified: on 3000 random finite-horizon MDPs the shifted first-step Q-values equal the original ones plus $\beta T$ exactly.*

**(c) Doubling the horizon**

**What is asked.** Whether some optimal policy for horizon $2T$ still takes $a_0^*$ at $s_0$. Verdict with a proof or a counterexample.

**Tool and why.** Backward induction in a finite horizon (cheat sheet §3): the optimal action depends on how many steps are left. A short horizon prefers a small reward now, a long one prefers a large reward later, so we build an MDP with exactly that trade-off.

**Definitions.** The counterexample has a start state $s_0$; a chain $c_1,\dots,c_{T-1}$ of zero-reward states that lead forward one by one (empty when $T=1$); a state $L$ that loops on itself with reward $1$; and an absorbing state $z$ with reward $0$. At $s_0$ action $a_1$ pays $\tfrac12$ and goes to $z$, and action $a_2$ pays $0$ and enters the chain (or goes straight to $L$ when $T=1$). The chain is there so that $L$ is reached only after the first $T$ steps.

**Solution.**

Verdict: **false.**

Step 1, horizon $T$: $a_1$ collects $\tfrac12$ and then zeros, so its value is $\tfrac12$. Action $a_2$ spends the $T$ steps in $s_0,c_1,\dots,c_{T-1}$, all with reward $0$, so its value is $0$. Hence $a_0^*=a_1$, uniquely.

Step 2, horizon $2T$: $a_1$ still collects $\tfrac12$. Action $a_2$ collects $0$ for $T$ steps and then $1$ in each of the remaining $T$ steps in $L$, so its value is $T$.

Step 3, compare: $T\ge1$, which is more than $\tfrac12$, so $a_2$ is strictly better and no optimal policy takes $a_1$.

| horizon | value of $a_1$ | value of $a_2$ |
|---|---|---|
| $T$ | $\tfrac12$ | $0$ |
| $2T$ | $\tfrac12$ | $T$ |

All rewards lie in $[-1,+1]$, as required.

**In words.** With few steps left you grab the reward in front of you; with more steps it pays to walk towards the state that pays every step.

**Sanity check.** For $T=1$ the chain is empty: with one step $a_1$ gives $\tfrac12$ and $a_2$ gives $0$, and with two steps $a_1$ gives $\tfrac12$ and $a_2$ gives $1$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Verdict: false.
- Counterexample: $a_1$ pays $\tfrac12$ and ends in a zero state; $a_2$ pays $0$ for $T$ steps and then $1$ per step.
- Horizon $T$: values $\tfrac12$ and $0$, so $a_1$; horizon $2T$: values $\tfrac12$ and $T$, so $a_2$.

*Verified: finite-horizon DP on this MDP for $T\in\{1,2,3,5\}$ gives first-step values $(\tfrac12,0)$ at horizon $T$ and $(\tfrac12,T)$ at horizon $2T$.*

**(d) Adding a reset action**

**What is asked.** Whether, after adding a zero-reward action that returns to $s_0$, some optimal policy still takes $a_0^*$ at $s_0$. Verdict with a proof or a counterexample.

**Tool and why.** Adding an action enlarges the set of policies (cheat sheet §5 and §12), so the new action can dominate the old optimum. It does so when the original rewards are negative, which the statement explicitly allows.

**Definitions.** Let $M_{\text{reset}}$ be $M$ with the extra action $\text{reset}$ in every state. No other new notation.

**Solution.**

Verdict: **false.**

Step 1, the counterexample: one state $s_0$ with one action $a$ of reward $-1$ that loops back to $s_0$; any horizon $T$. The only action is $a$, so $a_0^*=a$.

Step 2, after adding $\text{reset}$: starting with $a$ costs $-1$, and the best continuation is to reset in every later step, which adds $0$:

$$Q_T(s_0,a)=-1+0\cdot(T-1)=-1.$$

Starting with $\text{reset}$ and resetting forever gives $Q_T(s_0,\text{reset})=0$.

Step 3, compare: $0$ is more than $-1$, so $\text{reset}$ is strictly better and no optimal policy of $M_{\text{reset}}$ takes $a$ at $s_0$.

**In words.** Reset is a way to "do nothing" at zero cost. When every real action costs something, doing nothing wins.

**Sanity check.** The same holds in any MDP whose rewards are all strictly negative: resetting forever has value $0$, and any run that uses a non-reset action has a negative sum.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Verdict: false.
- Counterexample: one state, one action with reward $-1$, so $a_0^*=a$.
- After the change: $a$ is worth $-1$ and reset is worth $0$, so reset is strictly better.

*Verified: finite-horizon DP for $T=1$ and $T=3$ gives first-step values $-1$ for $a$ and $0$ for reset.*

**(e) Halving a non-negative reward of an unused action**

**What is asked.** Whether halving $r(s,a)$, for a pair with $a\ne\pi^*(s)$ and a non-negative reward, keeps some optimal policy that takes $a_0^*$. Verdict with a proof or a counterexample.

**Tool and why.** A dominance argument on rewards (cheat sheet §5): if rewards only go down, no policy's value goes up, and a policy that never uses the changed pair keeps its value. Together these keep $\pi^*$ on top.

**Definitions.** Let $M'$ be the modified MDP and $r'$ its reward. We read $a\ne\pi^*(s)$ as "$\pi^*$ never plays $a$ in $s$", at any time step.

**Solution.**

Verdict: **true.**

Step 1, rewards only go down: $r'(s,a)=\tfrac12r(s,a)$, which is at most $r(s,a)$ because $r(s,a)\ge0$. All other rewards are unchanged, so $r'\le r$ everywhere.

Step 2, every policy loses or keeps value: the transitions are the same, so a policy sees the same trajectories in both MDPs, each with a reward sum that is not larger in $M'$. Hence $V^\pi_{M'}\le V^\pi_M$ for every $\pi$.

Step 3, $\pi^*$ keeps its value: it never collects $r(s,a)$, so $V^{\pi^*}_{M'}=V^{\pi^*}_M$.

Step 4, chain of inequalities for every policy $\pi$, by Step 2, then the optimality of $\pi^*$ in $M$, then Step 3:

$$\begin{aligned}
V^\pi_{M'}&\le V^\pi_M\\
&\le V^{\pi^*}_M\\
&=V^{\pi^*}_{M'}.
\end{aligned}$$

So $\pi^*$ is optimal in $M'$ and still takes $a_0^*$ at $s_0$.

**Where the condition is used.** Only in Step 1. If $r(s,a)$ were negative, halving would raise it (for example from $-1$ to $-\tfrac12$), and $a$ could become optimal.

**In words.** Making an option that nobody chose even worse cannot change the best plan.

**Sanity check.** One state, $a_1$ with reward $1$ (chosen by $\pi^*$) and $a_2$ with reward $\tfrac12$: after halving, $a_2$ pays $\tfrac14$, and $a_1$ is still the best.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Verdict: true.
- Since $r(s,a)\ge0$, halving lowers it, so $V^\pi_{M'}\le V^\pi_M$ for every $\pi$.
- Since $\pi^*$ never uses $(s,a)$, $V^{\pi^*}_{M'}=V^{\pi^*}_M$.
- Chain: $\pi^*$ is still optimal and takes $a_0^*$.

*Verified: on 3232 random finite-horizon MDPs with a pair that the optimal policy never uses and a non-negative reward, halving kept $a_0^*$ optimal in every case.*

**Common mistakes.**

- In (a), checking only $\alpha>0$ and answering "true": the interval includes negative values.
- In (b), treating the shift like the scaling: a negative $\beta$ is harmless, because every policy collects the same number of rewards.
- In (c) and (d), stating "a counterexample exists" without a concrete MDP and its numbers; the grader needs the explicit values.

**💡 Trick.** Sort the five transforms by **what they preserve**. Positive affine facts that hold *per policy* survive: adding a constant shifts every horizon-$T$ return by $\beta T$ (b), and lowering an *unused* action changes nothing (e). What breaks optimality is anything that reorders returns: negative scaling (a), a new dominating option (d, the reset), or changing how many steps you optimize over (c).

**⚠️ Watch out.** Two traps: (i) $\alpha$ and $\beta$ live in $[-1,+1]$, so you *must* consider the **negative** case in (a) (where it fails) even though it holds for $\beta$ in (b) — scaling and shifting behave differently. (ii) In (d) the reset looks harmless (reward $0$) but is decisive precisely when the original rewards are negative — always test the negative-reward regime the problem hands you.

## Q3 (20 pts) — Value-function approximation by Monte-Carlo stochastic gradient descent
**Topics:** value-function approximation, quadratic objective, gradient descent, Monte-Carlo return, stochastic gradient / SGD | **Pillar:** Approximation | **Difficulty:** 3
**Maps to:** lecture_08, lecture_06
**Statement (English translation):**
Given an MDP where every state $s$ has a feature vector $\phi(s)\in\mathbb{R}^d$. A value-function approximator with parameter $\theta\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is $\hat V(s;\theta)=\exp\big(\phi(s)\cdot\theta\big)$. For a policy $\pi$ we minimize the quadratic error
$$J^\pi(\theta)=\frac12\sum_s\mu(s)\big(V^\pi(s)-\hat V(s;\theta)\big)^2,$$
where $\mu(s)$ is the probability that policy $\pi$ reaches state $s$ (assume a state is not visited twice in the same run). We minimize $J^\pi(\theta)$ by gradient updates.

a. Write the gradient-descent update for minimizing $J^\pi(\theta)$ (as a function of $\phi(s),\mu(s),V^\pi(s)$ and learning rate $\alpha$).

b. Propose how to obtain an unbiased estimate of $V^\pi(s)$ for a state $s$, given an episode of $\pi$ in which $s$ appears, using Monte-Carlo.

c. Write the Monte-Carlo SGD update for minimizing $J^\pi(\theta)$.

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) The gradient-descent update**

**What is asked.** The update $\theta\leftarrow\theta-\alpha\nabla_\theta J^\pi$ written explicitly in terms of $\phi(s)$, $\mu(s)$, $V^\pi(s)$ and $\alpha$. The answer is a formula.

**Tool and why.** Full gradient descent on a squared error, with the chain rule (cheat sheet §8). The objective is a weighted sum of squares of a smooth function of $\theta$, so we differentiate each term.

**Definitions.** Let $\delta(s)=\hat V(s;\theta)-V^\pi(s)$ be the error at state $s$. We introduce it only to shorten the formulas.

**Solution.**

Step 1, derivative of the approximator, by the chain rule (the exponential is its own derivative, and the gradient of $\phi(s)\cdot\theta$ is $\phi(s)$):

$$\nabla_\theta\hat V(s;\theta)=\exp(\phi(s)\cdot\theta)\,\phi(s)=\hat V(s;\theta)\,\phi(s)$$

Step 2, derivative of one term: $\mu(s)$ and $V^\pi(s)$ do not depend on $\theta$, so

$$\nabla_\theta\tfrac12\big(V^\pi(s)-\hat V(s;\theta)\big)^2=\delta(s)\,\hat V(s;\theta)\,\phi(s).$$

The minus sign from the inner derivative turns $V^\pi-\hat V$ into $\delta(s)$.

Step 3, sum over states with weights $\mu(s)$:

$$\nabla_\theta J^\pi=\sum_s\mu(s)\big(\hat V(s;\theta)-V^\pi(s)\big)\hat V(s;\theta)\,\phi(s)$$

Step 4, a gradient step of size $\alpha$:

$$\boxed{\theta^{t+1}=\theta^{t}-\alpha\sum_s\mu(s)\big(\hat V(s;\theta^t)-V^\pi(s)\big)\hat V(s;\theta^t)\,\phi(s)}$$

**In words.** Every state pulls $\theta$ along its feature vector, with a strength equal to its weight times its error times $\hat V(s;\theta)$, the slope of the exponential at that point.

**Sanity check.** If $\hat V$ equals $V^\pi$ on every state with positive weight, the gradient is $0$ and nothing moves. In one dimension with one state, $J=\tfrac12(v-e^\theta)^2$ and the formula gives $(e^\theta-v)e^\theta$, the ordinary derivative.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Chain rule: the gradient of $\hat V(s;\theta)$ is $\hat V(s;\theta)\,\phi(s)$.
- Gradient: the $\mu$-weighted sum over $s$ of $\delta(s)\,\hat V(s;\theta)\,\phi(s)$.
- Update: $\theta$ minus $\alpha$ times that sum, as in the box.

*Verified: on a random instance with $d=3$ and five states the analytic gradient matches central finite differences to $5\cdot10^{-11}$.*

**(b) An unbiased Monte-Carlo estimate of the state value**

**What is asked.** A quantity computed from one episode of $\pi$ that visits $s$, whose expectation is $V^\pi(s)$. The answer is a formula with a justification.

**Tool and why.** Monte-Carlo policy evaluation (cheat sheet §7): $V^\pi(s)$ is by definition the expected return from $s$, so the return observed from the visit is an unbiased sample of it. No model of the transitions is needed.

**Definitions.** Let $t_s$ be the time step at which the episode visits $s$ (there is only one such step), $r_k$ the reward at step $k$, and $\gamma$ the discount ($\gamma=1$ is allowed, since the episode ends).

**Solution.**

Step 1, the estimate: the return from the visit,

$$G_s=\sum_{k\ge t_s}\gamma^{\,k-t_s}r_k,$$

the discounted sum of the rewards observed from $s$ on.

Step 2, unbiasedness: given that the episode is at $s$ at time $t_s$, what happens afterwards depends only on $s$ and $\pi$ (Markov property), and its expected discounted sum is $V^\pi(s)$ by definition. So $\mathbb{E}[G_s]=V^\pi(s)$.

Step 3, well defined: a state is visited at most once per run, so "first visit" and "every visit" give the same single return.

**In words.** The value of a state is the average return from it, so one observed return is an honest sample of that average.

**Sanity check.** If the policy and the transitions are deterministic, $G_s$ equals $V^\pi(s)$ exactly. In a small random episodic MDP the average of $G_s$ over many episodes was $1.4527$ against $V^\pi(s)=1.45$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Estimate: the return $G_s$ from the visit to $s$.
- Reason: $\mathbb{E}[G_s]=V^\pi(s)$ by definition of $V^\pi$ and the Markov property.

*Verified: 100000 simulated episodes of a four-state episodic MDP give a mean return $1.4527$ at a state whose exact value is $1.45$.*

**(c) The Monte-Carlo SGD update**

**What is asked.** A stochastic update that uses one episode instead of the sum over all states and the unknown $V^\pi$. The answer is a formula.

**Tool and why.** Stochastic gradient descent (cheat sheet §8): replace the exact gradient by an unbiased sample of it. Running $\pi$ samples states with the weights $\mu(s)$, and part (b) replaces $V^\pi(s)$ by the return.

**Definitions.** Let $s_t$ be the state visited at step $t$ of the episode and $G_t$ its return, as in part (b). Let $g$ be the sum of the per-visit terms over one episode, with $\theta$ held fixed during the episode:

$$g=\sum_t\big(\hat V(s_t;\theta)-G_t\big)\hat V(s_t;\theta)\,\phi(s_t)$$

We define $g$ to show that it is an unbiased estimate of the gradient.

**Solution.**

Step 1, the update: at every visited state $s_t$, replace $V^\pi(s_t)$ by $G_t$ in one term of part (a):

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\theta^{t+1}&=\theta^{t}-\alpha\big(\hat V(s_t;\theta^t)-G_t\big)\hat V(s_t;\theta^t)\,\phi(s_t)\\
&=\theta^{t}+\alpha\big(G_t-\hat V(s_t;\theta^t)\big)\hat V(s_t;\theta^t)\,\phi(s_t)
\end{aligned}}$$

Step 2, why it is unbiased: each state appears at most once per episode, with probability $\mu(s)$, and by part (b) the return at that visit has mean $V^\pi(s)$. So

$$\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}[g]&=\sum_s\mu(s)\,\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat V(s;\theta)-G_s)\hat V(s;\theta)\phi(s)\big]\\
&=\sum_s\mu(s)\big(\hat V(s;\theta)-V^\pi(s)\big)\hat V(s;\theta)\phi(s)\\
&=\nabla_\theta J^\pi.
\end{aligned}$$

The first row uses the single-visit assumption; the second uses part (b), because $\hat V(s;\theta)\phi(s)$ is fixed once $s$ is known.

Step 3, the algorithm: run an episode of $\pi$, compute the returns $G_t$ backwards, and apply the update of Step 1 at each visited state.

**In words.** Sampling on-policy supplies the weights $\mu$, and the return supplies $V^\pi$. Both unknown pieces of the exact gradient are replaced by samples with the right mean.

**Sanity check.** If $G_t$ equals $\hat V(s_t;\theta)$ there is no move. If $G_t$ is larger, $\phi(s_t)\cdot\theta$ grows by $\alpha(G_t-\hat V)\hat V\|\phi(s_t)\|^2$, which is positive because $\hat V$ is positive, so the prediction at $s_t$ rises towards the return.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Replace $V^\pi(s_t)$ by $G_t$ and the $\mu$-weighted sum by the visited states.
- Update: add $\alpha\big(G_t-\hat V(s_t;\theta)\big)$ times $\hat V(s_t;\theta)\,\phi(s_t)$ to $\theta$.
- Unbiased: single visits occur with probability $\mu(s)$ and $\mathbb{E}[G_s]=V^\pi(s)$.

*Verified: over 200000 simulated episodes the mean of $g$ is $(-0.5389,-0.0253,-0.7976)$ against the exact gradient $(-0.5387,-0.0250,-0.7991)$.*

**Common mistakes.**

- Dropping the factor $\hat V(s;\theta)$ that the chain rule produces; the update is then the one for a linear model.
- Multiplying the SGD step by $\mu(s_t)$ again: sampling on-policy already gives the weight, so this counts it twice.
- Putting $\hat V$ in the place of $V^\pi$ in the target, which makes the error identically zero.

**💡 Trick.** Two facts do all the work: the chain rule on $\hat V=\exp(\phi\cdot\theta)$ gives the extra factor $\hat V(s;\theta)$ in the gradient (its own derivative), and Monte-Carlo turns the *two* intractable pieces of $J$ into samples at once — sampling states on-policy supplies $\mu$, and the return $G_t$ supplies an unbiased $V^\pi(s_t)$.

**⚠️ Watch out.** Don't drop the $\hat V(s;\theta)$ factor from $\nabla_\theta\hat V$ (the exponential differentiates to itself times $\phi$). And $G_t$ estimates $V^\pi$, not $\hat V$ — the residual is $\hat V(s_t;\theta)-G_t$; using $\hat V$ on both sides would zero the update.

## Q4 (20 pts) — γ-contraction of an operator with a global look-ahead max
**Topics:** Bellman-type operator, $\gamma$-contraction in sup-norm, fixed point, global maximum term | **Pillar:** Planning | **Difficulty:** 3
**Maps to:** lecture_04, lecture_03
**Statement (English translation):**
Given an MDP $M=(S,A,p,s_0,r)$. Define the operator $T$ for $U\in\mathbb{R}^{|S|}$, with $0<\gamma<1$:
$$(TU)(s)=\max_a\ \mathbb{E}_{s'\sim p(\cdot\mid s,a)}\Big[\,r(s,a)+\gamma\,\max_{\tilde s}\,U(\tilde s)\,\Big].$$

a. Write the definition of when an operator $H$ is $\gamma$-contracting for the norm $\|\cdot\|_\infty$.

b. Prove that $T$ is $\gamma$-contracting for the norm $\|\cdot\|_\infty$.

c. Let $U^*$ be the fixed point of $T$ for the given MDP $M$. Write its value $U^*(s)$ for every state, as a function of $\gamma$ and the reward $r(s,a)$ of $M$. Hint: think first about the state–action $(s,a)$ that maximizes $r(s,a)$.

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) Definition of a γ-contraction**

**What is asked.** The definition of "$H$ is a $\gamma$-contraction in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$". The answer is a definition.

**Tool and why.** Operators, contraction and fixed points (cheat sheet §6): this definition is the hypothesis of the Banach fixed-point theorem, which we use in parts (b) and (c).

**Definitions.** For $U\in\mathbb{R}^{|S|}$, $\|U\|_\infty=\max_s|U(s)|$, the largest entry in absolute value.

**Solution.**

Step 1, the definition: an operator $H$ from $\mathbb{R}^{|S|}$ to itself is a $\gamma$-contraction in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$, with $\gamma\in[0,1)$, if for all $U,U'\in\mathbb{R}^{|S|}$

$$\|HU-HU'\|_\infty\le\gamma\,\|U-U'\|_\infty.$$

Step 2, what it gives (Banach): such an $H$ has a unique fixed point, and iterating $H$ from any start converges to it at rate $\gamma^k$.

**In words.** Applying $H$ brings any two vectors closer together by at least the factor $\gamma$, in the worst coordinate.

**Sanity check.** The identity operator satisfies the inequality only with factor $1$, so it is not a contraction, and indeed every vector is a fixed point of it, not a unique one.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Definition: for all $U,U'$, $\|HU-HU'\|_\infty\le\gamma\|U-U'\|_\infty$, with $\gamma\in[0,1)$.

*Verified: this inequality is exactly the ratio test run in part (b) on 2000 random MDPs.*

**(b) Proof that T is a γ-contraction**

**What is asked.** A proof of the inequality of part (a) for the given $T$.

**Tool and why.** Contraction in the sup-norm (cheat sheet §6). We first simplify $T$: the bracket does not depend on $s'$, so the expectation disappears and $T$ becomes the best immediate reward plus a single number. Then we bound how much that number can change.

**Definitions.** Let $\bar r(s)=\max_a r(s,a)$ be the best immediate reward at $s$, and $M(U)=\max_{\tilde s}U(\tilde s)$ the largest entry of $U$. Both make the simplified form of $T$ short.

**Solution.**

Step 1, remove the expectation: neither $r(s,a)$ nor $\max_{\tilde s}U(\tilde s)$ involves $s'$, and $p(\cdot\mid s,a)$ sums to $1$, so the expectation of the constant is the constant. The term $\gamma M(U)$ is the same for every $a$, so it leaves the maximum:

$$(TU)(s)=\bar r(s)+\gamma M(U).$$

Step 2, the difference: the term $\bar r(s)$ cancels between $U$ and $U'$,

$$(TU)(s)-(TU')(s)=\gamma\big(M(U)-M(U')\big).$$

Step 3, **Claim.** $|M(U)-M(U')|\le\|U-U'\|_\infty$.

Let $s_1$ be a state with $U(s_1)=M(U)$. Since $M(U')$ is at least $U'(s_1)$,

$$\begin{aligned}
M(U)-M(U')&\le U(s_1)-U'(s_1)\\
&\le\|U-U'\|_\infty.
\end{aligned}$$

Exchanging the roles of $U$ and $U'$ gives the same bound for $M(U')-M(U)$, which proves the claim.

Step 4, conclusion: by Steps 2 and 3, $|(TU)(s)-(TU')(s)|\le\gamma\|U-U'\|_\infty$ for every $s$. Taking the maximum over $s$:

$$\|TU-TU'\|_\infty\le\gamma\,\|U-U'\|_\infty.$$

By Banach, $T$ has a unique fixed point $U^*$.

**In words.** All of $T$'s dependence on $U$ goes through one number, the maximum of $U$, and the maximum cannot move more than the largest change of an entry.

**Sanity check.** For $U'=U+c\mathbf{1}$ the maximum moves by exactly $c$, so $TU'=TU+\gamma c\mathbf{1}$ and the ratio is exactly $\gamma$: the bound is tight.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Simplify: the bracket does not depend on $s'$, so $(TU)(s)=\bar r(s)+\gamma M(U)$.
- Difference: $\gamma\big(M(U)-M(U')\big)$.
- Bound: $|M(U)-M(U')|\le\|U-U'\|_\infty$, via the state that attains the maximum, in both directions.
- Conclusion: the max over $s$ gives the contraction.

*Verified: on 2000 random MDPs with random pairs $U,U'$ the ratio $\|TU-TU'\|_\infty/\|U-U'\|_\infty$ never exceeded $\gamma$ (largest ratio divided by $\gamma$: $1.0000$).*

**(c) The fixed point**

**What is asked.** An explicit formula for $U^*(s)$ in terms of $\gamma$ and the rewards. The answer is a formula.

**Tool and why.** Fixed-point equation (cheat sheet §6): by part (b) the fixed point is unique, so it is enough to find one vector that solves $U=TU$. The hint suggests looking at the largest reward, because the maximum of $U^*$ is attained where $\bar r$ is largest.

**Definitions.** Let $R_{\max}=\max_{s,a}r(s,a)$, the largest reward in $M$, and $c=M(U^*)$, the largest entry of the fixed point. Reducing the problem to the single unknown $c$ is the whole trick.

**Solution.**

Step 1, the fixed-point equation: by Step 1 of part (b), for every $s$

$$U^*(s)=\bar r(s)+\gamma c.$$

Step 2, take the maximum over $s$ on both sides: the term $\gamma c$ is the same for all $s$, and the maximum of $\bar r(s)$ is $R_{\max}$, so

$$c=R_{\max}+\gamma c.$$

Step 3, solve for $c$: $(1-\gamma)c=R_{\max}$, so

$$c=\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}.$$

Step 4, substitute back into Step 1:

$$\boxed{U^*(s)=\max_a r(s,a)+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\,\max_{s',a'}r(s',a')}$$

Step 5, check: the maximum of this vector is $R_{\max}+\tfrac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}R_{\max}$, which is $\tfrac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}$, the value of $c$ we used. So the vector really is a fixed point, and by part (b) it is the only one.

A worked example with $\gamma=\tfrac12$, so $\tfrac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}=1$, and $R_{\max}=2$:

| state | $r(s,a_1)$ | $r(s,a_2)$ | $U^*(s)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $s_1$ | $1$ | $0$ | $3$ |
| $s_2$ | $\tfrac12$ | $2$ | $4$ |
| $s_3$ | $-1$ | $-\tfrac12$ | $\tfrac32$ |

**In words.** Each state collects its own best reward once, and from the next step on it is credited as if it collected the global best reward $R_{\max}$ forever, discounted.

**Sanity check.** If all rewards equal $r_0$, the formula gives $r_0+\tfrac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}r_0$, which is $\tfrac{r_0}{1-\gamma}$, the value of collecting $r_0$ forever.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Fixed point: $U^*(s)=\bar r(s)+\gamma c$ with $c=\max_s U^*(s)$.
- Max over $s$: $c=R_{\max}+\gamma c$, so $c=\tfrac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}$.
- Answer: $U^*(s)$ is $\max_a r(s,a)$ plus $\tfrac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}R_{\max}$; unique by part (b).

*Verified: 3000 iterations of $T$ on 2000 random MDPs (rewards of both signs) match the closed form to $10^{-12}$; the example table was recomputed.*

**Common mistakes.**

- Asserting $|M(U)-M(U')|\le\|U-U'\|_\infty$ without the one-line argument through the state that attains the maximum.
- Answering $\tfrac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}$ for every state, forgetting the local term $\max_a r(s,a)$ at the first step.
- Using $\max_s|U(s)|$ for the maximum entry: with negative entries the two differ, and the fixed-point equation needs $\max_s U(s)$.

**💡 Trick.** Spot that the look-ahead is $\max_{\tilde s}U(\tilde s)$ — a **global** maximum over the whole vector, independent of $s$ and $s'$ — so the expectation is a red herring and the operator is just $\max_a r(s,a)$ plus a **single scalar** $\gamma\max_{\tilde s}U(\tilde s)$. That scalar solves a one-dimensional fixed point $c=R_{\max}+\gamma c$, giving the $\tfrac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}R_{\max}$ tail.

**⚠️ Watch out.** Do not read $\max_{\tilde s}U(\tilde s)$ as $\max_{a'}U(s')$ (the usual next-state look-ahead) — here it is the maximum entry of $U$ over **all** states, the same constant in every coordinate. That is exactly why $U^*$ collapses to "local best reward $+$ a global constant," rather than the standard optimal value.
