## Q1 (30 pts) — Selling to random offers: an optimal-stopping / inventory MDP
**Topics:** MDP formalism, finite-horizon optimal stopping, threshold policy, monotonicity in inventory, inventory management | **Pillar:** Planning | **Difficulty:** 4
**Maps to:** lecture_03, lecture_04
**Statement (English translation):**
A sequential decision problem with parameters $k\ge1$, $m\ge1$, $T\ge1$. A seller has $k$ identical products. At each time $1\le t\le T$ a customer offers a price $p_t$ drawn uniformly from $[m]=\{1,\dots,m\}$, i.e. $\Pr[p_t=i]=1/m$ for $i\in[m]$. If the seller still has products, he responds to $p_t$ with one of two actions:
- **sell**: sell one of the remaining products, earn $p_t$, and remain with one fewer product.
- **hold**: do not sell, earn $0$, remain with the same number of products.

If the seller has no products left, the reward is $0$ for the remaining steps. The objective is the finite-horizon return with parameter $T\ge1$.

a. Write the MDP formally. (The initial state $s_0$ may be a distribution over states.)

b. Is it necessarily true that increasing the number of products $k$ increases the optimal expected profit? (I.e., for $k_1>k_2$, must 

$$\mathbb{E}_{s_0^1}V^*_{k_1,m,T}(s_0^1)>\mathbb{E}_{s_0^2}V^*_{k_2,m,T}(s_0^2)$$

?)

c. Given $p>q\ge1$ and $t,r\ge1$. Is it possible that the optimal policy at time $t$ with $r$ products left plays **sell** for $p_t=p$ and **hold** for $p_t=q$? Hint: check the optimality conditions on $Q^\pi$ for the proposed policy $\pi$.

d. Here $k=1$ and $m=2$. For each of the four options below, decide whether there are values of $T$ for which it is the optimal policy, and if so specify them. Option 1: the optimal policy always plays **sell** (every $t$, every $p_t$). Option 2: the optimal policy at $t=1$ plays **hold** for $p_1=1$ and **sell** for $p_1=2$. Option 3: the optimal policy at $t=2$ plays **sell** for $p_2=1$ and **hold** for $p_2=2$. Option 4: the optimal policy always plays **hold**.

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) The MDP, formally**

**What is asked.** Translate the story into the components of a finite-horizon MDP: states, actions, rewards, transitions, initial state and horizon. The answer is a definition, not a number.

**Tool and why.** Modelling a story as an MDP: the state is whatever must be known at the moment of decision in order to decide correctly (cheat sheet §12). The horizon is $T$ steps, so this is a finite-horizon problem with no discounting (cheat sheet §3).

**Definitions.** Let $j$ be the number of products still in stock and $x$ the price offered right now. We put $x$ into the state because the seller sees the offer before choosing, and the right choice depends on it. Let $t\in\{1,\dots,T\}$ be the current time; in a finite horizon the policy may depend on $t$.

**Solution.**

Step 1, states: the pairs $(j,x)$ with $j\in\{0,\dots,k\}$ and $x\in[m]$. This is all that matters, because the past affects the future only through the remaining stock, and the next offer is independent of everything.

Step 2, actions: $A=\{\text{sell},\text{hold}\}$. When $j=0$ both actions do the same thing (nothing), as the statement says.

Step 3, rewards, by the rules of the game:

$$r\big((j,x),\text{sell}\big)=\begin{cases}x, & j\ge1,\\ 0, & j=0,\end{cases}\qquad r\big((j,x),\text{hold}\big)=0.$$

Step 4, transitions: the next offer $x'$ is uniform on $[m]$ and independent of the past, in both actions.

| State and action | Next state | Probability |
|---|---|---|
| $(j,x)$, sell, $j\ge1$ | $(j-1,x')$ | $\tfrac1m$ for each $x'$ |
| $(j,x)$, hold | $(j,x')$ | $\tfrac1m$ for each $x'$ |
| $(0,x)$, any action | $(0,x')$ | $\tfrac1m$ for each $x'$ |

Step 5, initial state: the first offer $p_1$ is uniform on $[m]$, so $s_0$ is the uniform distribution over the $m$ states $(k,1),\dots,(k,m)$.

Step 6, objective: maximize the expected sum of the $T$ rewards, at times $1$ to $T$, with $\gamma=1$.

**In words.** The seller looks at the offer and his stock, and decides. The stock only goes down, the offers are fresh each step, and the game ends after $T$ offers.

**Sanity check.** With $k=1$ and $T=1$ the seller faces one offer with one item; selling earns $x$ and holding earns $0$. The model gives exactly that.

**What to write in the exam.**

- States $(j,x)$: stock $j\in\{0,\dots,k\}$ and current offer $x\in[m]$.
- Actions sell and hold; sell is allowed only when $j\ge1$ (otherwise it does nothing).
- Rewards: sell pays $x$, hold pays $0$; stock goes to $j-1$ after a sale and stays $j$ otherwise.
- Next offer uniform on $[m]$; $s_0$ uniform over $(k,1),\dots,(k,m)$; horizon $T$, $\gamma=1$.

*Verified: an exact-fraction DP built on this MDP in Python reproduces every value in parts (b) to (d).*

**(b) Does more stock always mean strictly more profit?**

**What is asked.** Whether $k_1>k_2$ forces a strictly larger optimal expected profit. The answer is a verdict with a proof or a counterexample.

**Tool and why.** Finite-horizon value comparison (cheat sheet §3): bound the optimal value from above by a quantity that no policy can beat, and show it is attained. The key observation is that at most one product is sold per step.

**Definitions.** Let $V_\tau(j)$ be the optimal expected profit when $j$ products remain and $\tau$ offers are still to come, before the next offer is seen. Then the quantity in the statement is

$$\mathbb{E}_{s_0}V^*_{k,m,T}(s_0)=V_T(k).$$

We introduce $V_\tau(j)$ because it averages out the unseen offer, so it is one number per stock level.

**Solution.**

The verdict: **no.** The profit never decreases in $k$, but it stops growing once $k\ge T$.

Step 1, never decreasing: a seller with $j+1$ products can copy any policy of a seller with $j$ products and simply never use the extra product. He earns exactly the same, so

$$V_\tau(j+1)\ge V_\tau(j).$$

Step 2, an upper bound: in each step at most one product is sold, at price at most the offer. So any policy earns at most the sum of all $T$ offers, whose expectation is

$$T\cdot\mathbb{E}[p_t]=T\cdot\tfrac{m+1}{2}.$$

Step 3, the bound is attained when $k\ge T$: the policy "always sell" never runs out, because it sells at most $T$ products. It earns every offer, so for every $k\ge T$:

$$V_T(k)=\tfrac{T(m+1)}{2}.$$

Step 4, counterexample: take $T=1$, $m=2$, $k_1=2$ and $k_2=1$. Both sellers sell the single offer, and

$$V_1(2)=V_1(1)=\tfrac32.$$

So $k_1>k_2$ but the optimal profits are equal, and the strict inequality fails. The plateau value is

$$\boxed{V_T(k)=\tfrac{T(m+1)}{2}\ \ \forall k\ge T}$$

**In words.** Extra stock is worth something only if there is a chance to sell it. With $T$ offers you can sell at most $T$ items, so item number $T+1$ is never used.

**Sanity check.** With $m=1$ every offer is $1$, and the optimal profit is $\min\{k,T\}$: it grows by $1$ per item up to $T$, then stays flat. That matches Steps 1 to 3.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Verdict: no, not necessarily.
- Non-decreasing: the larger stock can imitate the smaller one.
- At most one sale per step, so profit is at most $T(m+1)/2$, attained by always selling when $k\ge T$.
- Counterexample: $T=1$, $m=2$, $k=1$ versus $k=2$, both give $\tfrac32$.

*Verified: exact DP for $m\le5$ and $T\le7$ shows $V_T(k)$ non-decreasing in $k$, strictly increasing up to $k=T$, and constant at $T(m+1)/2$ for $k\ge T$.*

**(c) Sell the high price and hold the low one?**

**What is asked.** Given prices $p>q$, time $t$ and stock $r$, whether the optimal policy can sell at offer $p$ and hold at offer $q$. The answer is a verdict with a justification.

**Tool and why.** Optimality conditions through $Q$ (cheat sheet §2) and threshold structure (cheat sheet §4): a policy is optimal exactly when, in every state, it picks an action with the largest $Q^*$ value. We compare the two $Q^*$ values and see that the comparison has a threshold form in the price.

**Definitions.** Let $\tau=T-t$ be the number of offers that come after time $t$. Let $\Delta_\tau(r)$ be the marginal value of the $r$-th product:

$$\Delta_\tau(r)=V_\tau(r)-V_\tau(r-1).$$

It is introduced because it is the price at which selling and holding are worth the same.

**Solution.**

The verdict: **yes**, and this is exactly the shape every optimal policy has.

Step 1, the two $Q^*$ values at time $t$ with stock $r$ and offer $x$: selling pays $x$ now and leaves $r-1$ products, holding pays $0$ and leaves $r$ products.

$$Q^*_t\big((r,x),\text{sell}\big)=x+V_\tau(r-1)$$

$$Q^*_t\big((r,x),\text{hold}\big)=V_\tau(r)$$

Step 2, the comparison: subtract the second line from the first. Selling is optimal if and only if

$$x\ge\Delta_\tau(r).$$

The right side does not depend on $x$, so the optimal policy is a price threshold: it sells every offer at or above $\Delta_\tau(r)$ and holds every offer below it.

Step 3, consequence: "sell at $p$ and hold at $q$" is optimal exactly when

$$q<\Delta_\tau(r)\le p.$$

Since $p>q$, this interval is not empty, so nothing rules it out.

Step 4, a concrete instance: $m=2$, $p=2$, $q=1$, $r=1$ and $t=T-1$, so $\tau=1$. One offer remains after time $t$, and the last product will surely be sold then, for an average of $\tfrac32$. Hence

$$\Delta_1(1)=V_1(1)-V_1(0)=\tfrac32-0=\tfrac32,$$

which lies between $1$ and $2$. So the optimal policy holds the offer $1$ and sells the offer $2$.

Step 5, the same for any given $t$ and $r$: take $m=p$ and a long horizon. Two bounds pin down $\Delta_\tau(r)$.

- Upper bound: a seller with $r-1$ products can copy the sales of an optimal $r$-product seller until he runs out; he misses at most one sale, worth at most $m$. So $\Delta_\tau(r)\le m$.
- Lower bound: $V_\tau(r-1)$ is at most $(r-1)m$. Let $N_\tau$ be the number of offers equal to $m$ among the next $\tau$; the policy "sell only at price $m$" shows that $V_\tau(r)$ is at least $m\,\mathbb{E}[\min\{r,N_\tau\}]$.

As $\tau$ grows, $N_\tau\ge r$ with probability tending to $1$, so the lower bound tends to $rm-(r-1)m=m$. For $\tau$ large enough $\Delta_\tau(r)>q$, while $\Delta_\tau(r)\le p$ because $m=p$. By Step 3 the optimal policy sells $p$ and holds $q$.

**In words.** Holding a product keeps an option on a future offer. That option has one fixed value, whatever the current offer is, so you sell exactly the offers that beat it: high offers are sold, low offers are held.

**Sanity check.** At the last step, $\tau=0$ and $\Delta_0(r)=0$, so every positive offer is sold and holding $q$ is never optimal. This is why the example needs $t<T$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Verdict: yes.
- $Q^*$ of sell is $x+V_\tau(r-1)$, $Q^*$ of hold is $V_\tau(r)$; sell iff $x\ge\Delta_\tau(r)$.
- The threshold does not depend on $x$, so selling $p$ and holding $q$ is optimal iff $\Delta_\tau(r)\in(q,p]$.
- Example: $m=2$, $r=1$, one step left after $t$: $\Delta=\tfrac32$, so hold $1$ and sell $2$.

*Verified: exact DP for $m\le6$, $r\le3$, $\tau\le24$ gives $\Delta_\tau(r)\le m$, non-decreasing in $\tau$, and eventually above every $q<m$; for $m=2$ it gives $\Delta_1(1)=\tfrac32$.*

**(d) The case $k=1$, $m=2$**

**What is asked.** For each of the four described policies, the values of $T$ (possibly none) for which it is the optimal policy. The answer is a set of $T$ values per option.

**Tool and why.** Backward induction (cheat sheet §3) together with the threshold from part (c) (cheat sheet §4): with one product, the threshold at time $t$ is just the value of keeping the product for the remaining offers.

**Definitions.** Write $V_\tau$ for $V_\tau(1)$, the value of holding the single product with $\tau$ offers still to come. By part (c) with $r=1$ and $V_\tau(0)=0$, at time $t$ the seller sells offer $x$ if and only if

$$x\ge V_{T-t}.$$

**Solution.**

Step 1, the Bellman recursion with $V_0=0$: the next offer is $1$ or $2$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each, and the seller takes the better of selling and keeping.

$$V_\tau=\tfrac12\max\{1,V_{\tau-1}\}+\tfrac12\max\{2,V_{\tau-1}\}$$

Step 2, **Claim.** For every $\tau\ge1$,

$$V_\tau=2-2^{-\tau}.$$

In particular $V_\tau$ lies in the interval $[\tfrac32,2)$. The proof is by induction on $\tau$, upwards from $1$.

**Base.** For $\tau=1$, since $V_0=0$:

$$V_1=\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac12\cdot2=\tfrac32.$$

**Hypothesis.** For some $\tau\ge2$, $V_{\tau-1}=2-2^{-(\tau-1)}$, so $V_{\tau-1}$ is at least $\tfrac32$ and below $2$.

**Step.** By the hypothesis, $\max\{1,V_{\tau-1}\}=V_{\tau-1}$ and $\max\{2,V_{\tau-1}\}=2$. This is the only place the hypothesis is used. Substituting into Step 1:

$$\begin{aligned}
V_\tau&=\tfrac12V_{\tau-1}+1\\
&=\tfrac12\big(2-2^{-(\tau-1)}\big)+1\\
&=2-2^{-\tau}.
\end{aligned}$$

Step 3, the optimal policy: at $t=T$ the threshold is $V_0=0$, so both offers are sold. At $t<T$ the threshold is $V_{T-t}$, which is at least $\tfrac32$ and below $2$: offer $1$ is held (strictly better) and offer $2$ is sold (strictly better). For example, with $T=3$:

| Time $t$ | Threshold | Offer $1$ | Offer $2$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1$ | $\tfrac74$ | hold | sell |
| $2$ | $\tfrac32$ | hold | sell |
| $3$ | $0$ | sell | sell |

Step 4, the four options.

- Option 1 (always sell): only $\boxed{T=1}$. For $T\ge2$, at $t=1$ the offer $1$ is below the threshold $V_{T-1}$, so holding is strictly better.
- Option 2 (at $t=1$ hold $1$, sell $2$): every $\boxed{T\ge2}$, because then $t=1$ is not the last step and the threshold $V_{T-1}$ lies strictly between $1$ and $2$. For $T=1$ the offer $1$ must be sold.
- Option 3 (at $t=2$ sell $1$, hold $2$): no $T$ at all. It needs $T\ge2$; selling $1$ at $t=2$ is optimal only when $V_{T-2}\le1$, that is $T=2$, but then the threshold is $0$ and holding $2$ loses $2$. It is also "sell low, hold high", which part (c) rules out.
- Option 4 (always hold): no $T$ at all. At $t=T$ holding earns $0$ while selling earns at least $1$.

**In words.** Keeping the product is worth almost $2$ when many offers remain, so an offer of $1$ is never worth taking early. Only at the very end, when keeping is worth nothing, does the seller accept $1$.

**Sanity check.** As $\tau$ grows, $V_\tau$ tends to $2$: with many offers left, an offer of $2$ is almost sure to arrive. And $V_1=\tfrac32$ is simply the average offer, which is what one forced sale earns.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Recursion from $V_0=0$:

$$V_\tau=\tfrac12\max\{1,V_{\tau-1}\}+\tfrac12\max\{2,V_{\tau-1}\}$$

- Induction gives $V_\tau=2-2^{-\tau}$ for $\tau\ge1$.
- Last step: threshold $0$, sell both. Earlier steps: threshold in $[\tfrac32,2)$, hold $1$, sell $2$.
- Option 1 only for $T=1$; option 2 for every $T\ge2$; options 3 and 4 never.

*Verified: exact DP gives $V_\tau=2-2^{-\tau}$ for $\tau\le14$, and checking the four options for $T=1,\dots,7$ gives option 1 only at $T=1$, option 2 at every $T\ge2$, options 3 and 4 never.*

**Common mistakes.**

- Leaving the current offer out of the state. Then the decision cannot depend on the price, and the threshold structure is lost.
- In (b), answering "yes" because extra stock never hurts. Never decreasing is not the same as strictly increasing; beyond $k=T$ the profit is flat.
- In (d), using the threshold $(1.5,2)$ at every early step and forgetting the last step, where the threshold is $0$ and every offer is sold.

**💡 Trick.** The optimal rule is a price **threshold** (sell iff the offer beats the value of waiting), which immediately gives (c) — "sell high, hold low" is exactly a threshold — and (d) — early steps hold the low price, the last step sells anything. And you can sell at most one item per step, so profit saturates once $k\ge T$, answering (b).

**⚠️ Watch out.** The threshold only ever says "sell high, hold low," never the reverse, so Option 3 (sell $1$, hold $2$) is impossible — don't be tempted to fit a $T$ to it. And "more products" helps only up to $k=T$; beyond that the answer to (b) is a firm "no, not strictly."

## Q2 (30 pts) — Identifying the k-th order statistic of the arm means
**Topics:** stochastic multi-armed bandits, order-statistic / best-arm identification, Hoeffding + union bound, explore-then-commit, confidence intervals | **Pillar:** Bandits | **Difficulty:** 5
**Maps to:** lecture_10
**Statement (English translation):**
A MAB with $n$ arms; arm $a_i$ has a Bernoulli reward with parameter $p_i$, and there is a gap $\Delta>0$ with $|p_i-p_j|\ge\Delta$ for all $i\ne j$. For a set $S\subset[0,1]^n$, the **k-statistic** is the $k$-th smallest value: sorting $x_1\le\cdots\le x_n$, it is $x_k$. Let $p_k$ be the k-statistic of $S=\{p_i:i\in[n]\}$. An explore-exploit algorithm with parameter $m$: **explore** — sample each arm $i$ exactly $m$ times; **exploit** — compute each empirical mean $\hat p_i$, form $\hat S=\{\hat p_i\}$, take its k-statistic $\hat p_k$, and return the matching index $\hat\imath_k$.

a. Find $m>0$ as a function of $n,\delta,\varepsilon$ such that with probability $\ge1-\delta$, $|\hat p_i-p_i|\le\varepsilon/2$ for every arm $i$ (as small as possible up to the multiplicative constant).

b. Define $S_0=\{i:p_i\le p_k-\varepsilon\}$, $S_1=\{i:p_i\ge p_k+\varepsilon\}$, $T_0=\{i:p_i\le p_k\}$, $T_1=\{i:p_i\ge p_k\}$. Assuming (from part a) $|\hat p_i-p_i|\le\varepsilon/2$ for all $i$, prove: (1) for $i\in S_1$ and $j\in T_0$, $\hat p_i\ge\hat p_j$; (2) for $i\in S_0$ and $j\in T_1$, $\hat p_i\le\hat p_j$.

c. Prove that $\hat\imath_k\notin S_1\cup S_0$. Hint: use part (b).

d. Show that $|p_{\hat\imath_k}-p_k|\le\varepsilon$.

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) How many samples per arm**

**What is asked.** A number of samples $m$, as a function of $n$, $\delta$ and $\varepsilon$, such that with probability at least $1-\delta$ every empirical mean is within $\varepsilon/2$ of its true mean. The answer is a formula.

**Tool and why.** Hoeffding's inequality for one arm, then a union bound over all $n$ arms (cheat sheet §10). Hoeffding fits because each reward lies in $[0,1]$ and the $m$ samples of an arm are independent; the union bound fits because we need all arms to be accurate at the same time.

**Definitions.** Let $E_i$ be the bad event of arm $i$:

$$E_i=\big\{|\hat p_i-p_i|>\varepsilon/2\big\}.$$

We introduce it so that "all arms are accurate" becomes "no $E_i$ happens".

**Solution.**

Step 1, one arm: $\hat p_i$ is an average of $m$ independent rewards in $[0,1]$, so Hoeffding with deviation $s$ gives $2e^{-2ms^2}$. With $s=\varepsilon/2$:

$$\begin{aligned}
\Pr[E_i]&\le2e^{-2m(\varepsilon/2)^2}\\
&=2e^{-m\varepsilon^2/2}.
\end{aligned}$$

Step 2, all arms: by the union bound, the probability that at least one arm is inaccurate is at most the sum over arms.

$$\Pr\Big[\bigcup_{i=1}^nE_i\Big]\le2n\,e^{-m\varepsilon^2/2}$$

Step 3, force the bound below $\delta$ and solve for $m$: divide by $2n$, take logarithms, and multiply by $-2/\varepsilon^2$, which flips the inequality.

$$\begin{aligned}
2n\,e^{-m\varepsilon^2/2}&\le\delta\\
-\tfrac{m\varepsilon^2}{2}&\le\ln\tfrac{\delta}{2n}\\
m&\ge\tfrac{2}{\varepsilon^2}\ln\tfrac{2n}{\delta}.
\end{aligned}$$

So the smallest integer that works is

$$m=\Big\lceil\frac{2}{\varepsilon^2}\ln\!\frac{2n}{\delta}\Big\rceil.$$

This is the standard order $\ln(n/\delta)/\varepsilon^2$, and the question accepts it up to the multiplicative constant.

**In words.** Accuracy $\varepsilon/2$ costs $1/\varepsilon^2$ samples, and asking it of $n$ arms at once costs only a logarithmic factor $\ln n$.

**Sanity check.** For $n=10$, $\delta=0.05$ and $\varepsilon=0.1$ the formula gives $m=1199$, and the union bound is then $0.0498$, just below $\delta$. A simulation with these values fails much less often, at a rate of about $0.001$, because the union bound and Hoeffding are both loose.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Hoeffding for one arm: the failure probability is at most $2e^{-m\varepsilon^2/2}$.
- Union bound over $n$ arms: at most $2n\,e^{-m\varepsilon^2/2}$.
- Set this to $\delta$, so $m=\lceil\tfrac{2}{\varepsilon^2}\ln\tfrac{2n}{\delta}\rceil$.

*Verified: for $n=10$, $\delta=0.05$, $\varepsilon=0.1$ the formula gives $m=1199$ with bound $0.0498$, and $4000$ simulated runs failed at rate $0.00125$.*

**(b) Far arms stay on the correct side**

**What is asked.** Two inequalities between empirical means, assuming every arm is accurate to $\varepsilon/2$: arms of $S_1$ are empirically at least as high as arms of $T_0$, and arms of $S_0$ at least as low as arms of $T_1$. The answer is a proof.

**Tool and why.** Confidence intervals (cheat sheet §10): each empirical mean lies in $[p_i-\varepsilon/2,\,p_i+\varepsilon/2]$. The sets are defined with a margin $\varepsilon$ around $p_k$, and two half-widths $\varepsilon/2$ fit exactly into that margin.

**Definitions.** No new notation. We use the event from part (a): $|\hat p_i-p_i|\le\varepsilon/2$ for every $i$.

**Solution.**

**Claim (1).** If $i\in S_1$ and $j\in T_0$, then $\hat p_i\ge\hat p_j$.

Step 1, a lower bound on $\hat p_i$: by accuracy and then by the definition of $S_1$, which says $p_i\ge p_k+\varepsilon$.

$$\begin{aligned}
\hat p_i&\ge p_i-\tfrac\varepsilon2\\
&\ge p_k+\varepsilon-\tfrac\varepsilon2\\
&=p_k+\tfrac\varepsilon2.
\end{aligned}$$

Step 2, an upper bound on $\hat p_j$: by accuracy and then by the definition of $T_0$, which says $p_j\le p_k$.

$$\begin{aligned}
\hat p_j&\le p_j+\tfrac\varepsilon2\\
&\le p_k+\tfrac\varepsilon2.
\end{aligned}$$

Step 3, combine: both are compared with the same number $p_k+\tfrac\varepsilon2$, so $\hat p_i\ge\hat p_j$.

**Claim (2).** If $i\in S_0$ and $j\in T_1$, then $\hat p_i\le\hat p_j$.

Step 4, the mirror image: $S_0$ gives $p_i\le p_k-\varepsilon$ and $T_1$ gives $p_j\ge p_k$, so

$$\begin{aligned}
\hat p_i&\le p_i+\tfrac\varepsilon2\le p_k-\tfrac\varepsilon2,\\
\hat p_j&\ge p_j-\tfrac\varepsilon2\ge p_k-\tfrac\varepsilon2.
\end{aligned}$$

Both are compared with $p_k-\tfrac\varepsilon2$, so $\hat p_i\le\hat p_j$.

**In words.** The number $p_k+\varepsilon/2$ is a fence: arms of $S_1$ cannot fall below it, and arms of $T_0$ cannot climb above it. The same holds for $p_k-\varepsilon/2$ with $S_0$ and $T_1$.

**Sanity check.** Equality in Claim (1) needs both bounds to be tight at once: $p_i=p_k+\varepsilon$ with $\hat p_i$ at the bottom of its interval, and $p_j=p_k$ with $\hat p_j$ at the top. In every other case the inequality is strict.

**What to write in the exam.**

- For $i\in S_1$: $\hat p_i\ge p_i-\tfrac\varepsilon2$, which is at least $p_k+\tfrac\varepsilon2$.
- For $j\in T_0$: $\hat p_j\le p_j+\tfrac\varepsilon2$, which is at most $p_k+\tfrac\varepsilon2$.
- Hence $\hat p_i\ge\hat p_j$; part (2) is symmetric around $p_k-\tfrac\varepsilon2$.

*Verified: $10^5$ random instances with every $\hat p_i$ inside its $\varepsilon/2$ interval, many of them on the interval edges, never violated (1) or (2).*

**(c) The returned arm is not far from $p_k$**

**What is asked.** A proof that the returned index $\hat\imath_k$ belongs to neither $S_0$ nor $S_1$. The answer is a proof.

**Tool and why.** A rank (counting) argument by contradiction, using part (b) (cheat sheet §10): an arm in $S_1$ has too many arms below it to be $k$-th, and an arm in $S_0$ has too many arms above it.

**Definitions.** The empirical rank of an arm is its position when the empirical means are sorted in increasing order; $\hat\imath_k$ is the arm in position $k$. Because any two means differ by at least $\Delta$, and $\Delta>0$, the true means are distinct, so exactly $k$ arms have $p_i\le p_k$ and exactly $n-k+1$ have $p_i\ge p_k$:

$$|T_0|=k,\qquad |T_1|=n-k+1.$$

We assume here that no two empirical means are equal; the tie case is treated at the end.

**Solution.**

**Claim.** $\hat\imath_k\notin S_1$ and $\hat\imath_k\notin S_0$.

Step 1, suppose $\hat\imath_k\in S_1$. Then its true mean is at least $p_k+\varepsilon$, which is above $p_k$, so $\hat\imath_k\notin T_0$. By part (b)(1), $\hat p_{\hat\imath_k}\ge\hat p_j$ for each of the $k$ arms $j$ of $T_0$, and with no ties these are strict. So at least $k$ other arms come before $\hat\imath_k$ in the sorted order, and its position is at least $k+1$. This contradicts position $k$.

Step 2, suppose $\hat\imath_k\in S_0$. Then $\hat\imath_k\notin T_1$, and by part (b)(2), $\hat p_{\hat\imath_k}\le\hat p_j$ for each of the $n-k+1$ arms of $T_1$. So at least $n-k+1$ other arms come after it. The arms before it are the remaining ones, at most

$$n-1-(n-k+1)=k-2.$$

So its position is at most $k-1$, again a contradiction.

Step 3, conclusion: $\hat\imath_k\notin S_0\cup S_1$.

**The tie case.** If equal empirical means are sorted in an arbitrary order, the claim can fail, but only on an exact tie. Example: $n=2$, $k=1$, $\varepsilon=0.2$, true means $0.5$ and $0.7$, and both empirical means equal to $0.6$; both are within $0.1$ of their true means. If the sort puts arm $2$ first, then $\hat\imath_1=2$, which is in $S_1$. In such a case the true mean of $\hat\imath_k$ is exactly $p_k\pm\varepsilon$, so part (d) still holds.

**In words.** The fences from part (b) keep all $k$ arms of $T_0$ empirically below any arm of $S_1$, so an $S_1$ arm is pushed to position $k+1$ or later. Symmetrically, an $S_0$ arm has $n-k+1$ arms above it and is pushed to position $k-1$ or earlier.

**Sanity check.** If $\varepsilon<\Delta$, every arm other than the true $k$-th one is in $S_0$ or $S_1$, so the algorithm returns exactly the true $k$-th arm.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Exactly $k$ arms in $T_0$ and $n-k+1$ in $T_1$, since the means are distinct.
- If $\hat\imath_k\in S_1$: by (b)(1) all $k$ arms of $T_0$ are below it, so its rank is at least $k+1$.
- If $\hat\imath_k\in S_0$: by (b)(2) all $n-k+1$ arms of $T_1$ are above it, so its rank is at most $k-1$.
- Both contradict rank $k$.

*Verified: in $10^5$ random accurate instances, the returned arm was in $S_0\cup S_1$ only when its empirical mean tied with another arm's (218 cases, all with distance exactly $\varepsilon$).*

**(d) The distance bound**

**What is asked.** That the true mean of the returned arm is within $\varepsilon$ of $p_k$. The answer is a short proof.

**Tool and why.** Translate part (c) back into the definitions of $S_0$ and $S_1$ (cheat sheet §10): not belonging to either set is exactly a statement about distance from $p_k$.

**Definitions.** No new notation.

**Solution.**

Step 1, by part (c), $\hat\imath_k\notin S_1$, so by the definition of $S_1$:

$$p_{\hat\imath_k}<p_k+\varepsilon.$$

Step 2, also $\hat\imath_k\notin S_0$, so by the definition of $S_0$:

$$p_{\hat\imath_k}>p_k-\varepsilon.$$

Step 3, together:

$$\boxed{|p_{\hat\imath_k}-p_k|\le\varepsilon}$$

Step 4, the tie case directly: suppose $p_{\hat\imath_k}>p_k+\varepsilon$. Then, as in part (b),

$$\hat p_{\hat\imath_k}>p_k+\tfrac\varepsilon2\ge\hat p_j$$

for every $j\in T_0$, strictly. So $k$ arms are strictly below it whatever the tie-breaking, and its position is at least $k+1$, a contradiction. The case $p_{\hat\imath_k}<p_k-\varepsilon$ is symmetric. So the bound holds in every case.

**In words.** With probability at least $1-\delta$ the algorithm returns an arm whose true mean is within $\varepsilon$ of the $k$-th smallest mean, using $m$ samples per arm from part (a).

**Sanity check.** With $k=n$ the $k$-statistic is the largest mean, and the result says the algorithm finds an $\varepsilon$-best arm, the usual guarantee of explore-then-commit.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Not in $S_1$ means $p_{\hat\imath_k}<p_k+\varepsilon$.
- Not in $S_0$ means $p_{\hat\imath_k}>p_k-\varepsilon$.
- Hence $|p_{\hat\imath_k}-p_k|\le\varepsilon$, with probability at least $1-\delta$ by part (a).

*Verified: the same $10^5$ random instances, with random tie-breaking, never gave distance above $\varepsilon$.*

**Common mistakes.**

- Using radius $\varepsilon$ instead of $\varepsilon/2$. Then the fences overlap and part (b) fails.
- Applying Hoeffding to a single arm and forgetting the union bound. The confidence is then $1-\delta$ per arm, not for all arms together, and $m$ lacks the $\ln n$ factor.
- Counting off by one in part (c): an arm with $k$ arms below it has rank $k+1$, and an arm with $n-k+1$ arms above it has at most $k-2$ arms below, so rank at most $k-1$.

**💡 Trick.** The whole proof is "estimate everything to $\pm\varepsilon/2$, then a rank argument." An $\varepsilon/2$ confidence radius (Hoeffding + union over $n$ arms) keeps every empirical mean on the correct side of $p_k\pm\varepsilon/2$; then any arm far from $p_k$ (in $S_0$ or $S_1$) is pushed past $\ge k$ or $\ge n-k+1$ competitors, so its empirical rank can't be exactly $k$ (except on an exact tie, where the distance is exactly $\varepsilon$).

**⚠️ Watch out.** Match the radius to the margin: with buffers $S_{0/1}$ at $\pm\varepsilon$ you need each estimate accurate to $\varepsilon/2$ (not $\varepsilon$) so the $T$-sets and $S$-sets separate. And the union bound is over **all $n$ arms** (the $\ln(2n/\delta)$ factor) — using a single-arm Hoeffding without the $n$ gives the wrong confidence.

## Q3 (20 pts) — Value approximation with a bilinear model (MC-SGD)
**Topics:** value-function approximation, bilinear/linear-in-$\theta$ model, gradient descent, Monte-Carlo return, stochastic gradient | **Pillar:** Approximation | **Difficulty:** 3
**Maps to:** lecture_08, lecture_06
**Statement (English translation):**
Given an MDP where every state $s$ has a feature vector $\phi(s)\in\mathbb{R}^d$. A value-function approximator with parameter $\theta\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is $\hat V(s;\theta)=\theta^\top M\phi(s)$, where $M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is a known fixed matrix. For a policy $\pi$ we minimize
$$J^\pi(\theta)=\frac12\sum_s\mu(s)\big(V^\pi(s)-\hat V(s;\theta)\big)^2,$$
where $\mu(s)$ is the probability that policy $\pi$ reaches state $s$ (assume a state is not visited twice in the same run). We minimize by gradient updates.

a. Write the gradient-descent update for minimizing $J^\pi(\theta)$ (as a function of $\phi(s),\mu(s),M,V^\pi(s)$ and learning rate $\alpha$).

b. Propose an unbiased Monte-Carlo estimate of $V^\pi(s)$ from an episode of $\pi$ in which $s$ appears.

c. Write the Monte-Carlo SGD update for minimizing $J^\pi(\theta)$.

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) The gradient-descent update**

**What is asked.** The full-gradient update of $\theta$ that minimizes $J^\pi(\theta)$, written with $\phi(s)$, $\mu(s)$, $M$, $V^\pi(s)$ and the step size $\alpha$. The answer is a formula.

**Tool and why.** Gradient descent on a weighted squared error (cheat sheet §8): the step is $-\alpha\nabla_\theta J^\pi$, and the gradient follows from the chain rule. The model is linear in $\theta$, so its gradient is a fixed vector.

**Definitions.** Let $x(s)$ be the effective feature vector:

$$x(s)=M\phi(s)\in\mathbb{R}^d.$$

We introduce it because then $\hat V(s;\theta)=\theta^\top x(s)$, an ordinary linear model in the features $x(s)$.

**Solution.**

Step 1, gradient of the model: $\theta^\top x(s)$ is linear in $\theta$, and $M$ is fixed, so

$$\nabla_\theta\hat V(s;\theta)=x(s)=M\phi(s).$$

Step 2, gradient of one squared term, by the chain rule: the outer derivative of $\tfrac12u^2$ is $u$, and the inner derivative of $V^\pi(s)-\hat V(s;\theta)$ is $-M\phi(s)$, because $V^\pi(s)$ does not depend on $\theta$.

$$\nabla_\theta\tfrac12\big(V^\pi(s)-\hat V(s;\theta)\big)^2=-\big(V^\pi(s)-\hat V(s;\theta)\big)M\phi(s)$$

Step 3, sum over states with weights $\mu(s)$, since the gradient of a sum is the sum of gradients:

$$\nabla_\theta J^\pi(\theta)=\sum_s\mu(s)\big(\hat V(s;\theta)-V^\pi(s)\big)M\phi(s).$$

Step 4, the update $\theta^{t+1}=\theta^t-\alpha\nabla_\theta J^\pi(\theta^t)$:

$$\boxed{\theta^{t+1}=\theta^{t}+\alpha\sum_s\mu(s)\big(V^\pi(s)-\theta^{t\top}M\phi(s)\big)\,M\phi(s)}$$

**In words.** Each state pushes $\theta$ in the direction of its effective feature $M\phi(s)$, by an amount equal to its error, and frequently reached states push harder.

**Sanity check.** If $\hat V(s;\theta)=V^\pi(s)$ for every state, every error is $0$ and $\theta$ does not move, as it should at a perfect fit.

**What to write in the exam.**

- The model is linear in $\theta$, with gradient $M\phi(s)$.
- Chain rule, then the descent step:

$$\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha\sum_s\mu(s)\big(V^\pi(s)-\hat V(s;\theta)\big)M\phi(s)$$

*Verified: on a random acyclic MDP with $d=3$ the formula matches central finite differences of $J^\pi$ to within $10^{-9}$.*

**(b) An unbiased Monte-Carlo estimate of $V^\pi(s)$**

**What is asked.** A quantity computed from one episode of $\pi$ that contains $s$, whose expectation is $V^\pi(s)$. The answer is a formula with a one-line justification.

**Tool and why.** Monte-Carlo evaluation (cheat sheet §7): by definition $V^\pi(s)$ is the expected discounted return from $s$ (cheat sheet §1), so the return observed from $s$ is a sample of exactly that random variable. It needs no model of the transitions.

**Definitions.** Let $t_s$ be the time at which the episode is at $s$; it is unique because a state is never visited twice in a run. Let $G_s$ be the discounted return from that time:

$$G_s=\sum_{j\ge0}\gamma^{\,j}\,r_{t_s+j}.$$

**Solution.**

Step 1, the estimate: use $G_s$, the sum of discounted rewards from the visit to $s$ until the episode ends.

Step 2, unbiasedness: by the Markov property, given that the episode is at $s$ at time $t_s$, the rest of the episode is distributed like a fresh run of $\pi$ started at $s$. The expectation of its discounted return is $V^\pi(s)$ by definition, so

$$\boxed{\mathbb{E}\big[G_s\mid s_{t_s}=s\big]=V^\pi(s)}$$

**In words.** To learn what a state is worth, see what you actually collected after visiting it; on average this is exactly its value.

**Sanity check.** At a state from which the episode ends immediately with reward $r$, the estimate is just $r$, which is its value.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Estimate: the return $G_s=\sum_{j\ge0}\gamma^{\,j}r_{t_s+j}$ from the visit to $s$.
- Unbiased by the Markov property and the definition of $V^\pi$.

*Verified: $10^5$ simulated episodes give mean returns within $0.004$ of the exact $V^\pi(s)$ for every state.*

**(c) The Monte-Carlo SGD update**

**What is asked.** A stochastic-gradient update for $J^\pi$ that uses only sampled episodes, not $\mu$ or $V^\pi$. The answer is a formula.

**Tool and why.** MC-SGD (cheat sheet §8): replace the unknown $V^\pi(s)$ by the return from part (b), and replace the weighted sum over states by the states that the episode actually visits. Both substitutions keep the gradient estimate unbiased.

**Definitions.** Let $s_0,s_1,\dots$ be the states of one episode of $\pi$, and $G_t$ the return from time $t$, as in part (b).

**Solution.**

Step 1, the sampled gradient: for each visited state $s_t$, replace $V^\pi(s_t)$ by $G_t$ in the gradient term from part (a):

$$g_t=\big(\hat V(s_t;\theta)-G_t\big)M\phi(s_t).$$

Step 2, why it is unbiased: a state is visited at most once, so for each $s$ the probability that the episode contains $s$ is exactly $\mu(s)$. Taking expectations of the sum over the episode, state by state:

$$\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_tg_t\Big]&=\sum_s\mu(s)\big(\hat V(s;\theta)-\mathbb{E}[G_s\mid s]\big)M\phi(s)\\
&=\sum_s\mu(s)\big(\hat V(s;\theta)-V^\pi(s)\big)M\phi(s)\\
&=\nabla_\theta J^\pi(\theta).
\end{aligned}$$

The first line uses that $g_t$ is linear in $G_t$, the second uses part (b), and the third is part (a).

Step 3, the update, applied at each visited state:

$$\boxed{\theta^{t+1}=\theta^{t}+\alpha\big(G_t-\theta^{t\top}M\phi(s_t)\big)M\phi(s_t)}$$

**In words.** This is part (a) with two substitutions: the true value becomes the observed return, and the weight $\mu(s)$ is supplied by how often the policy actually reaches $s$.

**Sanity check.** With $M=I$ the update is the familiar linear MC update with features $\phi(s)$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Run an episode of $\pi$ and compute the return $G_t$ at each visited state.
- Update at each visited state:

$$\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha\big(G_t-\theta^\top M\phi(s_t)\big)M\phi(s_t)$$

- Unbiased: states appear with probability $\mu(s)$, and $\mathbb{E}[G_t]=V^\pi(s_t)$.

*Verified: averaging the episode-sum $\sum_tg_t$ over $10^5$ simulated episodes reproduces the exact gradient of part (a) to within about $0.01$ per coordinate.*

**Common mistakes.**

- Differentiating $M$ as if it were a parameter, or writing $M^\top\phi(s)$. Only $\theta$ is learned, and the gradient of $\theta^\top M\phi(s)$ is $M\phi(s)$.
- Getting the sign wrong. Gradient descent subtracts $\alpha(\hat V-V^\pi)M\phi(s)$, so it adds $\alpha(V^\pi-\hat V)M\phi(s)$.
- Using the immediate reward, or a bootstrapped target, instead of the full return. Only the full return is an unbiased estimate of $V^\pi(s)$.

**💡 Trick.** Same MC-SGD template; only the model derivative changes. Because $\hat V=\theta^\top M\phi(s)$ is **linear** in $\theta$, its gradient is simply the fixed vector $M\phi(s)$ (a re-featurization of $\phi$ by $M$). Sampling states on-policy supplies $\mu$; the return $G_t$ supplies an unbiased $V^\pi(s_t)$.

**⚠️ Watch out.** Since $M$ is **fixed/known** (not a parameter), you differentiate only w.r.t. $\theta$: the gradient is $M\phi(s)$, not anything involving $\nabla M$. Treat $M\phi(s)$ as the effective feature; the objective is convex quadratic in $\theta$ (unlike a squared or exponential head).

## Q4 (20 pts) — Contraction of a min–min operator (and its trivial fixed point)
**Topics:** Bellman-type $Q$ operator, $\gamma$-contraction in sup-norm, fixed point, non-negativity of rewards | **Pillar:** Planning | **Difficulty:** 3
**Maps to:** lecture_04, lecture_03
**Statement (English translation):**
Given an MDP $M=(S,A,p,s_0,r)$ with $r(s,a)\in[0,1]$ for all $s,a$. Define the operator $T_M$ for $U\in\mathbb{R}^{|S\times A|}$, with $0<\gamma<1$:
$$(T_MU)(s,a)=\min\Big\{r(s,a),\ \gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'\sim p(\cdot\mid s,a)}\big[\min_{a'}U(s',a')\big]\Big\}.$$

a. Write the definition of when an operator $H$ is $\gamma$-contracting for the norm $\|\cdot\|_\infty$.

b. Prove that $T_M$ is $\gamma$-contracting for the norm $\|\cdot\|_\infty$. Hint: it can be proved for each $(s,a)$ separately, splitting into cases by which term attains the min.

c. Let $U^*$ be the fixed point of $T_M$. What is $U^*(s,a)$?

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) Definition of a $\gamma$-contraction**

**What is asked.** The definition of "$H$ is a $\gamma$-contraction in the sup norm". The answer is a definition.

**Tool and why.** Operators and contractions (cheat sheet §6): a contraction shrinks the distance between any two inputs by at least the factor $\gamma$.

**Definitions.** The sup norm of $U$ is its largest absolute entry:

$$\|U\|_\infty=\max_{s,a}|U(s,a)|.$$

**Solution.**

Step 1, the definition: $H$ is a $\gamma$-contraction in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$, with $\gamma\in(0,1)$, if for all $U,U'\in\mathbb{R}^{|S\times A|}$:

$$\boxed{\|HU-HU'\|_\infty\le\gamma\,\|U-U'\|_\infty}$$

Step 2, what it gives: by Banach's fixed-point theorem, a contraction has a unique fixed point, and iterating $H$ from any start converges to it.

**In words.** Applying $H$ brings any two functions closer together, uniformly over all state-action pairs, by a factor of at least $\gamma$.

**Sanity check.** The constant operator $HU=c$ satisfies the definition for every $\gamma$, since the left side is $0$; its unique fixed point is $c$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- For all $U$ and $U'$: $\|HU-HU'\|_\infty\le\gamma\|U-U'\|_\infty$, with $\gamma<1$.

*Verified: the definition is checked numerically in part (b) on random MDPs.*

**(b) $T_M$ is a $\gamma$-contraction**

**What is asked.** A proof that $T_M$ satisfies the definition from part (a). The answer is a proof.

**Tool and why.** The sup-norm contraction argument (cheat sheet §6), done separately for each $(s,a)$ as the hint suggests. Two small lemmas do all the work: a minimum with a constant does not increase distances, and neither does a minimum over actions.

**Definitions.** Let $D=\|U-U'\|_\infty$. For a fixed $(s,a)$ let $y$ and $y'$ be the second terms inside the minimum:

$$y=\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'}\big[\min_{a'}U(s',a')\big],\qquad y'=\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'}\big[\min_{a'}U'(s',a')\big].$$

With $c=r(s,a)$, we have $(T_MU)(s,a)=\min\{c,y\}$ and $(T_MU')(s,a)=\min\{c,y'\}$.

**Solution.**

Step 1, **Claim A.** For any numbers $c$, $y$ and $y'$:

$$\big|\min\{c,y\}-\min\{c,y'\}\big|\le|y-y'|.$$

By symmetry assume $y\le y'$. There are three cases, according to where $c$ lies.

- Case $c\le y$: both minima equal $c$, and the difference is $0$.
- Case $c$ between $y$ and $y'$: the minima are $y$ and $c$, and the difference is $c-y$, which is at most $y'-y$.
- Case $c\ge y'$: the minima are $y$ and $y'$, and the difference is $|y-y'|$.

Step 2, **Claim B.** For any two vectors $u$ and $u'$ indexed by actions:

$$\big|\min_{a'}u(a')-\min_{a'}u'(a')\big|\le\max_{a'}|u(a')-u'(a')|.$$

Let $a^*$ attain $\min_{a'}u'(a')$ and let $e=\max_{a'}|u(a')-u'(a')|$. Then

$$\begin{aligned}
\min_{a'}u(a')&\le u(a^*)\\
&\le u'(a^*)+e\\
&=\min_{a'}u'(a')+e.
\end{aligned}$$

Exchanging the roles of $u$ and $u'$ gives the other direction, so the difference is at most $e$ in absolute value.

Step 3, the bound on $|y-y'|$: take the difference inside the expectation, move the absolute value inside (the absolute value of an average is at most the average of absolute values), and apply Claim B with $u=U(s',\cdot)$ and $u'=U'(s',\cdot)$:

$$\begin{aligned}
|y-y'|&=\gamma\,\Big|\mathbb{E}_{s'}\big[\min_{a'}U(s',a')-\min_{a'}U'(s',a')\big]\Big|\\
&\le\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'}\Big|\min_{a'}U(s',a')-\min_{a'}U'(s',a')\Big|\\
&\le\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'}\max_{a'}|U(s',a')-U'(s',a')|\\
&\le\gamma D.
\end{aligned}$$

The last line holds because every entry of $U-U'$ is at most $D$ in absolute value.

Step 4, one coordinate: by Claim A with $c=r(s,a)$ and then Step 3:

$$\big|(T_MU)(s,a)-(T_MU')(s,a)\big|\le|y-y'|\le\gamma D.$$

Step 5, all coordinates: the bound of Step 4 holds for every $(s,a)$, so it holds for the maximum over $(s,a)$:

$$\boxed{\|T_MU-T_MU'\|_\infty\le\gamma\|U-U'\|_\infty}$$

By Banach's theorem $T_M$ has a unique fixed point $U^*$.

**In words.** The reward $r(s,a)$ is the same in both functions, and capping two numbers by the same constant cannot pull them further apart. All the shrinking comes from the factor $\gamma$ in front of the future term.

**Sanity check.** The proof never used $\min$ in a special way beyond Claims A and B, and both claims hold equally with $\max$. So the same proof shows the usual Bellman optimality operator is a $\gamma$-contraction, as expected.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Claim A: $|\min\{c,y\}-\min\{c,y'\}|\le|y-y'|$, by three cases.
- Claim B: the minimum over actions changes by at most the largest change over actions.
- Hence each coordinate of $T_MU-T_MU'$ is at most $\gamma\|U-U'\|_\infty$ in absolute value.
- Take the maximum over $(s,a)$; contraction, and a unique fixed point by Banach.

*Verified: on $3000$ random MDPs and random pairs $U,U'$ the ratio of the two sides never exceeded $\gamma$ beyond rounding error, and it reached $\gamma$, so the factor is tight; Claims A and B held on $10^6$ and $10^5$ random inputs.*

**(c) The fixed point**

**What is asked.** The value of $U^*(s,a)$, the fixed point of $T_M$. The answer is a function, with a check.

**Tool and why.** Guess and verify, then uniqueness (cheat sheet §6): if a candidate satisfies $T_MU=U$, it is the fixed point, because part (b) showed there is only one.

**Definitions.** Let $\mathbf{0}$ be the all-zeros function on $S\times A$.

**Solution.**

Step 1, the guess: try $U=\mathbf{0}$.

Step 2, the future term: for every $s'$ the minimum over actions of zeros is $0$, so the expectation is $0$, and so is $\gamma$ times it.

Step 3, the minimum: rewards are non-negative, so for every $(s,a)$

$$(T_M\mathbf{0})(s,a)=\min\{r(s,a),0\}=0.$$

Step 4, uniqueness: $T_M\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}$, and by part (b) the fixed point is unique, so

$$\boxed{U^*(s,a)=0\ \ \forall (s,a)}$$

**In words.** The operator takes the smaller of the reward and the discounted future. Since rewards are never negative, the future value $0$ always wins, and nothing can lift the value above it.

**Sanity check.** Non-negativity of the reward is essential. With one state, one action, reward $-0.5$ and $\gamma=0.5$, the fixed point is $-0.5$, not $0$, because $\min\{-0.5,\,0.5\cdot(-0.5)\}=-0.5$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Guess $U\equiv0$: the future term is $0$, and $\min\{r,0\}=0$ because $r\ge0$.
- So $T_M\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}$.
- By uniqueness from part (b), $U^*\equiv0$.

*Verified: $3000$ iterations of $T_M$ from random starts on $3000$ random MDPs end within $10^{-12}$ of $0$, and $T_M\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}$ exactly.*

**Common mistakes.**

- Proving only one direction of Claim B, or bounding the minimum over actions by the change at one fixed action. Both directions are needed for the absolute value.
- Forgetting that $r(s,a)$ is the same constant in both functions, and trying to bound $|r-r|$ or case-split on rewards that differ.
- Looking for a reward-dependent fixed point, like the max version's "reward plus a discounted tail". Here non-negative rewards make the answer $0$.

**💡 Trick.** Non-negativity does everything. Because $r\ge0$ and the look-ahead is a **min** shrunk by $\gamma$, the operator can never push a value above $0$ from below or hold it above $0$ — the discounted min drags the future term toward $0$ and $\min\{r,0\}=0$. So the unique fixed point is the all-zeros function.

**⚠️ Watch out.** Contrast with the **max** version (which yields "local reward $+$ a positive tail"): here every $\min$ and the $\gamma$-shrunk future collapse the fixed point to $0$. Don't look for a reward-dependent formula — the rewards' non-negativity is exactly why $U^*\equiv0$. The contraction proof is otherwise identical to the max case (the $|\min\{c,x\}-\min\{c,y\}|\le|x-y|$ lemma replaces its max twin).
