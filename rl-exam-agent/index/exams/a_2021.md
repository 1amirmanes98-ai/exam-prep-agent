## Q1 (30 pts) — Queue admission control: MDP and threshold optimal policy
**Topics:** MDP formalization, policy evaluation, structure of the optimal policy, threshold policy, monotonicity | **Pillar:** Planning | **Difficulty:** 4
**Maps to:** lecture_03, lecture_04
**Statement (English translation):**
A finite queue can hold up to $H$ units. At each time $t$:

(a) a unit arrives with value $v$ sampled from the uniform distribution over $\{0,\dots,N\}$;

(b) if the number of units currently in the queue is less than $H$ we decide whether to **add** the unit to the queue (gaining reward $v$) or **not add** it (gaining $0$); otherwise (the queue already holds $H$ units) the unit is not added;

(c) with probability $q$ the queue empties by one unit (one unit departs).
At $t=0$ the queue holds a single unit whose value is $v\sim\text{Uniform}\{0,\dots,N\}$. The return is discounted with parameter $\gamma>0$ ($\gamma<1$).

- **(a)** Write the MDP of the problem formally: give $S,\ A,\ s_0,\ R,\ P$.
- **(b)** What is the value function $V^\pi$ when the policy $\pi$ **always accepts whenever possible**?
- **(c1)** Let $\pi^*$ be the optimal policy (for the discounted return with $\gamma>0$). Suppose that at time $t$ the optimal policy accepts a unit of value $v$. Can we conclude that in the same state the optimal policy accepts **every** unit of value $v'>v$? (yes/no, explain.)
- **(c2)** Suppose that at time $t$ the optimal policy does **not** accept a unit of value $v$. Can we conclude that in the same state the optimal policy will not accept every unit of value $v'<v$? (yes/no, explain.)
- **(d)** Show that the optimal policy can be characterized by a sequence of thresholds $\theta_0,\dots,\theta_H$ such that a unit of value $v$ is accepted, when the queue holds $k$ units, if and only if $v\ge\theta_k$. (Hint: use part (c).)

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) The MDP, formally**

**What is asked.** Translate the queue story into the five components $S$, $A$, $s_0$, $R$, $P$, plus the discount. The answer is a definition, not a number.

**Tool and why.** Modelling a story as an MDP (cheat sheet §12): the state is what we must know at the moment of the decision. Here that is how full the queue is (it decides whether we may accept and how close we are to being blocked) and the value of the unit that is now waiting (it is the reward of accepting). The return is discounted over an infinite horizon (cheat sheet §1).

**Definitions.** Let $m$ be the number of units in the queue when the decision is made, and $v$ the value of the arriving unit. Let $m'$ be the count right after the decision, before the possible departure; we need it because the departure in rule (c) happens after the decision. We write $\text{acc}$ for "add the unit" and $\text{rej}$ for "do not add it". Let $D(j\mid m')$ be the probability that the count becomes $j$ after the departure step.

**Solution.**

Step 1, states: $s=(m,v)$, so

$$S=\{0,\dots,H\}\times\{0,\dots,N\}.$$

This is enough information: the arrivals are independent, so earlier values say nothing about the future, and the past acts on the future only through the current count.

Step 2, actions: $A=\{\text{acc},\text{rej}\}$. By rule (b), $\text{acc}$ is allowed only when $m<H$; when $m=H$ the only action is $\text{rej}$.

Step 3, initial state: $s_0=(1,v)$, where $v$ is uniform on $\{0,\dots,N\}$. The queue starts with one unit, and a fresh unit is waiting.

Step 4, rewards, by rule (b): accepting pays the value of the unit and rejecting pays nothing.

$$R\big((m,v),\text{acc}\big)=v\quad(m<H),\qquad R\big((m,v),\text{rej}\big)=0.$$

Step 5, count after the decision: $m'=m+1$ after $\text{acc}$, and $m'=m$ after $\text{rej}$.

Step 6, departure, by rule (c). A unit can leave only if there is one, so the case $m'=0$ is separate:

$$D(j\mid m')=\begin{cases}q, & j=m'-1,\ m'\ge1,\\ 1-q, & j=m',\ m'\ge1,\\ 1, & j=m'=0,\\ 0, & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

Step 7, transition kernel: the next value $v''$ is a fresh uniform draw, independent of everything else, so

$$P\big((j,v'')\mid(m,v),a\big)=\frac{1}{N+1}\,D(j\mid m').$$

Step 8, discount: $\gamma\in(0,1)$, infinite horizon.

**In words.** At every step we look at how full the queue is and at the offer in front of us; accepting pays now but uses a slot, and slots free up at random.

**Sanity check.** Each row of $P$ sums to $1$. For $m'\ge1$ the count moves to $m'-1$ or stays, with total probability $q+(1-q)$, and $v''$ has $N+1$ equally likely values. For $m'=0$ the count stays at $0$ with probability $1$. Without the separate case for $m'=0$ the probability $q$ of a departure from an empty queue would be lost.

**What to write in the exam.**

- States: $s=(m,v)$, occupancy and arriving value; start at $s_0=(1,v)$ with $v$ uniform.
- Actions: $\text{acc}$ (only if $m<H$) and $\text{rej}$.
- Rewards: $v$ for $\text{acc}$, $0$ for $\text{rej}$.
- Transitions: count $m'$ after the decision, then one departure with probability $q$ if $m'\ge1$, then a fresh uniform $v''$.

*Verified: the MDP coded in Python (exact fractions with sympy) has rows summing to $1$ and is the model used to check every number in parts (b)–(d).*

**(b) Value of the always-accept policy**

**What is asked.** The function $V^\pi(m,v)$ for the policy that accepts whenever $m<H$. The answer is a formula.

**Tool and why.** Bellman equation of a fixed policy (cheat sheet §2): there is no maximum, only the immediate reward plus the discounted expected value of the next state. The next value $v''$ is a fresh draw, so the continuation depends only on the next count. That splits $V^\pi$ into "$v$ now" plus a constant per count, and the constants solve a linear recurrence.

**Definitions.** Let $W_m=\mathbb E_{v}\big[V^\pi(m,v)\big]$ be the value at count $m$ averaged over the arriving value; it is what the continuation needs, because the next value is not known yet. Let

$$K=\frac{N}{2(1-\gamma)},\qquad \rho=\frac{\gamma(1-q)}{1-\gamma q}.$$

$K$ is the value of collecting the average offer $\tfrac N2$ at every step forever, and $\rho$ is the ratio that appears when we solve the recurrence. We assume $H\ge1$.

**Solution.**

Step 1, Bellman for $m<H$: the policy accepts, earns $v$, the count becomes $m+1$, and then a unit leaves with probability $q$:

$$V^\pi(m,v)=v+\gamma\big(q\,W_m+(1-q)\,W_{m+1}\big),\qquad m<H.$$

Step 2, Bellman for $m=H$: no unit can be added, the reward is $0$ and the count stays $H$ before the departure step:

$$V^\pi(H,v)=\gamma\big(q\,W_{H-1}+(1-q)\,W_H\big).$$

Step 3, structure: in both equations $v$ appears only in the immediate reward. So $V^\pi(m,v)$ is $v$ plus a constant when $m<H$, and a constant when $m=H$.

Step 4, average over $v$: the mean of a uniform value on $\{0,\dots,N\}$ is $\tfrac N2$, so

$$W_m=\tfrac N2+\gamma q\,W_m+\gamma(1-q)\,W_{m+1},\qquad m<H,$$

$$W_H=\gamma q\,W_{H-1}+\gamma(1-q)\,W_H.$$

This is a linear system with a unique solution, because policy evaluation with $\gamma<1$ is a contraction (cheat sheet §6).

**Claim.** For every $m\in\{0,\dots,H\}$,

$$W_m=K\big(1-(1-\gamma q)\,\rho^{H-m}\big).$$

We check that this vector satisfies both equations; by uniqueness it is the solution. Two facts are used throughout:

$$(1-\gamma q)\,\rho=\gamma(1-q),\qquad \tfrac N2+\gamma K=K.$$

The first is the definition of $\rho$; the second holds because $\tfrac N2=(1-\gamma)K$.

Step 5, the equation for $m=H$. The left side is $K\big(1-(1-\gamma q)\big)=K\gamma q$. The right side, using $(1-\gamma q)\rho=\gamma(1-q)$:

$$\begin{aligned}
\gamma q\,W_{H-1}+\gamma(1-q)\,W_H&=\gamma q\,K\big(1-\gamma(1-q)\big)+\gamma(1-q)\,K\gamma q\\
&=K\gamma q-K\gamma q\,\gamma(1-q)+K\gamma q\,\gamma(1-q)\\
&=K\gamma q.
\end{aligned}$$

Step 6, the equation for $m<H$. Write $c=1-\gamma q$, so that $W_m=K-Kc\,\rho^{H-m}$. Then

$$\begin{aligned}
\tfrac N2+\gamma q\,W_m+\gamma(1-q)\,W_{m+1}&=\tfrac N2+\gamma K-Kc\big(\gamma q\,\rho^{H-m}+\gamma(1-q)\,\rho^{H-m-1}\big)\\
&=K-Kc\big(\gamma q\,\rho^{H-m}+(1-\gamma q)\,\rho^{H-m}\big)\\
&=K-Kc\,\rho^{H-m}\\
&=W_m.
\end{aligned}$$

The second row uses both facts: $\tfrac N2+\gamma K=K$, and $\gamma(1-q)\rho^{H-m-1}=(1-\gamma q)\rho^{H-m}$. The third row uses $\gamma q+1-\gamma q=1$. This proves the claim.

Step 7, back to $V^\pi$: averaging Step 1 over $v$ gives $W_m=\tfrac N2+(\text{the constant})$, so the constant is $W_m-\tfrac N2$. Hence

$$\boxed{V^\pi(m,v)=\begin{cases}v-\tfrac N2+W_m, & m<H,\\ W_H, & m=H,\end{cases}\qquad W_m=K\big(1-(1-\gamma q)\rho^{H-m}\big)}$$

Step 8, a worked example with $H=2$, $N=4$, $q=\tfrac12$ and $\gamma=\tfrac{9}{10}$. Here $K=20$, $\rho=\tfrac{9}{11}$ and $1-\gamma q=\tfrac{11}{20}$:

| $m$ | $W_m$ | $V^\pi(m,v)$ |
|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $\tfrac{139}{11}\approx12.64$ | $v+\tfrac{117}{11}$ |
| $1$ | $11$ | $v+9$ |
| $2$ | $9$ | $9$ |

The expected value from the random start $s_0=(1,v)$ is $W_1=11$.

**In words.** $K$ would be the value if the queue never filled up. The correction $(1-\gamma q)\rho^{H-m}$ is the loss from the steps on which the queue is full and offers must be turned away; it is larger when $m$ is closer to $H$.

**Sanity check.** With $q=0$ nothing ever leaves: $\rho=\gamma$ and $W_m=\tfrac N2\big(1+\gamma+\dots+\gamma^{H-m-1}\big)$, exactly $H-m$ accepted offers and then a blocked queue forever. With $q=1$ a unit leaves every step: $\rho=0$, so $W_m=K$ for every $m<H$, because the queue never fills.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Bellman for $m<H$: $v$ plus $\gamma\big(qW_m+(1-q)W_{m+1}\big)$; for $m=H$ only the continuation.
- Structure: $V^\pi(m,v)$ is $v-\tfrac N2+W_m$ below $H$, and $W_H$ at $H$.
- Recurrence: $W_m=\tfrac N2+\gamma qW_m+\gamma(1-q)W_{m+1}$, solved by $W_m=K\big(1-(1-\gamma q)\rho^{H-m}\big)$.

*Verified: an exact sympy solve of the policy-evaluation system for $H\le4$, $N\in\{1,3,4\}$, five values of $q$ (including $0$ and $1$) and $\gamma\in\{\tfrac12,\tfrac{9}{10}\}$ equals the closed form in every case; the example table is recomputed exactly.*

**(c1) Accepting $v$ implies accepting every larger value**

**What is asked.** A yes/no verdict with a proof: if the optimal policy accepts value $v$ at some count, must it accept every $v'>v$ at the same count?

**Tool and why.** Compare the two actions through $Q^*$ (cheat sheet §1 and §2). The value $v$ enters only the immediate reward of $\text{acc}$, so the advantage of accepting is $v$ minus a quantity that does not depend on $v$. That is the threshold structure of cheat sheet §4.

**Definitions.** Let $W^*_m=\mathbb E_{v}\big[V^*(m,v)\big]$ be the optimal value at count $m$ averaged over the arriving value. Let $(m-1)^+=\max(m-1,0)$, the count after a departure from $m$ units. Let

$$A(m,v)=Q^*\big((m,v),\text{acc}\big)-Q^*\big((m,v),\text{rej}\big)$$

be the advantage of accepting; the optimal choice is decided by its sign. "The same state" means the same count $m<H$ (at $m=H$ there is no choice).

**Solution.**

Step 1, value of accepting: reward $v$, count $m+1$, then a departure with probability $q$:

$$Q^*\big((m,v),\text{acc}\big)=v+\gamma\big(q\,W^*_m+(1-q)\,W^*_{m+1}\big).$$

Step 2, value of rejecting: reward $0$, count $m$, then a departure with probability $q$ if $m\ge1$:

$$Q^*\big((m,v),\text{rej}\big)=\gamma\big(q\,W^*_{(m-1)^+}+(1-q)\,W^*_m\big).$$

Step 3, the advantage: subtracting Step 2 from Step 1,

$$A(m,v)=v-\theta_m,$$

$$\theta_m=\gamma q\big(W^*_{(m-1)^+}-W^*_m\big)+\gamma(1-q)\big(W^*_m-W^*_{m+1}\big).$$

The quantity $\theta_m$ does not depend on $v$: the value $v$ appears only in the immediate reward, and the next arriving value is drawn independently of $v$.

Step 4, the verdict. **Yes.** If an optimal policy accepts $v$ at count $m$, then $A(m,v)\ge0$; otherwise rejecting would be strictly better, contradicting optimality. For $v'>v$, by Step 3,

$$A(m,v')=A(m,v)+(v'-v)>0.$$

So accepting $v'$ is strictly better than rejecting it, and every optimal policy accepts $v'$.

**In words.** The price of using a slot is the same whatever unit takes it; only the payment depends on $v$. If a unit is worth the slot, a more valuable one is worth it even more.

**Sanity check.** In the example of part (b), value iteration gives

$$\theta_0=\tfrac{2925}{4762}\approx0.614,\qquad \theta_1=\tfrac{8271}{4762}\approx1.737.$$

At $m=1$ the optimal policy accepts $v=2$ and indeed also $v=3$ and $v=4$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Formula: $A(m,v)=v-\theta_m$, with $\theta_m$ built only from the $W^*$ values.
- Reason: $\theta_m$ does not depend on $v$, because the next arrival is independent.
- Verdict: yes; accepting gives $A(m,v)\ge0$, so $A(m,v')>0$ for $v'>v$.

*Verified: value iteration on 400 random instances ($H\le5$, $N\le8$, random $q$ and $\gamma$) gives $Q^*(\text{acc})-Q^*(\text{rej})=v-\theta_m$ with the formula above to $10^{-9}$ for every $m$ and $v$.*

**(c2) Rejecting $v$ implies rejecting every smaller value**

**What is asked.** A yes/no verdict: if the optimal policy rejects value $v$ at some count, must it reject every $v'<v$ there?

**Tool and why.** The same advantage $A(m,v)=v-\theta_m$ from part (c1) (cheat sheet §4), now read in the other direction.

**Definitions.** No new notation.

**Solution.**

Step 1, what rejecting tells us: if an optimal policy rejects $v$ at count $m<H$, then $A(m,v)\le0$; otherwise accepting would be strictly better. Equality is possible: the policy may reject when both actions are equally good.

Step 2, smaller values: for $v'<v$,

$$A(m,v')=A(m,v)-(v-v')<0.$$

So rejecting $v'$ is strictly better, and every optimal policy rejects it.

Step 3, the verdict. **Yes.** At $m=H$ every unit is rejected anyway, so the statement holds there too.

**In words.** If a unit is not worth a slot, a cheaper unit is worth even less, while the cost of the slot is the same.

**Sanity check.** In the example, at $m=1$ the optimal policy rejects $v=1$, because $1<\theta_1$, and it rejects $v=0$ as well.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Fact: rejecting $v$ gives $A(m,v)\le0$.
- Step: for $v'<v$, $A(m,v')$ is $A(m,v)$ minus $v-v'$, so it is negative.
- Verdict: yes, by the same monotonicity in $v$.

*Verified: the same 400 value-iteration instances as in part (c1); in each one the set of rejected values at every count is closed downward.*

**(d) A threshold for every occupancy**

**What is asked.** A proof that there are numbers $\theta_0,\dots,\theta_H$ such that the optimal policy accepts at count $k$ exactly when $v\ge\theta_k$.

**Tool and why.** Threshold structure (cheat sheet §4), assembled from part (c): the advantage is $v$ minus a constant, so its sign changes once as $v$ grows. A policy that is greedy with respect to $Q^*$ is optimal (cheat sheet §2 and §3).

**Definitions.** For $k<H$, $\theta_k$ is the constant of part (c1). For $k=H$ we set $\theta_H=+\infty$, which encodes "never accept". When both actions are equally good we accept; this tie rule is what makes the inequality $v\ge\theta_k$ non-strict.

**Solution.**

Step 1, counts below capacity: for $k<H$ the greedy policy accepts if and only if $A(k,v)\ge0$, and by part (c1) this is $v-\theta_k\ge0$, that is $v\ge\theta_k$.

Step 2, a full queue: at $k=H$ nothing can be accepted, and $v\ge\theta_H$ is false for every finite $v$, so the rule gives the right answer.

Step 3, optimality: the policy "accept if and only if $v\ge\theta_k$" picks an action that maximizes $Q^*$ in every state, so it is optimal.

Step 4, the same result from (c1) and (c2) alone: at a fixed count the set of accepted values is closed upward by (c1) and the set of rejected values is closed downward by (c2). A set of integers in $\{0,\dots,N\}$ that is closed upward is $\{v\ge\theta_k\}$, with $\theta_k$ its smallest element (or $+\infty$ if it is empty).

Step 5, the example of part (b), $H=2$, $N=4$, $q=\tfrac12$, $\gamma=\tfrac{9}{10}$:

| $k$ | $\theta_k$ | accepted values |
|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $\tfrac{2925}{4762}\approx0.614$ | $v\ge1$ |
| $1$ | $\tfrac{8271}{4762}\approx1.737$ | $v\ge2$ |
| $2$ | $+\infty$ | none |

The optimal value from the start is $W^*_1=\tfrac{32670}{2381}$, about $13.72$, against $11$ for always-accept: rejecting cheap units pays.

**In words.** The fuller the queue, the more a slot is worth, so the bar for using it rises; at every occupancy the decision is just "is this unit worth more than the slot".

**Sanity check.** In all 400 random instances $\theta_k\ge0$ and $W^*_m$ does not increase with $m$: an extra unit in the queue never helps. In the example the thresholds rise with $k$, as expected.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Advantage: $A(k,v)=v-\theta_k$ with $\theta_k$ independent of $v$, by part (c).
- Rule: accept if and only if $v\ge\theta_k$, since the greedy action w.r.t. $Q^*$ is optimal.
- Full queue: $\theta_H=+\infty$.

*Verified: exact policy evaluation (sympy) of the greedy threshold policy gives $W^*=\big(\tfrac{35920}{2381},\tfrac{32670}{2381},\tfrac{26730}{2381}\big)$, equal to value iteration to $10^{-12}$, and the thresholds in the table.*

**Common mistakes.**

- Writing the departure as "always to $m'-1$ with probability $q$": from an empty queue nothing can leave, and the transition row then sums to $1-q$.
- Letting the threshold depend on $v$, or comparing $v$ with $W^*_m$ instead of with the difference $\theta_m$ of continuation values.
- Claiming that always-accept is optimal; in the example it earns $11$ against the optimal $13.72$.

**💡 Trick.** Write the accept-vs-reject advantage as $A(m,v)=v-\theta_m$ where $\theta_m$ depends on the occupancy $m$ but not on $v$; monotonicity in $v$ then hands you both parts (c) and the threshold policy in (d) at once.

**⚠️ Watch out.** Don't claim "always accept" is optimal — it wastes scarce capacity on low-value units — and remember the full queue $k=H$ never accepts, i.e. $\theta_H=+\infty$.

## Q2 (30 pts) — Bernoulli MAB with the (pessimistic) LCB algorithm
**Topics:** multi-armed bandits, Hoeffding confidence intervals, UCB/LCB, exploration failure, regret | **Pillar:** Bandits | **Difficulty:** 4
**Maps to:** lecture_10
**Statement (English translation):**
Consider a MAB with $k$ arms. The reward of arm $a_i$ is Bernoulli with parameter $q_i$ (equals $1$ w.p. $q_i$, else $0$). Define $i^*=\arg\max_i q_i$ and $q^*=\max_i q_i$; all $q_i$ are unknown. Define the **LCB algorithm**: for $t\le k$ play $a_t=t$ (pull each arm once); for $t>k$ play $a_t=\arg\max_i \mathrm{LCB}^t_i$ and receive reward $r_t$. Let $n^t_i$ be the number of pulls of arm $i$ up to time $t$, and $\hat\mu^t_i=\frac{1}{n^t_i}\sum_{t:a_t=i} r_t$ its empirical mean.
- **(a)** Given a sample of $n^t_i$ rewards from arm $a_i$ of which $s^t_i$ are $1$, define $\mathrm{UCB}(n^t_i,s^t_i)$ and $\mathrm{LCB}(n^t_i,s^t_i)$ as functions of $\delta$ so that with probability $\ge 1-\delta$, $q_i\in[\mathrm{LCB},\mathrm{UCB}]$.
- From here on assume the UCB/LCB bounds always hold.
- **(b)** Compute a bound $M^*$ such that if arm $i^*$ has been pulled at least $M^*$ times then $a_t=i^*$ in the LCB algorithm.
- **(c)** Suppose $k=2$ with $q_1=\tfrac34,\ q_2=\tfrac13$, and that we observed $r_1=0$ (from pulling arm 1 at $t=1$) and $r_2=1$ (from arm 2 at $t=2$). What is the action $a_3$?
- **(d)** Is there a bound $M$ (a function of $q_1,\dots,q_k$ and $T$, intended $M\ll T$) such that every suboptimal arm ($a_i\ne i^*$) is played at most $M$ times? (yes/no, explain.)

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) Hoeffding confidence bounds**

**What is asked.** Formulas for $\mathrm{UCB}$ and $\mathrm{LCB}$ in terms of $n^t_i$, $s^t_i$ and $\delta$ such that $q_i$ lies between them with probability at least $1-\delta$. The answer is two formulas.

**Tool and why.** Hoeffding's inequality (cheat sheet §10): the rewards are independent and lie in $[0,1]$, so the empirical mean concentrates around $q_i$ with an explicit tail. Setting the tail equal to $\delta$ gives the radius.

**Definitions.** Write $n=n^t_i$ and $\hat\mu=s^t_i/n^t_i$, the empirical mean (the fraction of ones). Let $r(n)$ be the radius of the interval, found in Step 2.

**Solution.**

Step 1, Hoeffding for $n$ independent rewards in $[0,1]$ with mean $q_i$:

$$\Pr\big(|\hat\mu-q_i|\ge\varepsilon\big)\le2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}.$$

Step 2, choose $\varepsilon$ so that the right side equals $\delta$:

$$\begin{aligned}
2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}&=\delta\\
2n\varepsilon^2&=\ln(2/\delta)\\
\varepsilon&=\sqrt{\frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{2n}}.
\end{aligned}$$

We call this value $r(n)$.

Step 3, the bounds:

$$\mathrm{UCB}=\frac{s^t_i}{n^t_i}+\sqrt{\frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{2n^t_i}}$$

and

$$\mathrm{LCB}=\frac{s^t_i}{n^t_i}-\sqrt{\frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{2n^t_i}}$$

Step 4, the guarantee: the event $|\hat\mu-q_i|<r(n)$ has probability at least $1-\delta$ by Step 1, and on it

$$\mathrm{LCB}\le q_i\le\mathrm{UCB}.$$

**In words.** The interval is the empirical mean plus or minus a radius that shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$; the $2$ inside the logarithm pays for the two tails.

**Sanity check.** With $\delta=0.1$: $r(1)\approx1.224$, wider than $[0,1]$ itself, so one sample says almost nothing; $r(100)\approx0.122$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Hoeffding: the probability of $|\hat\mu-q_i|\ge\varepsilon$ is at most $2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}$.
- Radius: set the bound to $\delta$, giving $r(n)=\sqrt{\ln(2/\delta)/(2n)}$.
- Bounds: $\hat\mu\pm r(n)$.

*Verified: 200000 simulated binomial samples for each $n\in\{1,5,20,100\}$ and $q_i\in\{0.1,0.5,0.75\}$ give coverage at least $0.9$ with $\delta=0.1$.*

**(b) Enough pulls of the best arm make LCB choose it**

**What is asked.** A number $M^*$ such that $n^t_{i^*}$ above it forces $a_t=i^*$. The answer is a formula.

**Tool and why.** Interval arithmetic on the confidence bounds (cheat sheet §10), the same argument as in the UCB analysis: bound the best arm's LCB from below and every other arm's LCB from above, and compare with the gap.

**Definitions.** Let

$$\Delta=q^*-\max_{j\ne i^*}q_j$$

be the gap between the best and the second-best arm; we assume $\Delta>0$ (a unique best arm). Write $n=n^t_{i^*}$ and use $r(n)$ from part (a).

**Solution.**

Step 1, every other arm: its LCB lies below its mean, by the assumption that the bounds hold, so

$$\mathrm{LCB}_j\le q_j\le q^*-\Delta.$$

Step 2, the best arm: $q^*\le\mathrm{UCB}_{i^*}$ means $\hat\mu_{i^*}\ge q^*-r(n)$, and then

$$\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{LCB}_{i^*}&=\hat\mu_{i^*}-r(n)\\
&\ge q^*-2r(n).
\end{aligned}$$

Step 3, a sufficient condition: if $2r(n)<\Delta$, then $q^*-2r(n)>q^*-\Delta$, so by Steps 1 and 2 the LCB of $i^*$ is strictly larger than every other LCB and $a_t=i^*$.

Step 4, solve for $n$:

$$\begin{aligned}
2\sqrt{\frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{2n}}<\Delta&\iff\frac{4\ln(2/\delta)}{2n}<\Delta^2\\
&\iff n>\frac{2\ln(2/\delta)}{\Delta^2}.
\end{aligned}$$

Step 5, the answer:

$$\boxed{M^*=\dfrac{2\ln(2/\delta)}{\Delta^2}}$$

Strictly more than $M^*$ pulls guarantee $a_t=i^*$. At exactly $M^*$ pulls the inequalities may hold with equality and produce a tie, so as an integer one can take $\lfloor M^*\rfloor+1$.

**In words.** The best arm's LCB can sit up to two radii below $q^*$; once two radii are smaller than the gap, no other arm can beat it.

**Sanity check.** With $q=(\tfrac34,\tfrac13)$ and $\delta=0.1$: $\Delta=\tfrac{5}{12}$ and $M^*\approx34.5$, so $35$ pulls of arm 1 suffice. As $\Delta\to0$, $M^*\to\infty$, as it should.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Others: $\mathrm{LCB}_j\le q_j$, and $q_j\le q^*-\Delta$.
- Best arm: $\mathrm{LCB}_{i^*}\ge q^*-2r(n)$.
- Condition: $2r(n)<\Delta$ gives $n>2\ln(2/\delta)/\Delta^2$, so $M^*=2\ln(2/\delta)/\Delta^2$.

*Verified: 20000 random instances ($2$ to $5$ arms, random $\delta$) with every LCB drawn consistently with the bounds: whenever $n_{i^*}>M^*$, the best arm had the strictly largest LCB.*

**(c) The third action**

**What is asked.** Which arm the LCB algorithm plays at $t=3$, after $r_1=0$ from arm 1 and $r_2=1$ from arm 2. The answer is an arm.

**Tool and why.** Direct evaluation of the LCB formula from part (a) (cheat sheet §10); $t=3$ is larger than $k=2$, so the algorithm takes the argmax of the LCBs.

**Definitions.** No new notation; $r(1)$ is the radius after one pull.

**Solution.**

Step 1, counts: $n_1=1$, $n_2=1$, $s_1=0$ and $s_2=1$.

Step 2, both LCBs have the same radius $r(1)$:

$$\mathrm{LCB}_1=0-r(1),\qquad \mathrm{LCB}_2=1-r(1).$$

Step 3, compare: for every $\delta$,

$$\mathrm{LCB}_2-\mathrm{LCB}_1=1>0,$$

so

$$\boxed{a_3=2}$$

Step 4, numbers for $\delta=0.1$: $r(1)=\sqrt{\ln 20/2}$, about $1.224$, so $\mathrm{LCB}_1\approx-1.224$ and $\mathrm{LCB}_2\approx-0.224$.

**In words.** With one sample each, the only difference is the observed reward, and the arm that happened to pay wins, even though arm 2 is the worse arm, $q_2<q_1$.

**Sanity check.** The assumption that the bounds hold is not violated: the interval of arm 1 is about $[-1.224,1.224]$, which contains $q_1=\tfrac34$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- LCBs: $-r(1)$ for arm 1 and $1-r(1)$ for arm 2.
- Answer: $a_3=2$, independently of $\delta$.

*Verified: $r(1)$ and both LCBs recomputed for $\delta=0.1$.*

**(d) Can a suboptimal arm be played almost always?**

**What is asked.** Whether every suboptimal arm is played at most $M\ll T$ times. The answer is yes or no with an argument.

**Tool and why.** A counterexample built on part (c) (cheat sheet §10). The LCB of an arm that is not pulled never changes, while the LCB of a pulled arm can only rise toward its mean; we show this by induction on $t$.

**Definitions.** Let $T$ be the horizon. We stay in the scenario of part (c): $q=(\tfrac34,\tfrac13)$, $r_1=0$ and $r_2=1$.

**Solution.**

**Claim.** In this scenario $a_t=2$ for every $t$ from $2$ to $T$, whatever arm 2 pays later; arm 2 is played $T-1$ times.

The proof is by induction on $t\ge3$.

**Base.** $a_3=2$ by part (c).

**Hypothesis.** Arm 2 was played at every time from $3$ to $t-1$, so arm 1 still has $n_1=1$ and $s_1=0$.

**Step.** Arm 1 was not pulled, so its LCB is still $-r(1)$. Arm 2 has $n_2\ge2$ pulls, and its empirical mean is at least $0$. The radius decreases in $n$, so $r(n_2)<r(1)$, and

$$\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{LCB}_2&=\hat\mu_2-r(n_2)\\
&\ge-r(n_2)\\
&>-r(1)\\
&=\mathrm{LCB}_1.
\end{aligned}$$

So $a_t=2$. The hypothesis was used to know that $\mathrm{LCB}_1$ is still $-r(1)$.

Step 1, the scenario is not rare: the first two rewards are independent, so its probability is

$$\Pr(r_1=0,\ r_2=1)=\tfrac14\cdot\tfrac13=\tfrac{1}{12}.$$

Step 2, consequence: with probability at least $\tfrac{1}{12}$ the suboptimal arm is played $T-1$ times, and its expected number of pulls is at least $\tfrac{T-1}{12}$, linear in $T$. No bound $M\ll T$ can hold.

Step 3, why part (b) does not help: (b) needs $n_{i^*}>M^*$, but here arm 1 stays at one pull forever.

$$\boxed{\text{No}}$$

**In words.** LCB is pessimistic: it has no exploration bonus, so an arm that started badly is never retried, and the algorithm locks onto whatever arm looked good early.

**Sanity check.** Simulating $4000$ runs with $T=2000$: arm 2 was played $T-1$ times in a fraction $0.0833$ of the runs (against $\tfrac{1}{12}\approx0.0833$), in all $333$ runs that started with $r_1=0$ and $r_2=1$, and on average about $213$ times per run, above $\tfrac{1999}{12}\approx167$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Verdict: no.
- Counterexample: the scenario of (c), probability $\tfrac{1}{12}$.
- Induction: $\mathrm{LCB}_1=-r(1)$ is frozen, while $\mathrm{LCB}_2>-r(1)$ once $n_2\ge2$.
- Conclusion: arm 2 is played $T-1$ times, so its expected count is linear in $T$.

*Verified: the simulation above of the LCB algorithm with $\delta=0.1$.*

**Common mistakes.**

- Using the one-sided radius $\sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/(2n)}$ for a two-sided interval; the two tails need $\ln(2/\delta)$.
- In (b), bounding $\mathrm{LCB}_{i^*}$ by $q^*-r(n)$: the radius enters twice, once from the empirical mean and once from the LCB itself.
- In (d), answering "yes" by quoting (b): (b) is conditional on $n_{i^*}>M^*$, which the LCB algorithm may never reach.

**💡 Trick.** Bound the LCB gap with the Hoeffding radius: forcing $2r(n_{i^*})<\Delta$ yields $M^*=2\ln(2/\delta)/\Delta^2$, and the same argmax-LCB rule that gives (b) is exactly what breaks in (d).

**⚠️ Watch out.** Argmax-LCB is purely exploitative — it never explores — so one unlucky $r=0$ from the best arm keeps its $\mathrm{LCB}$ low forever; don't assume the premise "$i^*$ reaches $M^*$ pulls" is ever met.

## Q3 (20 pts) — Value-function fitting (SGD / Monte-Carlo) and REINFORCE with a Laplace policy
**Topics:** function approximation, SGD, Monte-Carlo return estimation, policy gradient, REINFORCE, score function | **Pillar:** Approximation | **Difficulty:** 4
**Maps to:** lecture_08, lecture_09
**Statement (English translation):**
*Setup 1.* Every state $s$ has a feature vector $\phi(s)\in\mathbb R^d$. A value function is parameterized by $\theta_1,\theta_2\in\mathbb R^d$ as 

$$V(s;\theta_1,\theta_2)=\tfrac12(\phi(s)\cdot\theta_2)^2+\phi(s)\cdot\theta_1$$

. For a policy $\pi$ we minimize the squared error 

$$J^\pi(\theta_1,\theta_2)=\sum_s\mu(s)\big(V^\pi(s)-V(s;\theta_1,\theta_2)\big)^2$$

, where $\mu(s)$ is $\pi$'s stationary distribution (assume every state is visited many times). Minimize $J^\pi$ by gradient updates.
- **(a)** Write the gradient-descent update for $\theta_1^{t+1},\theta_2^{t+1}$ (as a function of $\phi(s),\mu(s),V^\pi(s)$ and learning rate $\alpha$).
- **(b)** Propose how to obtain an unbiased estimate of $V^\pi(s)$ for a state $s\sim\mu$ using Monte-Carlo.
- **(c)** Write the Monte-Carlo SGD update for minimizing $J^\pi$.

*Setup 2.* The Laplace distribution with parameter $\mu$ has density $\tfrac12\exp(-|x-\mu|)$ for all $x$. Actions $a\ge0$ in state $s$ are drawn from this Laplace distribution with parameter $\mu=\theta\cdot\phi(s)$, where policy $\pi$ is characterized by $\theta\in\mathbb R^d$.
- **(d)** What is the probability (density) that action $a\ge0$ is drawn in state $s$?
- **(e)** Compute $\nabla_\theta\log\pi(a\mid s;\theta)$.
- **(f)** Write the REINFORCE update for the Laplace distribution.

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) The gradient-descent update**

**What is asked.** Explicit updates for $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ that take one gradient step on $J^\pi$. The answer is two formulas.

**Tool and why.** Full gradient descent on a squared error, with the chain rule (cheat sheet §8). $J^\pi$ is a weighted sum of squares of a smooth function of the parameters, so we differentiate each term.

**Definitions.** Let $e_t(s)=V^\pi(s)-V(s;\theta_1^t,\theta_2^t)$ be the error at state $s$ with the current parameters; it only shortens the formulas. The objective has no factor $\tfrac12$, so its gradient carries a factor $2$, which we absorb into $\alpha$.

**Solution.**

Step 1, gradient of the model in $\theta_1$: the first term does not involve $\theta_1$ and the second is linear in it, so

$$\nabla_{\theta_1}V(s;\theta_1,\theta_2)=\phi(s).$$

Step 2, gradient of the model in $\theta_2$: write $u=\phi(s)\cdot\theta_2$; the derivative of $\tfrac12u^2$ is $u$, and the gradient of $u$ is $\phi(s)$, so

$$\nabla_{\theta_2}V(s;\theta_1,\theta_2)=(\phi(s)\cdot\theta_2)\,\phi(s).$$

Step 3, gradient of the objective: $\mu(s)$ and $V^\pi(s)$ do not depend on the parameters, and the inner derivative of $V^\pi-V$ brings a minus sign:

$$\nabla_{\theta_i}J^\pi=-2\sum_s\mu(s)\,e_t(s)\,\nabla_{\theta_i}V(s;\theta_1,\theta_2),\qquad i=1,2.$$

Step 4, the step $-\alpha\nabla J^\pi$, with the $2$ absorbed into $\alpha$; both updates use the current parameters:

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\theta_1^{t+1}&=\theta_1^{t}+\alpha\sum_s\mu(s)\,e_t(s)\,\phi(s)\\
\theta_2^{t+1}&=\theta_2^{t}+\alpha\sum_s\mu(s)\,e_t(s)\,(\phi(s)\cdot\theta_2^{t})\,\phi(s)
\end{aligned}}$$

**In words.** Each state pulls the parameters along its feature vector, in proportion to its weight and its error; for $\theta_2$ the pull is also scaled by the current slope $\phi(s)\cdot\theta_2$ of the quadratic part.

**Sanity check.** If the model is exact on every state with positive weight, every $e_t(s)$ is $0$ and nothing moves. If $\theta_2^t=0$, then $\theta_2$ never moves: the gradient in $\theta_2$ vanishes there, so $\theta_2$ must be initialised away from $0$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Chain rule: $\nabla_{\theta_1}V=\phi(s)$ and $\nabla_{\theta_2}V=(\phi(s)\cdot\theta_2)\phi(s)$.
- Gradient: $-2\sum_s\mu(s)e_t(s)\nabla V$.
- Updates: the two lines in the box, with the $2$ absorbed into $\alpha$.

*Verified: on a random instance with $d=3$ and five states the analytic gradients match central finite differences to $9\cdot10^{-10}$.*

**(b) An unbiased Monte-Carlo estimate of the state value**

**What is asked.** A quantity computed from simulation whose expectation is $V^\pi(s)$. The answer is a procedure and a formula with a justification.

**Tool and why.** Monte-Carlo policy evaluation (cheat sheet §7): $V^\pi(s)$ is by definition the expected return from $s$, so one observed return from $s$ is an unbiased sample of it, and no model of the transitions is needed.

**Definitions.** Let $t_s$ be a time at which the trajectory of $\pi$ is in $s$, and let

$$\hat G(s)=\sum_{k\ge0}\gamma^k r_{t_s+k}$$

be the discounted return observed from that visit on.

**Solution.**

Step 1, sampling $s\sim\mu$: run $\pi$; since $\mu$ is its stationary distribution, the visited states are (after mixing) distributed according to $\mu$.

Step 2, the estimate: from the visit to $s$, keep following $\pi$ and add up the discounted rewards; this is $\hat G(s)$.

Step 3, unbiasedness: given that we are in $s$, what happens next depends only on $s$ and $\pi$ (Markov property), and the expected discounted sum of what happens next is $V^\pi(s)$ by definition. So $\mathbb E[\hat G(s)\mid s]=V^\pi(s)$.

Step 4, a practical note: if the task is episodic, sum until the episode ends. In a continuing task we stop after a long horizon $L$; the omitted tail is at most $\gamma^L R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)$ in absolute value, where $R_{\max}$ bounds the rewards, so the bias can be made as small as we like.

**In words.** The value of a state is the average return from it, so one return is a fair sample of that average.

**Sanity check.** If the policy and the transitions are deterministic, $\hat G(s)$ equals $V^\pi(s)$ exactly. In a small simulated chain the mean of $100000$ returns was $1.533$, against the exact $1.527$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Estimator: the discounted return $\hat G(s)$ from a visit to $s$ under $\pi$.
- Reason: $\mathbb E[\hat G(s)\mid s]=V^\pi(s)$ by the definition of $V^\pi$ and the Markov property.

*Verified: $100000$ simulated episodes of a four-state chain with noisy rewards ($\gamma=0.9$) give a mean return of $1.533$ at a state whose exact value is $1.527$.*

**(c) The Monte-Carlo SGD update**

**What is asked.** A stochastic update that uses one sampled state and its return instead of the sum over all states and the unknown $V^\pi$. The answer is two formulas.

**Tool and why.** Stochastic gradient descent (cheat sheet §8): replace the exact gradient by an unbiased sample of it. Running $\pi$ supplies states with weights $\mu(s)$, and part (b) supplies an unbiased replacement for $V^\pi(s)$.

**Definitions.** Let $s_t$ be the state sampled at step $t$ and

$$\hat e_t=\hat G(s_t)-V(s_t;\theta_1^t,\theta_2^t)$$

the sampled error; it plays the role of $e_t(s)$ from part (a).

**Solution.**

Step 1, sample a visited state $s_t\sim\mu$ by running $\pi$.

Step 2, compute its Monte-Carlo target $\hat G(s_t)$ as in part (b).

Step 3, the update: keep only the term of $s_t$ from part (a), without the weight $\mu(s_t)$ (the sampling already supplies it), and with $\hat G(s_t)$ in place of $V^\pi(s_t)$:

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\theta_1^{t+1}&=\theta_1^{t}+\alpha\,\hat e_t\,\phi(s_t)\\
\theta_2^{t+1}&=\theta_2^{t}+\alpha\,\hat e_t\,(\phi(s_t)\cdot\theta_2^{t})\,\phi(s_t)
\end{aligned}}$$

Step 4, why it is unbiased: given $s_t=s$, the factors $\phi(s)$ and $(\phi(s)\cdot\theta_2^t)\phi(s)$ are fixed, and by part (b) the target has mean $V^\pi(s)$. Averaging over $s\sim\mu$:

$$\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E\big[\hat e_t\,\phi(s_t)\big]&=\sum_s\mu(s)\,\mathbb E\big[\hat G(s)-V(s;\theta^t)\mid s\big]\,\phi(s)\\
&=\sum_s\mu(s)\,e_t(s)\,\phi(s)\\
&=-\tfrac12\nabla_{\theta_1}J^\pi.
\end{aligned}$$

The same computation with $(\phi(s)\cdot\theta_2^t)\phi(s)$ in place of $\phi(s)$ gives $-\tfrac12\nabla_{\theta_2}J^\pi$.

**In words.** The two unknowns of the exact gradient, the weights $\mu$ and the values $V^\pi$, are both replaced by samples with the right expectation.

**Sanity check.** If $\hat G(s_t)=V(s_t;\theta^t)$ nothing moves. If the return is larger, the linear part $\phi(s_t)\cdot\theta_1$ grows by $\alpha\hat e_t\lVert\phi(s_t)\rVert^2>0$, so the prediction moves toward the return.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Sampling: $s_t\sim\mu$ by running $\pi$, target $\hat G(s_t)$.
- Update: the two lines in the box, with $\hat e_t=\hat G(s_t)-V(s_t;\theta^t)$.
- Unbiased: $\mathbb E[\hat G(s)\mid s]=V^\pi(s)$, so the expected step is the full-gradient step.

*Verified: $60000$ sampled states with simulated returns give a mean $\theta_1$-direction of $(-0.551,-1.029)$, against the exact $(-0.554,-1.034)$.*

**(d) The density of an action**

**What is asked.** The density of the policy at an action $a\ge0$ in state $s$. The answer is a formula.

**Tool and why.** A parameterised policy (cheat sheet §9): substitute the location $\theta\cdot\phi(s)$ into the given Laplace density.

**Definitions.** The action is continuous, so a single value has probability $0$; "probability" here means the density. We read the statement as: the action is drawn from the Laplace distribution with location $\theta\cdot\phi(s)$, and we evaluate its density at a value $a\ge0$.

**Solution.**

Step 1, substitute the location $\theta\cdot\phi(s)$ for the parameter of the Laplace density:

$$\boxed{\pi(a\mid s;\theta)=\tfrac12\exp\!\big(-|a-\theta\cdot\phi(s)|\big)}$$

Step 2, a remark on the support: if the policy were the Laplace distribution truncated to $a\ge0$, the density would be divided by the probability $Z$ that a Laplace variable with location $\theta\cdot\phi(s)$ is non-negative, and $\nabla_\theta\log Z$ would join the score in part (e). The statement gives the untruncated density, so we keep it.

**In words.** The density peaks at $\theta\cdot\phi(s)$ and decays exponentially, with scale $1$, on both sides.

**Sanity check.** It integrates to $1$: each side contributes $\tfrac12\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\,dx=\tfrac12$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Density: $\tfrac12\exp(-|a-\theta\cdot\phi(s)|)$, the Laplace density with location $\theta\cdot\phi(s)$.

*Verified: numerical integration for four locations gives total mass $1$.*

**(e) The score function**

**What is asked.** The gradient of the log-density with respect to $\theta$. The answer is a formula.

**Tool and why.** The log-derivative used by policy gradient (cheat sheet §9): the logarithm removes the exponential, and the derivative of $|u|$ is $\operatorname{sign}(u)$ wherever $u\ne0$.

**Definitions.** Let $u=a-\theta\cdot\phi(s)$, the distance of the action from the location, with a sign.

**Solution.**

Step 1, take the logarithm:

$$\log\pi(a\mid s;\theta)=\log\tfrac12-|u|.$$

Step 2, the inner gradient: $\nabla_\theta u=-\phi(s)$, since $a$ does not depend on $\theta$.

Step 3, the chain rule: the gradient of $-|u|$ is $-\operatorname{sign}(u)\nabla_\theta u$, and the two minus signs cancel:

$$\boxed{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(a\mid s;\theta)=\operatorname{sign}\!\big(a-\theta\cdot\phi(s)\big)\,\phi(s)}$$

So it is $+\phi(s)$ when $a>\theta\cdot\phi(s)$ and $-\phi(s)$ when $a<\theta\cdot\phi(s)$.

Step 4, the point $u=0$: there $|u|$ has no derivative, but this happens with probability $0$, so it does not matter for the update.

**In words.** The score points in the direction that moves the location toward the sampled action; its size does not depend on how far the action is.

**Sanity check.** If $a>\theta\cdot\phi(s)$, a small step along $+\phi(s)$ raises $\theta\cdot\phi(s)$ toward $a$ and so raises the density at $a$, as a score should.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Logarithm: $\log\tfrac12-|a-\theta\cdot\phi(s)|$.
- Score: $\operatorname{sign}(a-\theta\cdot\phi(s))\,\phi(s)$, by the chain rule.

*Verified: central finite differences on 1000 random $(\theta,\phi,a)$ with $d=4$ match the formula to $10^{-5}$.*

**(f) The REINFORCE update**

**What is asked.** The REINFORCE update for this Laplace policy. The answer is a formula.

**Tool and why.** REINFORCE (cheat sheet §9): the policy gradient equals the expected return times the score, so each visited step moves $\theta$ by $\alpha$ times the return times the score from part (e).

**Definitions.** Let $(s_0,a_0,r_0,s_1,\dots)$ be an episode generated by $\pi$, and $G_t$ the return from time $t$.

**Solution.**

Step 1, the general rule:

$$\theta^{t+1}=\theta^{t}+\alpha\,G_t\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi(a_t\mid s_t;\theta).$$

Step 2, plug in the score from part (e):

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}\theta^{t+1}&=\theta^{t}+\alpha\,G_t\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi(a_t\mid s_t;\theta)\\ &=\theta^{t}+\alpha\,G_t\,\operatorname{sign}\!\big(a_t-\theta\cdot\phi(s_t)\big)\phi(s_t)\end{aligned}}$$

where $G_t=\sum_{k\ge0}\gamma^k r_{t+k}$ is the return from time $t$.

Step 3, the algorithm: run an episode, compute the returns from the end backward, and apply the update at every step. Optionally subtract a baseline $b(s_t)$ from $G_t$ to reduce the variance, or multiply by $\gamma^t$ for the discounted objective from the start state.

**In words.** Each action pulls the location toward itself, with a strength equal to the return that followed it; with a baseline, actions that did better than average pull and worse ones push.

**Sanity check.** One-step problem with $\phi=1$ and reward $-(a-2)^2$: the objective is $-\big((\theta-2)^2+2\big)$, because a Laplace variable has variance $2$, so the true gradient is $-2(\theta-2)$. The average of reward times score over four million samples gives $2.996$, $2.003$ and $-2.002$ at $\theta=0.5$, $1$ and $3$, against $3$, $2$ and $-2$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Rule: $\theta$ plus $\alpha G_t$ times the score.
- Score: $\operatorname{sign}(a_t-\theta\cdot\phi(s_t))\,\phi(s_t)$ from part (e).
- Return: $G_t$, optionally minus a baseline.

*Verified: the Monte-Carlo gradient check above, with four million Laplace samples per value of $\theta$.*

**Common mistakes.**

- Dropping the factor $(\phi(s)\cdot\theta_2)$ in the $\theta_2$ gradient, that is, treating the model as linear in $\theta_2$.
- Getting the sign of the score wrong: differentiating $-|a-\theta\cdot\phi|$ gives a plus sign, because the inner derivative is $-\phi$.
- Using $V^\pi(s)$ (unknown) or $V(s;\theta)$ (which makes the error zero) as the SGD target instead of $\hat G(s)$.

**💡 Trick.** Everything reduces to two chain-rule facts: $\nabla_{\theta_2}V=(\phi\cdot\theta_2)\phi$ for the quadratic model, and $\nabla_\theta\log\pi=\operatorname{sign}(a-\theta\cdot\phi)\,\phi$ for the Laplace score; plug an unbiased MC return $\hat G$ in for the unknown $V^\pi$ / $G_t$.

**⚠️ Watch out.** Mind the sign when differentiating $|a-\theta\cdot\phi(s)|$ — the two minus signs cancel to give $+\operatorname{sign}(a-\theta\cdot\phi)\phi$ — and use the sampled return, never the true $V^\pi(s)$, as the SGD target.

## Q4 (20 pts) — Log-transition Bellman operator: γ-contraction and its reward
**Topics:** Bellman operator, γ-contraction, sup-norm, fixed point, Bellman optimality equation | **Pillar:** Planning | **Difficulty:** 3
**Maps to:** lecture_04, lecture_03
**Statement (English translation):**
Given an MDP $M=(S,A,p,s_0,R)$, define for $U\in\mathbb R^{|S|}$ the operator
$$(TU)(s)=\max_a \mathbb E_{s'\sim p(\cdot\mid s,a)}\big[\ln p(s'\mid s,a)+\gamma U(s')\big].$$
- **(a)** State the definition of an operator $H$ being $\gamma$-contracting with respect to the norm $\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty$.
- **(b)** Prove that $T$ is $\gamma$-contracting with respect to $\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty$.
- **(c)** Define a reward function $r(s,a)$ so that $TV^{\pi^*}=V^{\pi^*}$, where $\pi^*$ is the optimal policy and $V^{\pi^*}$ is its discounted return with parameter $\gamma$. (Give $r(s,a)$ and explain.)

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) Definition of a γ-contraction**

**What is asked.** The definition of "$H$ is a $\gamma$-contraction in $\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty$". The answer is a definition.

**Tool and why.** Operators, contraction and fixed points (cheat sheet §6): this definition is the hypothesis of Banach's fixed-point theorem, which part (c) relies on.

**Definitions.** For $U\in\mathbb R^{|S|}$, $\lVert U\rVert_\infty=\max_s|U(s)|$, the largest entry in absolute value.

**Solution.**

Step 1, the definition: an operator $H$ from $\mathbb R^{|S|}$ to itself is a $\gamma$-contraction in $\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty$, with $\gamma\in[0,1)$, if for all $U,V$

$$\lVert HU-HV\rVert_\infty\le\gamma\lVert U-V\rVert_\infty$$

Step 2, what it gives (Banach): such an $H$ has a unique fixed point, and repeated application of $H$ from any start converges to it at rate $\gamma^k$.

**In words.** Applying $H$ brings any two vectors closer by at least a factor $\gamma$, in the worst entry.

**Sanity check.** The identity operator satisfies the inequality only with factor $1$, so it is not a contraction, and indeed every vector is a fixed point of it, not a unique one.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Definition: for all $U,V$, $\lVert HU-HV\rVert_\infty\le\gamma\lVert U-V\rVert_\infty$, with $\gamma\in[0,1)$.

*Verified: this inequality is exactly the ratio test run in part (b) on 2000 random MDPs.*

**(b) Proof that T is a γ-contraction**

**What is asked.** A proof of the inequality of part (a) for the given $T$.

**Tool and why.** The standard contraction proof for a Bellman operator (cheat sheet §6). The term $\ln p(s'\mid s,a)$ is the same in $TU$ and in $TV$, so it cancels, and what remains is $\gamma$ times an expectation of $U-V$.

**Definitions.** Fix a state $s$. For each action $a$ let

$$f_a(U)=\mathbb E_{s'\sim p(\cdot\mid s,a)}\big[\ln p(s'\mid s,a)+\gamma U(s')\big],$$

so that $(TU)(s)=\max_a f_a(U)$. The expectation runs only over $s'$ with $p(s'\mid s,a)>0$, so the logarithm is finite.

**Solution.**

Step 1, **Claim.** For any two functions $f,g$ on a finite set of actions,

$$\big|\max_a f(a)-\max_a g(a)\big|\le\max_a|f(a)-g(a)|.$$

Let $a_1$ be an action at which $f$ is largest. Since $\max_a g(a)$ is at least $g(a_1)$,

$$\begin{aligned}
\max_a f(a)-\max_a g(a)&\le f(a_1)-g(a_1)\\
&\le\max_a|f(a)-g(a)|.
\end{aligned}$$

Exchanging $f$ and $g$ bounds the difference in the other direction, which proves the claim.

Step 2, the difference for one action: by linearity of expectation the logarithm terms cancel,

$$f_a(U)-f_a(V)=\gamma\,\mathbb E_{s'}\big[U(s')-V(s')\big].$$

Step 3, bound it:

$$\begin{aligned}
|f_a(U)-f_a(V)|&\le\gamma\,\mathbb E_{s'}\big|U(s')-V(s')\big|\\
&\le\gamma\lVert U-V\rVert_\infty.
\end{aligned}$$

The first row moves the absolute value inside the expectation; the second bounds every entry by the largest one.

Step 4, combine: by the claim and Step 3, $|(TU)(s)-(TV)(s)|\le\gamma\lVert U-V\rVert_\infty$ for every $s$. Taking the maximum over $s$:

$$\boxed{\lVert TU-TV\rVert_\infty\le\gamma\lVert U-V\rVert_\infty}$$

**In words.** The log term is a fixed bonus that does not depend on $U$, so it shifts both sides equally; only the discounted look-ahead depends on $U$, and it shrinks differences by $\gamma$.

**Sanity check.** For $V=U+c\mathbf 1$ every $f_a$ moves by exactly $\gamma c$, so $TV=TU+\gamma c\mathbf 1$ and the ratio is exactly $\gamma$: the bound is tight.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Lemma: $|\max f-\max g|\le\max|f-g|$, via the maximizer of $f$, in both directions.
- Cancellation: the $\ln p$ terms are equal, so $f_a(U)-f_a(V)=\gamma\,\mathbb E[U-V]$.
- Bound: at most $\gamma\lVert U-V\rVert_\infty$; take the maximum over $s$.

*Verified: on 2000 random MDPs (with some zero transition probabilities) and random pairs $U,V$, the ratio of $\lVert TU-TV\rVert_\infty$ to $\lVert U-V\rVert_\infty$ never exceeded $\gamma$ (largest ratio divided by $\gamma$: $1.0000$).*

**(c) The reward that makes T a Bellman optimality operator**

**What is asked.** A reward $r(s,a)$ for which $V^{\pi^*}$ is a fixed point of $T$, with an explanation. The answer is a formula.

**Tool and why.** The Bellman optimality equation (cheat sheet §2) and the uniqueness of a fixed point (cheat sheet §6). Linearity of expectation splits the log term off as a quantity that depends only on $(s,a)$, which is exactly what a reward is.

**Definitions.** Let $\mathcal H(p)=-\sum_x p(x)\ln p(x)$ be the entropy of a distribution. We read $\pi^*$ and $V^{\pi^*}$ as the optimal policy and its value in the MDP with the same states, actions and transitions $p$, and with the reward $r$ defined below.

**Solution.**

Step 1, split the expectation:

$$\mathbb E_{s'}\big[\ln p(s'\mid s,a)+\gamma U(s')\big]=\mathbb E_{s'}\big[\ln p(s'\mid s,a)\big]+\gamma\,\mathbb E_{s'}\big[U(s')\big].$$

Step 2, define the reward as the first term:

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}
r(s,a)&=\mathbb E_{s'\sim p(\cdot\mid s,a)}[\ln p(s'\mid s,a)]\\
&=\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)\ln p(s'\mid s,a)\\
&=-\mathcal H\big(p(\cdot\mid s,a)\big)
\end{aligned}}$$

It depends only on $(s,a)$, as a reward should.

Step 3, rewrite $T$:

$$(TU)(s)=\max_a\big[r(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb E_{s'}U(s')\big]$$

This is exactly the Bellman optimality operator of the MDP with reward $r$.

Step 4, conclusion: the optimal value $V^{\pi^*}$ of that MDP satisfies the Bellman optimality equation, so $TV^{\pi^*}=V^{\pi^*}$. By part (b) and Banach's theorem it is the only fixed point of $T$.

Step 5, an example: with two next states of probability $\tfrac12$ each, $r=-\ln2$, about $-0.693$; with a deterministic transition, $r=0$.

**In words.** The reward is the negative entropy of the next-state distribution, so the optimal agent prefers actions whose outcome is predictable.

**Sanity check.** Every $r(s,a)$ is at most $0$, with equality exactly for deterministic transitions; so $V^{\pi^*}\le0$, consistent with $T$ applied to $0$ giving the best immediate $r$, which is at most $0$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Split: $\mathbb E[\ln p+\gamma U]=\mathbb E[\ln p]+\gamma\,\mathbb E[U]$.
- Reward: $r(s,a)=\sum_{s'}p\ln p$, the negative entropy.
- Then $T$ is the Bellman optimality operator, so $V^{\pi^*}$ is its unique fixed point.

*Verified: on 300 random MDPs, 3000 iterations of $T$ match the optimal value of the MDP with reward $r$, found by evaluating every deterministic policy, to $10^{-9}$; the example $-\ln2$ recomputed.*

**Common mistakes.**

- Giving the raw $\ln p(s'\mid s,a)$ as the reward: it depends on $s'$, and the question asks for $r(s,a)$; its expectation over $s'$ is the answer.
- Asserting $|\max f-\max g|\le\max|f-g|$ without the one-line argument through the maximizer, or proving only one direction.
- Reading $\pi^*$ as optimal for the original reward $R$: the fixed point of $T$ is the optimal value for the new reward $r$.

**💡 Trick.** The $\ln p(s'\mid s,a)$ term is identical in $TU$ and $TV$, so it cancels in the contraction proof; reading it as the reward, $r(s,a)=\mathbb E_{s'}[\ln p]$, which is $-\mathcal H(p(\cdot\mid s,a))$, turns $T$ into a standard Bellman optimality operator.

**⚠️ Watch out.** The reward is $\mathbb E_{s'\sim p}[\ln p(s'\mid s,a)]$ (a function of $(s,a)$ only), not the raw $\ln p(s'\mid s,a)$; and use $|\max_a f-\max_a g|\le\max_a|f-g|$ to handle the $\max$ in the contraction step.
