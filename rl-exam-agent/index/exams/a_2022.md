## Q1 (30 pts) — Job-search MDP and the threshold "work" policy
**Topics:** MDP formalism, optimal policy structure, threshold policy, Q-function algebra | **Pillar:** Planning | **Difficulty:** 4
**Maps to:** lecture_02, lecture_03
**Statement (English translation):**
A sequential decision problem. There is a single worker and $n$ jobs; the wage of job $i$ is $r_i > 0$. At the beginning of each month:
- If the worker is **employed** at job $i$, he chooses one of:
  1. **work** — earns wage $r_i$; at month's end he is fired with probability $\alpha$ and becomes unemployed, and with probability $1-\alpha$ stays employed at job $i$.
  2. **no-work** — earns no wage this month and becomes unemployed.
- If the worker is **unemployed**, he receives an offer to work at one of the $n$ jobs; the offer is job $i$ with probability $1/n$. Given the offer for job $i$ he chooses one of:
  1. **work** — same dynamics as employed-at-$i$: earns $r_i$, then fired w.p. $\alpha$ (becomes unemployed), stays employed at $i$ w.p. $1-\alpha$.
  2. **no-work** — stays unemployed and earns no wage this month.

At the start ($t=0$) the worker is employed at job 1. Let $\pi^*$ be the optimal policy for the discounted return with discount $0<\gamma<1$, with value functions $Q^*, V^*$; $r(s,a)$ is the immediate reward.

**(a)** Write the MDP formally ($S, A, s_0, R, P$). **Hint:** it can be written with two actions and $n$ states.

**(b)** Show there exists a value $\Phi^*$ such that $Q^*(s, \text{no-work}) = \gamma\Phi^*$ for **every** state $s$.

**(c)** Given two states $s_1, s_2$ with $\pi^*(s_1)=\text{work}$ and $\pi^*(s_2)=\text{no-work}$, show $Q^*(s_1,\text{work}) \ge Q^*(s_2,\text{work})$.

**(d)** Show there exists a threshold $\Psi$ such that for every state $s$: if $\Psi < r(s,\text{work})$ then $\pi^*(s)=\text{work}$, and if $\Psi > r(s,\text{work})$ then $\pi^*(s)=\text{no-work}$. **Hint:** first show 

$$Q^*(s,\text{work}) \ge \frac{1}{1-\gamma(1-\alpha)}\big[r(s,\text{work}) + \gamma\alpha\Phi^*\big]$$

.

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) The MDP**

**What is asked.** Write the job-search story as a formal MDP: states, actions, start state, rewards and transition probabilities. The answer is a definition.

**Tool and why.** Modeling a story as an MDP (cheat sheet §12): the state is what you must know at the moment of the decision. The only decision-relevant fact is which job is on the table this month, so $n$ states suffice, as the hint says.

**Definitions.** We write $W$ for the action "work" and $\bar W$ for "no-work"; the short symbols keep the formulas readable. We write $P_a(j\mid i)$ for the probability of moving from state $i$ to state $j$ under action $a$.

**Solution.**

Step 1, states: $S=\{1,\dots,n\}$, where state $i$ means "at the start of this month job $i$ is available". This covers both "employed at $i$" and "unemployed with offer $i$". The two situations can be merged because they have the same two actions, the same reward and the same transitions: the statement says that working from an offer has the same dynamics as employed-at-$i$, and no-work leads to unemployment in both cases.

Step 2, unemployment is not a separate state: an unemployed worker receives an offer at the beginning of the month and decides at once, so "unemployed" is never a decision point. It only appears as the uniform draw of next month's offer.

Step 3, actions and start: $A=\{W,\bar W\}$ in every state, and $s_0=1$, because at $t=0$ the worker is employed at job 1.

Step 4, rewards, by the statement: $r(i,W)=r_i$ and $r(i,\bar W)=0$.

Step 5, transitions under $W$: with probability $1-\alpha$ the worker keeps job $i$. With probability $\alpha$ he is fired, and next month's offer is $j$ with probability $\tfrac1n$, which can again be $i$. Adding the two ways of landing on $i$:

$$\begin{aligned}
P_W(i\mid i)&=1-\alpha+\tfrac{\alpha}{n},\\
P_W(j\mid i)&=\tfrac{\alpha}{n},\quad j\ne i.
\end{aligned}$$

Step 6, transitions under $\bar W$: the worker becomes (or stays) unemployed, and next month's offer is uniform. So $P_{\bar W}(j\mid i)=\tfrac1n$ for every $j$, whatever $i$ is.

| state and action | next state | probability | reward |
|---|---|---|---|
| $(i,W)$ | $i$ | $1-\alpha+\tfrac{\alpha}{n}$ | $r_i$ |
| $(i,W)$ | each $j\ne i$ | $\tfrac{\alpha}{n}$ | $r_i$ |
| $(i,\bar W)$ | each $j$ | $\tfrac1n$ | $0$ |

**In words.** The worker's whole situation is "which job can I take right now". Being fired or quitting only means that the next job is drawn uniformly at random.

**Sanity check.** Every row of probabilities sums to one. Under $W$:

$$1-\alpha+\tfrac{\alpha}{n}+(n-1)\tfrac{\alpha}{n}=1.$$

Under $\bar W$ the sum is $n\cdot\tfrac1n$, which is $1$. With $\alpha=0$ a working worker keeps his job forever, exactly as the story says.

**What to write in the exam.**

- States $S=\{1,\dots,n\}$: the job currently held or offered; start $s_0=1$.
- Actions $A=\{W,\bar W\}$; rewards $r(i,W)=r_i$ and $r(i,\bar W)=0$.
- Work: stay at $i$ with probability $1-\alpha+\tfrac{\alpha}{n}$, move to each $j\ne i$ with probability $\tfrac{\alpha}{n}$.
- No-work: move to each $j$ with probability $\tfrac1n$.

*Verified: the transition matrices built in Python have unit row sums, and value iteration on them reproduces every identity of parts (b)–(d) on 3000 random instances.*

**(b) The value of no-work**

**What is asked.** Show that $Q^*(s,\bar W)$ is one and the same number $\gamma\Phi^*$ in every state, and say what $\Phi^*$ is. The answer is a formula with a short proof.

**Tool and why.** The Bellman form of $Q^*$ (cheat sheet §1 and §2): the immediate reward plus $\gamma$ times the expected optimal value of the next state. It fits because the next-state distribution of $\bar W$ does not depend on $s$.

**Definitions.** We define $\Phi^*$ as the average optimal value over the states:

$$\Phi^*=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n V^*(j).$$

It is the value of starting a month unemployed, before the offer is drawn. We introduce it because it is the only thing that no-work leads to.

**Solution.**

Step 1, Bellman equation for $\bar W$: the reward is $0$ and every next state has probability $\tfrac1n$, by part (a).

$$\begin{aligned}
Q^*(s,\bar W)&=r(s,\bar W)+\gamma\sum_{j=1}^n P_{\bar W}(j\mid s)\,V^*(j)\\
&=0+\gamma\sum_{j=1}^n\tfrac1n\,V^*(j)\\
&=\gamma\,\Phi^*.
\end{aligned}$$

Step 2, independence of $s$: the last line contains no $s$. $\Phi^*$ is a fixed number, determined by $V^*$ alone, so the same $\Phi^*$ works for every state. This proves the claim.

Running example, used in all the parts: $n=2$ jobs with wages $r_1=1$ and $r_2=14$, and $\gamma=\tfrac12$, $\alpha=\tfrac12$. Part (d) shows how to solve it exactly; the result is $V^*(1)=8$ and $V^*(2)=24$, so $\Phi^*=16$ and $Q^*(s,\bar W)=8$ in both states.

**In words.** Quitting or refusing throws the worker into the same lottery whatever job he had, so its value cannot depend on that job.

**Sanity check.** In the running example $\gamma\Phi^*$ is $\tfrac12\cdot16$, that is $8$, and computing $Q^*(1,\bar W)$ and $Q^*(2,\bar W)$ directly from the Bellman equation gives $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{8+24}{2}=8$ in both.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Bellman: $Q^*(s,\bar W)=0+\gamma\sum_j\tfrac1n V^*(j)$.
- Define $\Phi^*=\tfrac1n\sum_j V^*(j)$; it does not depend on $s$, so $Q^*(s,\bar W)=\gamma\Phi^*$ for all $s$.

*Verified: value iteration on 3000 random instances gives $Q^*(s,\bar W)=\gamma\Phi^*$ in every state to $10^{-9}$; the running example was solved in exact fractions.*

**(c) Comparing the work values**

**What is asked.** Given a state $s_1$ where work is optimal and a state $s_2$ where no-work is optimal, prove $Q^*(s_1,W)\ge Q^*(s_2,W)$. The answer is a proof.

**Tool and why.** An optimal action has the largest $Q^*$ value in its state (cheat sheet §1). Combined with part (b), both states compare work with the same number $\gamma\Phi^*$, so the two comparisons can be chained.

**Definitions.** No new notation.

**Solution.**

Step 1, at $s_1$: work is optimal, so its $Q^*$ is at least that of no-work, and by part (b) the latter is $\gamma\Phi^*$:

$$Q^*(s_1,W)\ge Q^*(s_1,\bar W)=\gamma\Phi^*.$$

Step 2, at $s_2$: no-work is optimal, so work is at most as good, and again by part (b):

$$Q^*(s_2,W)\le Q^*(s_2,\bar W)=\gamma\Phi^*.$$

Step 3, chain the two:

$$Q^*(s_1,W)\ge\gamma\Phi^*\ge Q^*(s_2,W).$$

**In words.** $\gamma\Phi^*$ is a common bar. Every state where the worker works clears it, and every state where he refuses falls below it.

**Sanity check.** In the running example work is optimal in state $2$ and no-work in state $1$ (part (d)). The work value of state $1$ is

$$Q^*(1,W)=1+\tfrac12\big(\tfrac12\cdot8+\tfrac12\cdot16\big)=7,$$

and indeed $Q^*(2,W)=24$, then $\gamma\Phi^*=8$, then $Q^*(1,W)=7$, in decreasing order.

**What to write in the exam.**

- At $s_1$: $Q^*(s_1,W)\ge Q^*(s_1,\bar W)$, which equals $\gamma\Phi^*$ by (b).
- At $s_2$: $Q^*(s_2,W)\le Q^*(s_2,\bar W)$, which equals $\gamma\Phi^*$ by (b).
- Chain the two inequalities.

*Verified: in 3000 random instances every pair of a work state and a no-work state satisfied the inequality.*

**(d) The threshold**

**What is asked.** Find one number $\Psi$, the same for all states, such that a wage above $\Psi$ forces work and a wage below $\Psi$ forces no-work. The answer is a formula for $\Psi$ and a proof of both directions.

**Tool and why.** Threshold structure (cheat sheet §4) through the Bellman equation of $W$ (§2). By part (b) the decision is "$Q^*(s,W)$ against the constant $\gamma\Phi^*$". The hint bounds $Q^*(s,W)$ from below by an expression that increases with the wage, which turns the decision into a comparison of the wage with one number.

**Definitions.** We write $r_s=r(s,W)$ for the wage in state $s$, and

$$\kappa=1-\gamma(1-\alpha),$$

which is positive because $\gamma(1-\alpha)<1$. We write $L(s)$ for the hint's lower bound:

$$L(s)=\frac{r_s+\gamma\alpha\Phi^*}{\kappa}.$$

**Solution.**

Step 1, Bellman equation for $W$, with the transitions of part (a). In the second line the term $\tfrac{\alpha}{n}V^*(s)$ moves from the first coefficient into the sum, which then runs over all $j$:

$$\begin{aligned}
Q^*(s,W)&=r_s+\gamma\Big[\big(1-\alpha+\tfrac{\alpha}{n}\big)V^*(s)+\sum_{j\ne s}\tfrac{\alpha}{n}V^*(j)\Big]\\
&=r_s+\gamma\Big[(1-\alpha)V^*(s)+\alpha\cdot\tfrac1n\sum_{j=1}^n V^*(j)\Big]\\
&=r_s+\gamma\big[(1-\alpha)V^*(s)+\alpha\Phi^*\big].
\end{aligned}$$

Step 2, the hint: $V^*(s)$ is the maximum over the two actions, so $V^*(s)\ge Q^*(s,W)$. Its coefficient $\gamma(1-\alpha)$ is non-negative, so substituting keeps the direction:

$$Q^*(s,W)\ge r_s+\gamma(1-\alpha)\,Q^*(s,W)+\gamma\alpha\Phi^*.$$

Moving the $Q^*$ term to the left:

$$\kappa\,Q^*(s,W)\ge r_s+\gamma\alpha\Phi^*.$$

Dividing by $\kappa>0$ gives $Q^*(s,W)\ge L(s)$, which is the hint.

Step 3, the equality case: if $\pi^*(s)=W$ then $V^*(s)=Q^*(s,W)$, so the inequality in step 2 is an equality and $Q^*(s,W)=L(s)$.

Step 4, compare $L(s)$ with $\gamma\Phi^*$. Put both over $\kappa$ and expand $\gamma\Phi^*\kappa$:

$$\begin{aligned}
L(s)-\gamma\Phi^*&=\frac{r_s+\gamma\alpha\Phi^*-\gamma\Phi^*\kappa}{\kappa}\\
&=\frac{r_s+\gamma\alpha\Phi^*-\gamma\Phi^*+\gamma^2(1-\alpha)\Phi^*}{\kappa}\\
&=\frac{r_s-\gamma(1-\alpha)(1-\gamma)\Phi^*}{\kappa}.
\end{aligned}$$

In the last line, $\gamma\alpha\Phi^*-\gamma\Phi^*$ is $-\gamma(1-\alpha)\Phi^*$, and adding $\gamma^2(1-\alpha)\Phi^*$ leaves $-\gamma(1-\alpha)(1-\gamma)\Phi^*$. We define

$$\boxed{\Psi=\gamma(1-\alpha)(1-\gamma)\,\Phi^*}$$

so that $L(s)-\gamma\Phi^*=\frac{r_s-\Psi}{\kappa}$. Since $\kappa>0$, $L(s)$ is above $\gamma\Phi^*$ exactly when $r_s$ is above $\Psi$.

Step 5, **Claim 1.** If $\Psi<r_s$ then $\pi^*(s)=W$.

By step 4, $L(s)>\gamma\Phi^*$. With step 2 and part (b):

$$Q^*(s,W)\ge L(s)>\gamma\Phi^*=Q^*(s,\bar W).$$

Work is strictly better than no-work, so $\pi^*(s)=W$.

Step 6, **Claim 2.** If $\Psi>r_s$ then $\pi^*(s)=\bar W$.

Suppose, for contradiction, that $\pi^*(s)=W$. By step 3, $Q^*(s,W)=L(s)$, and by step 4, $L(s)<\gamma\Phi^*$. So

$$Q^*(s,W)<\gamma\Phi^*=Q^*(s,\bar W),$$

and work is not optimal in $s$, a contradiction. Hence $\pi^*(s)=\bar W$.

Step 7, $\Psi$ is the same for every state: it depends only on $\gamma$, $\alpha$ and $\Phi^*$, and none of them depends on $s$. (When $r_s=\Psi$ both actions tie; the question does not ask about this case.)

Step 8, the running example: $\gamma=\tfrac12$ and $\alpha=\tfrac12$ give $\kappa=\tfrac34$. Guess that job 1 is refused and job 2 is taken. Then $V^*(1)=\gamma\Phi^*$, which is $\tfrac12\Phi^*$, and by step 3:

$$V^*(2)=L(2)=\frac{14+\tfrac14\Phi^*}{\tfrac34}=\frac{56}{3}+\frac{\Phi^*}{3}.$$

Averaging the two values gives an equation for $\Phi^*$:

$$\begin{aligned}
2\Phi^*&=\tfrac12\Phi^*+\tfrac{56}{3}+\tfrac13\Phi^*\\
\tfrac76\Phi^*&=\tfrac{56}{3}\\
\Phi^*&=16.
\end{aligned}$$

So $V^*(1)=8$ and $V^*(2)=24$, and $\Psi=\tfrac18\cdot16$, which is $2$. The guess is consistent: $r_1=1$ is below $\Psi$ and $r_2=14$ is above it.

| state | wage | $Q^*(s,W)$ | $L(s)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1$ | $1$ | $7$ | $\tfrac{20}{3}$ |
| $2$ | $14$ | $24$ | $24$ |

Both have $Q^*(s,\bar W)=8$. In state 2 the hint holds with equality (work is optimal); in state 1 it is strict, $7$ against $\tfrac{20}{3}$.

**In words.** This is a reservation wage: take a job exactly when its wage beats $\Psi$. Better outside options (larger $\Phi^*$) make the worker pickier, and a large firing rate $\alpha$ makes him less picky, because a bad job will not last long anyway.

**Sanity check.** With $\alpha=1$ the formula gives $\Psi=0$, so every job is taken. That is right: working then pays $r_s$ and returns the worker to the lottery, so $Q^*(s,W)=r_s+\gamma\Phi^*$, which beats $\gamma\Phi^*$. As $\gamma$ tends to $0$, $\Psi$ also tends to $0$: a myopic worker takes any positive wage.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Bellman equation for work:

$$Q^*(s,W)=r_s+\gamma\big[(1-\alpha)V^*(s)+\alpha\Phi^*\big].$$

- Using $V^*(s)\ge Q^*(s,W)$ gives the hint bound $L(s)$, with equality when work is optimal.
- Algebra: $L(s)-\gamma\Phi^*=\frac{r_s-\Psi}{\kappa}$, with $\Psi=\gamma(1-\alpha)(1-\gamma)\Phi^*$.
- If $r_s>\Psi$: then $L(s)>\gamma\Phi^*$ and $Q^*(s,W)\ge L(s)$, so work.
- If $r_s<\Psi$ and work were optimal, then $Q^*(s,W)=L(s)$, which is below $\gamma\Phi^*$, a contradiction; so no-work.

*Verified: value iteration on 3000 random instances ($n$ up to 8, random $\gamma$ and $\alpha$): the rule "work iff $r_s>\Psi$" matched the optimal action in all 14842 non-tied states, and $Q^*(s,W)=L(s)$ held in every work state; the running example was solved exactly with sympy.*

**Common mistakes.**

- Writing $P_W(i\mid i)=1-\alpha$ and forgetting that a fired worker may be offered the same job again, which adds $\tfrac{\alpha}{n}$.
- Using the hint bound as an equality in every state. It is an equality only where work is optimal; in the running example state 1 has $7$ against $\tfrac{20}{3}$.
- Proving only Claim 1. The threshold statement has two directions, and Claim 2 needs the equality case of step 3.

**💡 Trick.** "No-work" resets every state to the same uniform next-state distribution, so $Q^*(s,\text{no-work})=\gamma\Phi^*$ is a single state-independent constant — the optimal decision reduces to comparing $r(s,\text{work})$ against one global threshold.

**⚠️ Watch out.** The clean form $Q^*(s,\text{work})=L(s)$, with $L(s)$ the hint bound, is an *equality* only where work is optimal (there $V^*(s)=Q^*(s,\text{work})$); using it as an equality everywhere, instead of the inequality $V^*(s)\ge Q^*(s,\text{work})$, breaks the argument.

## Q2 (30 pts) — POMDP: belief update and finite-horizon value iteration
**Topics:** POMDP, belief state, Bayesian filtering, value iteration, horizon | **Pillar:** Planning | **Difficulty:** 4
**Maps to:** lecture_11, lecture_03
**Statement (English translation):**
A POMDP with two states, three actions; the **observation is the immediate reward**.
$S=\{s_1,s_2\}$, $A=\{a_1,a_2,a_3\}$. Transitions: $p(s\mid s,a_1)=p(s\mid s,a_2)=1$ for all $s$ (actions $a_1,a_2$ are the identity); 

$$p(s_1\mid s_2,a_3)=p(s_2\mid s_2,a_3)=p(s_2\mid s_1,a_3)=p(s_1\mid s_1,a_3)=1/2$$

 ($a_3$ jumps to a uniform next state).
Reward $r(s,a)=1$ if $(s,a)\in\{(s_1,a_1),(s_2,a_2)\}$; $=2/3$ if $a=a_3$; $=0$ otherwise.
Observations $O=\{0,2/3,1\}$; nonzero $Ob(\cdot\mid s',a)$ (with $s'$ the **next** state): $Ob(1\mid s_1,a_1)=Ob(1\mid s_2,a_2)=1$; $Ob(0\mid s_1,a_2)=Ob(0\mid s_2,a_1)=1$; $Ob(2/3\mid s_1,a_3)=Ob(2/3\mid s_2,a_3)=1$.
Let $x$ = probability of being in $s_1$ (belief).

**(a)** Compute the belief-update posterior in the cases $P(s_1\mid a_1,o=1,x)$, $P(s_1\mid a_1,o=0,x)$, $P(s_1\mid a_3,o=2/3,x)$.

**(b)** Assume horizon $H=1$. (b1) Compute $V_1^*(x\mid a_i)$ for each action. (b2) Compute $V_1^*(x)$.

**(c)** For $H=2$, compute $V_2^*(x)$. **Hint:** $V_2^*(x\mid a_1)=V_2^*(1-x\mid a_2)$.

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) Belief updates**

**What is asked.** Three posteriors: the probability of $s_1$ after taking an action and seeing an observation, starting from belief $x$. The answers are numbers.

**Tool and why.** The Bayesian belief update of a POMDP (cheat sheet §11): first push the belief through the transition, then reweight by the observation likelihood and normalize. The observations here are deterministic given the next state, so the reweighting either keeps a state or kills it.

**Definitions.** The prior belief is $b=(x,1-x)$ over $(s_1,s_2)$. For a fixed action $a$ and observation $o$ we define the unnormalized weight of a next state $s'$:

$$w(s')=Ob(o\mid s',a)\sum_s b(s)\,p(s'\mid s,a).$$

The posterior of $s_1$ is $w(s_1)$ divided by $w(s_1)+w(s_2)$, and that sum is the probability of seeing $o$.

**Solution.**

Step 1, prediction under $a_1$: $a_1$ is the identity, so the predicted distribution of the next state is still $(x,1-x)$.

Step 2, $a_1$ with $o=1$: the likelihoods are $Ob(1\mid s_1,a_1)=1$ and $Ob(1\mid s_2,a_1)=0$, so

$$P(s_1\mid a_1,o=1,x)=\frac{x\cdot1}{x\cdot1+(1-x)\cdot0}=1.$$

This needs $x>0$, which is exactly when $o=1$ can be observed.

Step 3, $a_1$ with $o=0$: the likelihoods are $Ob(0\mid s_1,a_1)=0$ and $Ob(0\mid s_2,a_1)=1$, so

$$P(s_1\mid a_1,o=0,x)=\frac{x\cdot0}{x\cdot0+(1-x)\cdot1}=0.$$

This needs $x<1$, again exactly when $o=0$ can be observed.

Step 4, $a_3$: from either state $a_3$ moves to each state with probability $\tfrac12$, so the predicted probability of $s_1$ is $\tfrac12x+\tfrac12(1-x)$, which is $\tfrac12$. Both states give the observation $\tfrac23$ with probability $1$, so

$$P(s_1\mid a_3,o=2/3,x)=\frac{\tfrac12\cdot1}{\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac12\cdot1}=\frac12.$$

| action and observation | probability of it | posterior of $s_1$ |
|---|---|---|
| $a_1$, $o=1$ | $x$ | $1$ |
| $a_1$, $o=0$ | $1-x$ | $0$ |
| $a_3$, $o=2/3$ | $1$ | $\tfrac12$ |

**In words.** Under $a_1$ the reward says whether we were in $s_1$, and $a_1$ does not move us, so the state becomes known. Under $a_3$ the state is scrambled and the observation is the same in both states, so we learn nothing and the belief is the uniform one.

**Sanity check.** The average posterior under $a_1$ must equal the prior: $x\cdot1+(1-x)\cdot0$ is $x$. Under $a_3$ the posterior does not depend on $x$ at all, as it should after a uniform jump.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Formula:

$$b'(s')\propto Ob(o\mid s',a)\sum_s b(s)p(s'\mid s,a).$$

- Action $a_1$: the observation reveals the state, so the posterior is $1$ after $o=1$ and $0$ after $o=0$.
- Action $a_3$: the next state is uniform and the observation is uninformative, so the posterior is $\tfrac12$.

*Verified: the Bayes update in exact fractions at 61 belief values gives exactly these posteriors and observation probabilities.*

**(b) Horizon 1**

**What is asked.** (b1) the value of each action with one step left, and (b2) the optimal one-step value, both as functions of $x$.

**Tool and why.** Finite-horizon value iteration on beliefs (cheat sheet §3 and §11). With one step left there is no future, so the value of an action is its expected immediate reward under the belief.

**Definitions.** We write $R(x,a)$ for the expected immediate reward of action $a$ under belief $x$:

$$R(x,a)=x\,r(s_1,a)+(1-x)\,r(s_2,a).$$

With $H=1$, $V_1^*(x\mid a)=R(x,a)$.

**Solution.**

Step 1, $a_1$: reward $1$ in $s_1$ and $0$ in $s_2$, so $V_1^*(x\mid a_1)=x$.

Step 2, $a_2$: reward $0$ in $s_1$ and $1$ in $s_2$, so $V_1^*(x\mid a_2)=1-x$.

Step 3, $a_3$: reward $\tfrac23$ in both states, so $V_1^*(x\mid a_3)=\tfrac23$.

Step 4, (b2), take the best action:

$$V_1^*(x)=\max(x,\,1-x,\,2/3)$$

The line $1-x$ is at least $\tfrac23$ exactly when $x\le\tfrac13$, and $x$ is at least $\tfrac23$ exactly when $x\ge\tfrac23$. So

$$V_1^*(x)=\begin{cases}1-x, & x\le\tfrac13,\\ \tfrac23, & \tfrac13\le x\le\tfrac23,\\ x, & x\ge\tfrac23.\end{cases}$$

The values needed in part (c) are $V_1^*(0)=1$, $V_1^*(1)=1$ and $V_1^*(\tfrac12)=\tfrac23$.

**In words.** When we are fairly sure of the state we bet on it ($a_1$ or $a_2$). When we are unsure, between $\tfrac13$ and $\tfrac23$, the safe $\tfrac23$ of $a_3$ is better than a guess.

**Sanity check.** At $x=\tfrac12$ a guess earns $\tfrac12$ on average, less than $\tfrac23$, so $a_3$ wins, as the middle branch says. $V_1^*$ is a maximum of lines, so it is convex in $x$, as every POMDP value function is.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Expected rewards: $x$ for $a_1$, $1-x$ for $a_2$, $\tfrac23$ for $a_3$.
- Then $V_1^*(x)=\max(x,1-x,\tfrac23)$: action $a_2$ up to $\tfrac13$, $a_3$ between $\tfrac13$ and $\tfrac23$, and $a_1$ from $\tfrac23$ on.

*Verified: exact-fraction evaluation at 61 belief values matches all three action values and the maximum.*

**(c) Horizon 2**

**What is asked.** The optimal two-step value $V_2^*(x)$ as a function of $x$.

**Tool and why.** One-step lookahead in belief space (cheat sheet §3 and §11): the value of an action is its expected immediate reward plus the expected one-step value at the posterior belief. The statement has no discount, so the two rewards are simply added.

**Definitions.** We write $x'_{a,o}$ for the posterior of part (a) after action $a$ and observation $o$, and $P(o\mid x,a)$ for the probability of that observation (the middle column of the table in part (a)). Then

$$V_2^*(x\mid a)=R(x,a)+\sum_o P(o\mid x,a)\,V_1^*(x'_{a,o}).$$

**Solution.**

Step 1, $a_1$: with probability $x$ we see $o=1$ and the posterior is $1$; with probability $1-x$ we see $o=0$ and the posterior is $0$. Both have one-step value $1$ by part (b):

$$\begin{aligned}
V_2^*(x\mid a_1)&=x+x\,V_1^*(1)+(1-x)\,V_1^*(0)\\
&=x+x\cdot1+(1-x)\cdot1\\
&=x+1.
\end{aligned}$$

Step 2, $a_2$: by the hint, $V_2^*(x\mid a_2)$ is $V_2^*(1-x\mid a_1)$, which by step 1 is $(1-x)+1$. Directly: the reward is $1-x$, and $a_2$ also reveals the state, so the next step is worth $1$ in both cases.

$$V_2^*(x\mid a_2)=2-x.$$

Step 3, $a_3$: the observation is $\tfrac23$ with probability $1$ and the posterior is $\tfrac12$, by part (a):

$$V_2^*(x\mid a_3)=\tfrac23+1\cdot V_1^*(\tfrac12)=\tfrac23+\tfrac23=\tfrac43.$$

Step 4, compare: the lines $x+1$ and $2-x$ cross at $x=\tfrac12$ with value $\tfrac32$, so their maximum is at least $\tfrac32$ for every $x$. Since $\tfrac32>\tfrac43$, action $a_3$ is never optimal at $H=2$, and

$$\boxed{V_2^*(x)=\max(x+1,\,2-x,\,4/3)=1+\max(x,1-x)}$$

| $x$ | $a_1$ | $a_2$ | $a_3$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $\tfrac43$ |
| $\tfrac14$ | $\tfrac54$ | $\tfrac74$ | $\tfrac43$ |
| $\tfrac12$ | $\tfrac32$ | $\tfrac32$ | $\tfrac43$ |
| $1$ | $2$ | $1$ | $\tfrac43$ |

**In words.** With two steps left, $a_1$ and $a_2$ do two jobs: they bet on the state now and reveal it, so the second step surely earns $1$. Action $a_3$ earns a safe $\tfrac23$ but scrambles the state and teaches nothing, so it only earns $\tfrac23$ again.

**Sanity check.** At $x=1$ the state is known to be $s_1$, and playing $a_1$ twice earns $2$, which matches $1+\max(1,0)$. At $x=\tfrac12$, where $a_3$ was best for one step, guessing now and exploiting next step gives $\tfrac32$, more than $a_3$ twice ($\tfrac43$).

**What to write in the exam.**

- Lookahead:

$$V_2^*(x\mid a)=R(x,a)+\sum_o P(o\mid x,a)V_1^*(x'_{a,o}).$$

- Action $a_1$: posteriors $1$ and $0$, both worth $1$, so $x+1$; by symmetry $a_2$ gives $2-x$.
- Action $a_3$: posterior $\tfrac12$, so $\tfrac23+\tfrac23=\tfrac43$.
- Answer: $V_2^*(x)=1+\max(x,1-x)$, since that maximum is at least $\tfrac32$.

*Verified: generic belief-space lookahead in exact fractions at 61 belief values gives these three action values, the hint identity, and $V_2^*$; its minimum is $\tfrac32$ at $x=\tfrac12$.*

**Common mistakes.**

- Ignoring the uniform jump of $a_3$ and leaving the belief at $x$; the posterior is $\tfrac12$.
- Evaluating the second step at the prior, writing $V_2^*(x\mid a_1)=x+V_1^*(x)$; this throws away the information that $a_1$ reveals.
- Concluding that $a_3$ is optimal near $x=\tfrac12$ at $H=2$ because it was optimal there at $H=1$.

**💡 Trick.** Because the observation *is* the reward, $a_1$ fully reveals the state (posterior collapses to $0$ or $1$), so its lookahead value is exact: next-step value is $1$ regardless, giving $V_2^*(x\mid a_1)=x+1$ and, by symmetry, $V_2^*(x\mid a_2)=2-x$.

**⚠️ Watch out.** Don't confuse the immediate reward $2/3$ of $a_3$ with a good long-run choice — at $H=2$ its total $4/3$ never beats $\max(x+1,2-x)\ge3/2$, so $a_3$ is never strictly optimal.

## Q3 (20 pts) — Quadratic-linear policy: representability, score function, REINFORCE
**Topics:** parametric policy, policy gradient, score function, REINFORCE | **Pillar:** Approximation | **Difficulty:** 3
**Maps to:** lecture_09, lecture_10
**Statement (English translation):**
An MDP where each state $s$ and action $a$ has a feature vector $\phi(s,a)\in\mathbb{R}^d$ ($S,A$ finite). A policy is parameterized by $\theta\in\mathbb{R}^d$, $\theta\neq 0$, with
$$\pi(a\mid s;\theta)=\frac{(\phi(s,a)\cdot\theta)^2}{\sum_{a'}(\phi(s,a')\cdot\theta)^2}.$$

**(a)** In state $s$ there are three actions with 

$$\phi(s,a_1)=(1,-1),\ \phi(s,a_2)=(-1,1),\ \phi(s,a_3)=(1,1)$$

.

 (a1) Is there $\theta$ with $\pi(a_2\mid s;\theta)=\pi(a_1\mid s;\theta)=0$ and $\pi(a_3\mid s;\theta)=1$? (yes/no; give $\theta$ or explain).

 (a2) Is there, for every distribution $(p_1,p_2,p_3)$, a $\theta$ with $\pi(a_i\mid s;\theta)=p_i$? (yes/no; give a counterexample or explain).

**(b)** Compute $\nabla_\theta\log\pi(a\mid s;\theta)$ (explain).

**(c)** Write the REINFORCE update.

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) Part a1: all the mass on $a_3$**

**What is asked.** Whether some $\theta$ gives the policy $(0,0,1)$ in state $s$. The answer is yes or no, with a $\theta$ or a reason.

**Tool and why.** A parametric policy (cheat sheet §9) is zero on an action exactly when its numerator is zero. The numerators are squares of linear functions of $\theta$, so the question becomes a small linear system.

**Definitions.** We write $\theta=(\theta_1,\theta_2)$ and $m_i=\phi(s,a_i)\cdot\theta$ for the inner product of action $a_i$, the number that is squared in the numerator.

**Solution.**

Step 1, the inner products: $m_1=\theta_1-\theta_2$, then $m_2=-\theta_1+\theta_2$, which is $-m_1$, and $m_3=\theta_1+\theta_2$.

Step 2, the conditions: $\pi(a_1\mid s;\theta)=0$ needs $m_1=0$, that is $\theta_1=\theta_2$. Then $m_2$ is $0$ too. With both zero, $\pi(a_3\mid s;\theta)$ is $\tfrac{m_3^2}{m_3^2}$, which is $1$ as long as $m_3\ne0$, that is $\theta_1\ne-\theta_2$.

Step 3, an example: $\theta=(1,1)$ gives $m_1=0$, $m_2=0$ and $m_3=2$. The numerators are $(0,0,4)$ and their sum is $4$, so $\pi=(0,0,1)$.

**Answer: yes**, for example $\theta=(1,1)$.

**In words.** $\theta=(1,1)$ is orthogonal to the features of $a_1$ and $a_2$ and not to that of $a_3$, so only $a_3$ has a nonzero numerator.

**Sanity check.** Any $\theta=(c,c)$ with $c\ne0$ works as well; $c=0$ is excluded by the statement, and it would give $\tfrac00$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Need $m_1=0$ and $m_2=0$, both meaning $\theta_1=\theta_2$, and $m_3\ne0$.
- Example $\theta=(1,1)$: numerators $(0,0,4)$, so $\pi=(0,0,1)$. Yes.

*Verified: numpy evaluation of the policy at $\theta=(1,1)$ gives exactly $(0,0,1)$.*

**(a) Part a2: every distribution?**

**What is asked.** Whether every distribution $(p_1,p_2,p_3)$ is $\pi(\cdot\mid s;\theta)$ for some $\theta$. The answer is yes or no, with a counterexample or a reason.

**Tool and why.** Look for a quantity that no $\theta$ can change. The features of $a_1$ and $a_2$ are opposite, and squaring forgets the sign, which is such an invariant.

**Definitions.** $m_i$ as in part a1. We also write $Z=m_1^2+m_2^2+m_3^2$ for the denominator.

**Solution.**

Step 1, the invariant: $\phi(s,a_2)=-\phi(s,a_1)$, so $m_2=-m_1$ and $m_2^2=m_1^2$ for every $\theta$.

Step 2, the denominator is positive: $Z=0$ would need $m_1=0$ and $m_3=0$, that is $\theta_1=\theta_2$ and $\theta_1=-\theta_2$, which forces $\theta=0$, excluded by the statement.

Step 3, consequence: $a_1$ and $a_2$ have equal numerators and the same denominator, so $\pi(a_1\mid s;\theta)=\pi(a_2\mid s;\theta)$ for every allowed $\theta$.

Step 4, counterexample: the distribution $(1,0,0)$ needs $\pi(a_1\mid s;\theta)=1$ and $\pi(a_2\mid s;\theta)=0$, which contradicts step 3. So no $\theta$ produces it.

**Answer: no**; for example $(1,0,0)$, or any target with $p_1\ne p_2$.

Step 5, what is reachable (beyond what is asked): with $u=m_1$ and $v=m_3$ the policy is

$$\pi=\Big(\frac{u^2}{2u^2+v^2},\ \frac{u^2}{2u^2+v^2},\ \frac{v^2}{2u^2+v^2}\Big).$$

The map from $\theta$ to $(u,v)$ is invertible, so every nonzero pair $(u,v)$ occurs, and the first entry takes every value from $0$ to $\tfrac12$. So the reachable distributions are exactly those with $p_1=p_2$. For example $\theta=(1,0)$ gives $(\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13)$, and $\theta=(1,-1)$ gives $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0)$.

**In words.** The square makes the policy blind to the sign of $\phi\cdot\theta$, and $a_1$, $a_2$ differ only by a sign, so the policy must treat them identically.

**Sanity check.** Counting parameters would not settle this: $\theta$ has two coordinates and the distributions of three actions also form a two-dimensional set. It is the symmetry that rules out half of them.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Features $\phi(s,a_2)=-\phi(s,a_1)$, so the squared inner products are equal for every $\theta$.
- Hence $\pi(a_1\mid s;\theta)=\pi(a_2\mid s;\theta)$ always.
- Counterexample $(1,0,0)$, or any $p_1\ne p_2$: No.

*Verified: for 100000 random $\theta$ the first two probabilities agreed to $10^{-12}$, and the largest $\pi(a_1\mid s;\theta)$ found was $0.49999\ldots$; the three example distributions were recomputed in exact fractions.*

**(b) The score function**

**What is asked.** The gradient of $\log\pi(a\mid s;\theta)$ with respect to $\theta$, with an explanation. The answer is a formula.

**Tool and why.** The score function (cheat sheet §9): the log of a ratio is a difference of logs, and each log is differentiated with the chain rule. The result has the usual form "own term minus its average under the policy".

**Definitions.** We write $m_a=\phi(s,a)\cdot\theta$ for the inner product of action $a$ and $Z(\theta)=\sum_{a'}m_{a'}^2$ for the normalizer. The score is defined where $m_a\ne0$, that is where $\pi(a\mid s;\theta)>0$; these are the only actions that can ever be sampled.

**Solution.**

Step 1, take logs: $\log\pi=\log m_a^2-\log Z$, which is $2\log|m_a|-\log Z$.

Step 2, the first term: $m_a$ is linear in $\theta$ with gradient $\phi(s,a)$, and the derivative of $\log|m|$ is $\tfrac1m$ for either sign of $m$. So the gradient of $2\log|m_a|$ is $\tfrac{2\phi(s,a)}{m_a}$.

Step 3, the second term: each $m_{a'}^2$ has gradient $2m_{a'}\phi(s,a')$, so the gradient of $\log Z$ is $\tfrac{2\sum_{a'}m_{a'}\phi(s,a')}{Z}$.

Step 4, combine:

$$\begin{aligned}\nabla_\theta\log\pi(a\mid s;\theta)&=\frac{2\,\phi(s,a)}{\phi(s,a)\cdot\theta}-\frac{2\sum_{a'}(\phi(s,a')\cdot\theta)\,\phi(s,a')}{\sum_{a'}(\phi(s,a')\cdot\theta)^2}\\ &=\frac{2\phi(s,a)}{\phi(s,a)\cdot\theta}-\mathbb{E}_{a'\sim\pi}\!\Big[\frac{2\phi(s,a')}{\phi(s,a')\cdot\theta}\Big].\end{aligned}$$

Step 5, why the second line equals the first: for each $a'$ with $m_{a'}\ne0$,

$$\pi(a'\mid s;\theta)\cdot\frac{2\phi(s,a')}{m_{a'}}=\frac{m_{a'}^2}{Z}\cdot\frac{2\phi(s,a')}{m_{a'}}=\frac{2m_{a'}\phi(s,a')}{Z},$$

and summing over $a'$ gives the second term of the first line. Actions with $m_{a'}=0$ contribute $0$ to the first line and have probability $0$ in the expectation.

Step 6, a worked example in the state of part (a) with $\theta=(1,0)$: the inner products are $(1,-1,1)$ and $Z=3$. The weighted sum of features is

$$1\cdot(1,-1)+(-1)\cdot(-1,1)+1\cdot(1,1)=(3,-1),$$

so the second term is $\tfrac23(3,-1)$, which is $(2,-\tfrac23)$.

| action | $\tfrac{2\phi(s,a)}{m_a}$ | score |
|---|---|---|
| $a_1$ | $(2,-2)$ | $(0,-\tfrac43)$ |
| $a_2$ | $(2,-2)$ | $(0,-\tfrac43)$ |
| $a_3$ | $(2,2)$ | $(0,\tfrac83)$ |

**In words.** The score pushes $\theta$ toward the action's own (scaled) feature and away from the policy's average feature; raising the probability of one action must lower the others.

**Sanity check.** Two checks on the table. The scores average to zero under $\pi=(\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13)$, as every score does, because the probabilities sum to one for every $\theta$. And every score is orthogonal to $\theta=(1,0)$: the policy does not change when $\theta$ is scaled, so the derivative along $\theta$ is zero.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Logs:

$$\log\pi=2\log|\phi(s,a)\cdot\theta|-\log\sum_{a'}(\phi(s,a')\cdot\theta)^2.$$

- Differentiate each term with the chain rule.
- Result: $\tfrac{2\phi(s,a)}{\phi(s,a)\cdot\theta}$ minus its expectation under $\pi$, as in the display above.

*Verified: on 2000 random instances (dimension up to 5, up to 5 actions) the formula matched central finite differences of $\log\pi$ to relative error $1.3\cdot10^{-5}$, both forms agreed, and the zero-mean and $\theta$-orthogonality identities held; the table was recomputed.*

**(c) The REINFORCE update**

**What is asked.** The REINFORCE update rule for this policy. The answer is an algorithm and an update formula.

**Tool and why.** The policy gradient theorem with Monte-Carlo returns (cheat sheet §9): the gradient of the expected return is an expectation of the score times the return that follows, so a sampled episode gives an unbiased estimate.

**Definitions.** An episode $s_0,a_0,r_0,\dots,s_T,a_T,r_T$ is generated with the current policy. $G_t=\sum_{k\ge t}\gamma^{k-t}r_k$ is the return-to-go from time $t$, and $\eta_t$ is a step size.

**Solution.**

Step 1, sample an episode with $\pi(\cdot\mid\cdot;\theta)$.

Step 2, compute the returns backwards: $G_T=r_T$, then $G_t=r_t+\gamma G_{t+1}$.

Step 3, for each $t$ update with the score of part (b) at $(s_t,a_t)$:

$$\theta\leftarrow\theta+\eta_t\,\gamma^t G_t\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi(a_t\mid s_t;\theta)$$

Step 4, written out with part (b):

$$\begin{aligned}
\theta\leftarrow\theta+2\eta_t\gamma^tG_t\Big[&\frac{\phi(s_t,a_t)}{\phi(s_t,a_t)\cdot\theta}\\
&-\frac{\sum_{a'}(\phi(s_t,a')\cdot\theta)\,\phi(s_t,a')}{\sum_{a'}(\phi(s_t,a')\cdot\theta)^2}\Big].
\end{aligned}$$

Step 5, the lecture's undiscounted episodic form is the case $\gamma=1$, with $R_{t:T}$ the sum of rewards from $t$ on:

$$\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha\sum_t R_{t:T}\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi(a_t\mid s_t;\theta).$$

**In words.** Actions followed by large returns get their log-probability raised more than actions followed by small returns.

**Sanity check.** The update never divides by zero: a sampled action $a_t$ has positive probability, so $\phi(s_t,a_t)\cdot\theta\ne0$. And on a one-state, one-step problem the expected update direction $\sum_a\pi(a)\,r(a)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi(a)$ equals the true gradient of the expected reward.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Sample an episode with $\pi_\theta$ and compute the returns $G_t$.
- Update, with the score of (b):

$$\theta\leftarrow\theta+\eta_t\gamma^tG_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi(a_t\mid s_t;\theta).$$


*Verified: on a random one-state problem with three actions the exact expected REINFORCE direction, $(-0.8494,1.1731)$, matched the finite-difference gradient of the expected reward.*

**Common mistakes.**

- Writing $\log\pi=2\log(\phi(s,a)\cdot\theta)-\log Z$ for a negative inner product; the absolute value is needed, though the derivative is $\tfrac1m$ either way.
- Forgetting the gradient of the normalizer, which gives a score without the expected-feature term and a nonzero mean.
- Answering "yes" in a2 by counting parameters; the symmetry $\phi(s,a_2)=-\phi(s,a_1)$ decides it.

**💡 Trick.** With a squared-linear policy $\log\pi=2\log|\phi(s,a)\cdot\theta|-\log Z$, so the score is $\frac{2\phi(s,a)}{\phi(s,a)\cdot\theta}$ minus its $\pi$-average — the familiar "feature minus expected feature" form. For (a2), $\phi(s,a_1)=-\phi(s,a_2)$ forces $\pi(a_1)=\pi(a_2)$ always, killing any target with $p_1\neq p_2$.

**⚠️ Watch out.** The square means the policy sees $|\phi\cdot\theta|$, not its sign, so opposite feature vectors are indistinguishable; also the score blows up where $\phi(s,a)\cdot\theta=0$, so don't treat it as defined at a zero-numerator action.

## Q4 (20 pts) — A max-over-all Bellman-type operator: contraction and fixed point
**Topics:** contraction mapping, sup-norm, Bellman operator, fixed point | **Pillar:** Planning | **Difficulty:** 3
**Maps to:** lecture_03, lecture_02
**Statement (English translation):**
MDP $M=(S,A,p,s_0,r)$. For $U\in\mathbb{R}^{|S\times A|}$ and $0<\gamma<1$ define
$$(TU)(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\max_{a',s'}U(s',a').$$

**(a)** State the definition of $H$ being $\gamma$-contracting in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$.

**(b)** Prove $T$ is $\gamma$-contracting in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$.

**(c)** What is the fixed point $U^*$ of $T$? For every $(s,a)$ give the value $T$ converges to, as a function of the MDP parameters only. **Hint:** start from the $(s,a)$ with maximal reward.

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) Definition of a γ-contraction**

**What is asked.** The definition of "$H$ is a $\gamma$-contraction in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$". The answer is a definition.

**Tool and why.** Operators, contraction and fixed points (cheat sheet §6): this definition is the assumption of Banach's fixed-point theorem, which parts (b) and (c) use.

**Definitions.** For $U\in\mathbb{R}^{|S\times A|}$, $\|U\|_\infty=\max_{s,a}|U(s,a)|$, the largest entry in absolute value.

**Solution.**

Step 1, the definition: an operator $H$ from $\mathbb{R}^{|S\times A|}$ to itself is a $\gamma$-contraction in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$, with $\gamma\in[0,1)$, if for all $U,V$

$$\|HU-HV\|_\infty\le\gamma\|U-V\|_\infty.$$

Step 2, what it gives (Banach): such an $H$ has a unique fixed point, and repeated application of $H$ from any starting point converges to it at rate $\gamma^k$.

**In words.** Applying $H$ brings any two vectors closer by at least a factor $\gamma$, measured at their worst entry.

**Sanity check.** The identity operator satisfies the inequality only with factor $1$, so it is not a contraction; indeed every vector is a fixed point of it, not a unique one.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Definition: for all $U,V$, $\|HU-HV\|_\infty\le\gamma\|U-V\|_\infty$, with $\gamma\in[0,1)$.

*Verified: this inequality is exactly the ratio test run in part (b) on 2000 random MDPs.*

**(b) Proof that T is a γ-contraction**

**What is asked.** A proof of the inequality of part (a) for the given $T$.

**Tool and why.** Contraction in the sup norm (cheat sheet §6). The reward term is the same for $U$ and $V$ and cancels, so everything reduces to how much a maximum can change.

**Definitions.** We write $M(U)=\max_{s',a'}U(s',a')$ for the largest entry of $U$, so that $(TU)(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma M(U)$.

**Solution.**

Step 1, the difference: $r(s,a)$ cancels, for every $(s,a)$:

$$(TU)(s,a)-(TV)(s,a)=\gamma\big[\max_{s',a'}U(s',a')-\max_{s',a'}V(s',a')\big]$$

Step 2, **Claim.** $|M(U)-M(V)|\le\|U-V\|_\infty$.

Let $(s_1,a_1)$ be an entry where $U$ is largest, so $U(s_1,a_1)=M(U)$. Since $M(V)$ is at least $V(s_1,a_1)$:

$$\begin{aligned}
M(U)-M(V)&\le U(s_1,a_1)-V(s_1,a_1)\\
&\le\|U-V\|_\infty.
\end{aligned}$$

Swapping the roles of $U$ and $V$ gives the same bound for $M(V)-M(U)$, which proves the claim.

Step 3, conclusion: by steps 1 and 2, $|(TU)(s,a)-(TV)(s,a)|\le\gamma\|U-V\|_\infty$ for every $(s,a)$. The right side does not depend on $(s,a)$, so taking the maximum over $(s,a)$:

$$\|TU-TV\|_\infty\le\gamma\|U-V\|_\infty.$$

**In words.** $T$ depends on $U$ only through one number, the largest entry of $U$, and that number cannot move by more than the largest change of an entry.

**Sanity check.** For $V=U+c$ (every entry raised by $c$) the maximum moves by exactly $c$, so $TV=TU+\gamma c$ and the ratio is exactly $\gamma$: the bound is tight.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Difference: $r(s,a)$ cancels, leaving $\gamma\big(M(U)-M(V)\big)$.
- Bound $|M(U)-M(V)|\le\|U-V\|_\infty$ through the entry where the maximum is attained, in both directions.
- Conclusion: maximum over $(s,a)$ gives the contraction.

*Verified: on 2000 random MDPs with random pairs $U,V$, the ratio $\|TU-TV\|_\infty/\|U-V\|_\infty$ never exceeded $\gamma$ (largest ratio divided by $\gamma$: $1.0000$), the shift $V=U+c$ gave ratio exactly $\gamma$, and the max lemma held on 10000 random pairs.*

**(c) The fixed point**

**What is asked.** An explicit formula for $U^*(s,a)$ in terms of $\gamma$ and the rewards. The answer is a formula.

**Tool and why.** Fixed-point equation (cheat sheet §6): by part (b) and Banach the fixed point is unique, so it suffices to find one vector solving $U=TU$. The hint suggests looking at the largest reward, because the largest entry of $U^*$ sits where the reward is largest.

**Definitions.** We write $r_{\max}=\max_{s',a'}r(s',a')$ for the largest reward, and $C=\max_{s',a'}U^*(s',a')$ for the largest entry of the fixed point. Reducing the problem to the single unknown $C$ is the whole trick.

**Solution.**

Step 1, the fixed-point equation, for every $(s,a)$:

$$U^*(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma C.$$

Step 2, take the maximum over $(s,a)$ on both sides: $\gamma C$ is the same for all $(s,a)$, and the maximum of $r(s,a)$ is $r_{\max}$, so

$$C=r_{\max}+\gamma C.$$

Step 3, solve for $C$: $(1-\gamma)C=r_{\max}$, so

$$C=\frac{r_{\max}}{1-\gamma}.$$

Step 4, substitute back into step 1:

$$\boxed{U^*(s,a)=r(s,a)+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\,r_{\max},\qquad r_{\max}=\max_{s',a'}r(s',a')}$$

Step 5, check: the largest entry of this vector is $r_{\max}+\tfrac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}r_{\max}$, which is $\tfrac{r_{\max}}{1-\gamma}$, the value of $C$ we used. So the vector really is a fixed point, and by part (b) it is the only one; iterating $T$ from any $U$ converges to it.

A worked example with $\gamma=\tfrac12$, so that $\tfrac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}=1$, and $r_{\max}=2$:

| $(s,a)$ | $r(s,a)$ | $U^*(s,a)$ |
|---|---|---|
| $(s_1,a_1)$ | $1$ | $3$ |
| $(s_1,a_2)$ | $0$ | $2$ |
| $(s_2,a_1)$ | $\tfrac12$ | $\tfrac52$ |
| $(s_2,a_2)$ | $2$ | $4$ |

Here $C=4$, which is $\tfrac{2}{1-1/2}$, and each entry is $r(s,a)+\tfrac12\cdot4$.

**In words.** Each pair collects its own reward once, and from the next step on it is credited as if it collected the best reward $r_{\max}$ forever, discounted.

**Sanity check.** If all rewards equal $r_0$, the formula gives $r_0+\tfrac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}r_0$, which is $\tfrac{r_0}{1-\gamma}$, the value of collecting $r_0$ forever.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Fixed point: $U^*(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma C$ with $C=\max U^*$.
- Maximum over $(s,a)$: $C=r_{\max}+\gamma C$, so $C=\tfrac{r_{\max}}{1-\gamma}$.
- Answer: $U^*(s,a)$ is $r(s,a)$ plus $\tfrac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}r_{\max}$; unique by (b).

*Verified: 3000 iterations of $T$ on 2000 random MDPs (rewards of both signs) match the closed form to $5\cdot10^{-12}$; the example table was recomputed in exact fractions.*

**Common mistakes.**

- Writing $\max f-\max g=\max(f-g)$; only the inequality through the maximizing entry holds, and it is needed in both directions.
- Answering $\tfrac{r_{\max}}{1-\gamma}$ for every pair and forgetting the local reward $r(s,a)$ of the first step.
- Reading $\max_{a',s'}U(s',a')$ as an expectation over $p(\cdot\mid s,a)$; the operator does not use $p$ at all.

**💡 Trick.** The max inside $T$ ignores its own $(s,a)$ argument, so at the fixed point $C=\max_{s',a'}U^*$ is one scalar; maximizing $U^*(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma C$ over $(s,a)$ gives $C=r_{\max}+\gamma C$, i.e. $C=\frac{r_{\max}}{1-\gamma}$.

**⚠️ Watch out.** In the contraction proof you need $|\max f-\max g|\le\max|f-g|$ (not $\max f-\max g=\max(f-g)$); the $r(s,a)$ terms cancel, so only the discounted max term is bounded by $\gamma\|U-V\|_\infty$.
