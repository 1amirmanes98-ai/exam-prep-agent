## Q1 (30 pts) — Recycling-robot MDP: policy evaluation and optimal-action proofs
**Topics:** MDP formalism, policy evaluation, optimal policy, discounted return | **Pillar:** Planning | **Difficulty:** 4
**Maps to:** lecture_02, lecture_03
**Statement (English translation):**
A vacuum-cleaning robot runs on a battery with three states: **high**, **low**, **empty**. Each step the robot chooses among 3 actions: **clean**, **wait**, **charge**. Dynamics:
- **charge**: battery → high, reward $0$.
- **wait**: battery stays in the same state, reward $R_{wait}$ (with $0<R_{wait}$).
- **clean**: reward $R_{clean}$ (with $R_{wait}<R_{clean}$), and the battery updates: from **high**, w.p. $1/3$ → low and w.p. $2/3$ stays high; from **low**, w.p. $1/2$ → empty and w.p. $1/2$ stays low.
- In state **empty** only **charge** is allowed: a human must recharge, reward $-10$, battery → high.

The battery starts in **high**. Criterion: discounted reward, $\gamma=0.9$.

(a) Write the MDP formally ($S,A,s_0,R,P$).

(b) The policy $\pi$ does **clean** in high and **charge** in low. Write $V^\pi(\text{high})$ and $V^\pi(\text{low})$ (as functions of $R_{clean}$).

(c) Prove that for all $0<R_{wait}<R_{clean}$ the optimal policy does **clean** in state high.

(d) For each pair $(R_{clean},R_{wait})$ with $0<R_{wait}<R_{clean}$, state which action the optimal policy does in **low** (clean / charge / wait), with a brief (informal) explanation: (i) $R_{clean}=1000,R_{wait}=1$; (ii) $R_{clean}=1,R_{wait}=0.99$; (iii) $R_{clean}=1,R_{wait}=0.01$.

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) The MDP formally**

**What is asked.** Write the five components $S$, $A$, $s_0$, $R$, $P$ of the battery MDP. The answer is a definition, not a number.

**Tool and why.** Modelling a story as an MDP (cheat sheet §12): the state is whatever the robot must know at decision time. Here rewards and transitions depend only on the battery level and the chosen action, so the battery level is the state. The criterion is the discounted return with $\gamma=0.9$ (cheat sheet §1).

**Definitions.** We abbreviate the states as $H$ (high), $L$ (low) and $E$ (empty), and keep the action names clean, wait and charge. The short names keep the formulas of the later parts readable. We write $A(s)$ for the set of actions allowed in state $s$, because in empty the set shrinks.

**Solution.**

Step 1, states and start: $S=\{H,L,E\}$ and $s_0=H$, because the battery starts full.

Step 2, actions: $A=\{\text{clean},\text{wait},\text{charge}\}$. In $H$ and in $L$ all three are allowed. In $E$ a human must recharge, so $A(E)=\{\text{charge}\}$.

Step 3, rewards, read off the story: clean pays $R_{clean}$ and wait pays $R_{wait}$, in $H$ and in $L$. Charge pays $0$ in $H$ and in $L$, and $-10$ in $E$, the human's fee.

Step 4, transitions, one row per outcome:

| state, action | probability | next state | reward |
|---|---|---|---|
| $H$, clean | $\tfrac23$ | $H$ | $R_{clean}$ |
| $H$, clean | $\tfrac13$ | $L$ | $R_{clean}$ |
| $L$, clean | $\tfrac12$ | $L$ | $R_{clean}$ |
| $L$, clean | $\tfrac12$ | $E$ | $R_{clean}$ |
| $H$ or $L$, wait | $1$ | same state | $R_{wait}$ |
| $H$ or $L$, charge | $1$ | $H$ | $0$ |
| $E$, charge | $1$ | $H$ | $-10$ |

Step 5, criterion: maximize $\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^t r_t\big]$ from $s_0$, with $\gamma=0.9$.

**In words.** The battery level is all the robot needs to remember. Cleaning is the only action that drains the battery, and running empty costs a fixed fee and then resets the battery to full.

**Sanity check.** For each state and action the probabilities sum to $1$: $\tfrac23+\tfrac13$ and $\tfrac12+\tfrac12$ for clean, and a single outcome otherwise. The rewards respect the story: $0<R_{wait}$ and $R_{wait}<R_{clean}$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- States $S=\{H,L,E\}$, start $s_0=H$.
- Actions clean, wait, charge in $H$ and $L$; only charge in $E$.
- Rewards: clean $R_{clean}$, wait $R_{wait}$, charge $0$, and $-10$ for charge in $E$.
- Transitions: clean moves $H$ to $L$ w.p. $\tfrac13$ and $L$ to $E$ w.p. $\tfrac12$, otherwise stays; wait stays; charge goes to $H$.
- Discount $\gamma=0.9$.

*Verified: this MDP, coded in Python with exact fractions, reproduces every value in parts (b)–(d).*

**(b) Evaluating clean-in-high, charge-in-low**

**What is asked.** The values $V^\pi(H)$ and $V^\pi(L)$ of the fixed policy $\pi$ that cleans in $H$ and charges in $L$, as multiples of $R_{clean}$. The answer is two formulas.

**Tool and why.** Bellman equation of a fixed policy (cheat sheet §2): the value of a state is the immediate reward plus $\gamma$ times the expected value of the next state. For a fixed policy this is a linear system, and here it has only two unknowns because $\pi$ never reaches $E$.

**Definitions.** No new notation: $V^\pi(s)$ is the discounted return of $\pi$ from $s$, and $H$, $L$, $E$ are from part (a).

**Solution.**

Step 1, which states are visited: from $H$, cleaning leads to $H$ or $L$; from $L$, charging leads to $H$. So the robot never enters $E$, and only $V^\pi(H)$ and $V^\pi(L)$ are needed.

Step 2, Bellman equation at $L$: charge pays $0$ and moves to $H$, so

$$V^\pi(L)=\gamma\,V^\pi(H).$$

Step 3, Bellman equation at $H$: clean pays $R_{clean}$, then the robot is in $H$ w.p. $\tfrac23$ and in $L$ w.p. $\tfrac13$, so

$$V^\pi(H)=R_{clean}+\gamma\Big(\tfrac23V^\pi(H)+\tfrac13V^\pi(L)\Big).$$

Step 4, substitute Step 2 into Step 3 and collect the $V^\pi(H)$ terms on the left:

$$\begin{aligned}
V^\pi(H)&=R_{clean}+\tfrac23\gamma V^\pi(H)+\tfrac13\gamma^2V^\pi(H),\\
V^\pi(H)\Big(1-\tfrac23\gamma-\tfrac13\gamma^2\Big)&=R_{clean}.
\end{aligned}$$

Step 5, plug in $\gamma=0.9$: $\tfrac23\cdot0.9$ is $0.6$ and $\tfrac13\cdot0.81$ is $0.27$, so the bracket is $1-0.6-0.27=0.13$, that is $\tfrac{13}{100}$. Dividing,

$$\boxed{V^\pi(H)=\tfrac{100}{13}R_{clean}\approx7.692\,R_{clean}}$$

Step 6, back to Step 2: $V^\pi(L)=0.9\cdot\tfrac{100}{13}R_{clean}$, so

$$\boxed{V^\pi(L)=\tfrac{90}{13}R_{clean}\approx6.923\,R_{clean}}$$

**In words.** In $H$ the robot earns $R_{clean}$ every step. A third of the time it drops to $L$ and spends one unpaid step charging. So it is paid on most steps, and the value is a little below the upper bound $\tfrac{R_{clean}}{1-\gamma}$, which is $10R_{clean}$.

**Sanity check.** Substitute back into Step 3 with $R_{clean}=1$:

$$\begin{aligned}
1+0.9\Big(\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac{100}{13}+\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac{90}{13}\Big)&=1+0.9\cdot\tfrac{290}{39}\\
&=1+\tfrac{87}{13}\\
&=\tfrac{100}{13}.
\end{aligned}$$

This is $V^\pi(H)$ again, as it must be.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Under $\pi$ the robot never reaches $E$, so there are two equations: $V^\pi(L)=\gamma V^\pi(H)$ and the clean equation at $H$.
- Substituting, $V^\pi(H)$ times $1-\tfrac23\gamma-\tfrac13\gamma^2$ equals $R_{clean}$, and that factor is $0.13$.
- Answer: $V^\pi(H)=\tfrac{100}{13}R_{clean}$ and $V^\pi(L)=\tfrac{90}{13}R_{clean}$.

*Verified: sympy solves the two-equation system symbolically and returns $\tfrac{100}{13}R_{clean}$ and $\tfrac{90}{13}R_{clean}$.*

**(c) Clean is optimal in high**

**What is asked.** A proof that for every pair with $0<R_{wait}$ and $R_{wait}<R_{clean}$, the optimal policy cleans in $H$. The answer is a proof.

**Tool and why.** Bellman optimality (cheat sheet §1 and §2): $V^*(s)$ is the maximum of $Q^*(s,a)$ over the actions. We compare the three Q-values at $H$: charge is beaten by wait directly, and wait is ruled out by contradiction, because assuming wait is optimal in $H$ pins $V^*(H)$ down exactly.

**Definitions.** $Q^*(s,a)$ is the immediate reward plus $\gamma$ times the expected $V^*$ of the next state. Let $\Delta$ be the advantage of clean over wait in $H$:

$$\Delta=Q^*(H,\text{clean})-Q^*(H,\text{wait}).$$

We introduce it because its sign decides between the two actions.

**Solution.**

Step 1, the three Q-values at $H$, from the table of part (a):

$$\begin{aligned}
Q^*(H,\text{clean})&=R_{clean}+\gamma\Big(\tfrac23V^*(H)+\tfrac13V^*(L)\Big),\\
Q^*(H,\text{wait})&=R_{wait}+\gamma V^*(H),\\
Q^*(H,\text{charge})&=\gamma V^*(H).
\end{aligned}$$

Step 2, charge loses to wait: both actions leave the robot in $H$, so they differ only in the immediate reward,

$$Q^*(H,\text{wait})-Q^*(H,\text{charge})=R_{wait}.$$

This is positive, so charge is never optimal in $H$.

Step 3, a formula for $\Delta$: subtract the second line of Step 1 from the first, writing $\gamma V^*(H)$ as $\gamma\big(\tfrac23V^*(H)+\tfrac13V^*(H)\big)$:

$$\begin{aligned}
\Delta&=R_{clean}-R_{wait}+\gamma\Big(\tfrac23V^*(H)+\tfrac13V^*(L)-V^*(H)\Big)\\
&=(R_{clean}-R_{wait})-\tfrac{\gamma}{3}\big(V^*(H)-V^*(L)\big).
\end{aligned}$$

Step 4, **Claim.** Wait is not optimal in $H$. The proof is by contradiction.

Suppose wait is optimal in $H$, so $V^*(H)=Q^*(H,\text{wait})$. By Step 1 this reads $V^*(H)=R_{wait}+\gamma V^*(H)$, and solving for $V^*(H)$,

$$V^*(H)=\frac{R_{wait}}{1-\gamma}.$$

Step 5, a lower bound on $V^*(L)$: the policy that waits forever in $L$ stays in $L$ and earns $R_{wait}$ every step, so its value from $L$ is $\tfrac{R_{wait}}{1-\gamma}$. The optimal value is at least the value of any policy, so by Step 4

$$V^*(L)\ge\frac{R_{wait}}{1-\gamma}=V^*(H).$$

Hence $V^*(H)-V^*(L)\le0$.

Step 6, the contradiction: in Step 3 the subtracted term is now at most $0$, so

$$\Delta\ge R_{clean}-R_{wait}>0.$$

So clean is strictly better than wait in $H$. But $V^*(H)$ is the maximum of the Q-values, which gives the impossible chain

$$V^*(H)\ge Q^*(H,\text{clean})>Q^*(H,\text{wait})=V^*(H).$$

Step 7, conclusion: wait is not optimal in $H$ (Steps 4–6) and charge is not either (Step 2). The only remaining action is clean, so the optimal policy cleans in $H$.

$$\boxed{\pi^*(H)=\text{clean}}$$

**In words.** If waiting were the best plan in $H$, the robot would just collect $R_{wait}$ forever. But it can collect the same stream from $L$, so dropping to $L$ would cost nothing. Then cleaning earns strictly more right now and loses nothing later, which contradicts waiting being best.

**Sanity check.** The closest case is part (d)(ii), with $R_{clean}=1$ and $R_{wait}=0.99$. There $V^*(H)=9.925$ and $V^*(L)=9.9$, so by Step 3 $\Delta=0.01-0.3\cdot0.025$, which is $0.0025$. Clean still wins, by a small but positive margin.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Charge and wait both stay in $H$, so wait beats charge by $R_{wait}$, which is positive.
- Compute the advantage of clean over wait in $H$:

$$\Delta=(R_{clean}-R_{wait})-\tfrac{\gamma}{3}\big(V^*(H)-V^*(L)\big).$$
- If wait were optimal in $H$, then $V^*(H)=\tfrac{R_{wait}}{1-\gamma}$, while waiting forever in $L$ gives $V^*(L)\ge\tfrac{R_{wait}}{1-\gamma}$.
- Then $\Delta\ge R_{clean}-R_{wait}$, which is positive: a contradiction. So clean is optimal in $H$.

*Verified: value iteration on a grid of 1600 pairs ($R_{clean}$ from $0.01$ to $1000$, ratio $R_{wait}/R_{clean}$ from $0.01$ to $0.99$) found clean strictly best in $H$ every time.*

**(d) The optimal action in low**

**What is asked.** For three reward pairs, which action the optimal policy takes in $L$, with a short explanation. The answer is one action per case.

**Tool and why.** Guess and verify with Bellman optimality (cheat sheet §2 and §3). By part (c) the robot cleans in $H$, so only the action in $L$ is open, which leaves three candidate policies. For each case we guess one, evaluate it exactly, and check that no single action improves on it in any state; a policy whose values pass this test is optimal.

**Definitions.** For a candidate policy let $V_H$, $V_L$, $V_E$ be its values, and let $Q(L,a)$ be the one-step lookahead from $L$ computed with those values:

$$\begin{aligned}
Q(L,\text{clean})&=R_{clean}+\gamma\big(\tfrac12V_L+\tfrac12V_E\big),\\
Q(L,\text{wait})&=R_{wait}+\gamma V_L,\\
Q(L,\text{charge})&=\gamma V_H.
\end{aligned}$$

In every case $V_E=-10+\gamma V_H$, because $E$ has the single action charge.

**Solution.**

Step 1, case (iii), $R_{clean}=1$ and $R_{wait}=0.01$; guess charge. This is exactly the policy of part (b), so $V_H=\tfrac{100}{13}$ and $V_L=\tfrac{90}{13}$. Then $V_E=-10+\tfrac{90}{13}$, which is $-\tfrac{40}{13}$. The three lookaheads:

$$\begin{aligned}
Q(L,\text{clean})&=1+0.9\cdot\tfrac12\Big(\tfrac{90}{13}-\tfrac{40}{13}\Big)=\tfrac{71}{26}\approx2.731,\\
Q(L,\text{wait})&=0.01+0.9\cdot\tfrac{90}{13}\approx6.241,\\
Q(L,\text{charge})&=0.9\cdot\tfrac{100}{13}=\tfrac{90}{13}\approx6.923.
\end{aligned}$$

Charge is the largest, so the guess is consistent.

Step 2, case (ii), $R_{clean}=1$ and $R_{wait}=0.99$; guess wait. Waiting forever in $L$ gives $V_L=\tfrac{0.99}{0.1}$, which is $9.9$. At $H$ the robot cleans:

$$\begin{aligned}
V_H&=1+0.9\Big(\tfrac23V_H+\tfrac13\cdot9.9\Big)=1+0.6V_H+2.97,\\
0.4V_H&=3.97,\\
V_H&=9.925.
\end{aligned}$$

Then $V_E=-10+0.9\cdot9.925$, which is $-1.0675$, and

$$\begin{aligned}
Q(L,\text{clean})&=1+0.9\big(4.95-0.53375\big)\approx4.975,\\
Q(L,\text{wait})&=0.99+0.9\cdot9.9=9.9,\\
Q(L,\text{charge})&=0.9\cdot9.925=8.9325.
\end{aligned}$$

Wait is the largest, so the guess is consistent.

Step 3, case (i), $R_{clean}=1000$ and $R_{wait}=1$; guess clean. Now all three states are visited, so we solve three equations:

$$\begin{aligned}
V_H&=1000+0.6V_H+0.3V_L,\\
V_L&=1000+0.45V_L+0.45V_E,\\
V_E&=-10+0.9V_H.
\end{aligned}$$

Substituting $V_E$ into the second line gives $0.55V_L=995.5+0.405V_H$. The first line gives $0.3V_L=0.4V_H-1000$. Write the latter as $V_L=\tfrac{0.4V_H-1000}{0.3}$, substitute it into the former and multiply both sides by $0.3$:

$$\begin{aligned}
0.22V_H-550&=298.65+0.1215V_H,\\
0.0985V_H&=848.65,\\
V_H&=\tfrac{1697300}{197}\approx8615.7.
\end{aligned}$$

Back-substituting, $V_L=\tfrac{1606400}{197}$, about $8154.3$, and $V_E$ is about $7744.2$. The lookaheads from $L$ are $Q(L,\text{clean})=V_L$, about $8154.3$, then $Q(L,\text{wait})=1+0.9V_L$, about $7339.9$, and $Q(L,\text{charge})=0.9V_H$, about $7754.2$. Clean is the largest, so the guess is consistent.

Step 4, the check at $H$: in all three cases clean also gives the largest lookahead at $H$ (for example in case (ii): $9.925$ against $9.9225$ for wait and $8.9325$ for charge), as part (c) guarantees. $E$ has one action. So each guessed policy satisfies the Bellman optimality equation in every state and is optimal.

Summary of the lookaheads from $L$:

| case | clean | wait | charge |
|---|---|---|---|
| (i) | $8154.3$ | $7339.9$ | $7754.2$ |
| (ii) | $4.975$ | $9.9$ | $8.933$ |
| (iii) | $2.731$ | $6.241$ | $6.923$ |

$$\boxed{\text{(i) clean, (ii) wait, (iii) charge}}$$

**In words.** Cleaning from $L$ risks a $\tfrac12$ chance of the $-10$ fee each step. (i) A cleaning reward of $1000$ dwarfs the fee, and after running empty the robot is back in $H$ anyway, so the risk is worth taking. (ii) Waiting pays almost as much as cleaning with no risk and no unpaid step, so the robot sits in $L$ collecting $0.99$. (iii) Waiting pays almost nothing and cleaning is risky, so the robot gives up one step (reward $0$) to charge back to $H$, where cleaning is safe for a while. The answer is not monotone in the ratio $R_{clean}/R_{wait}$: the ratios $1000$, about $1.01$ and $100$ give clean, wait and charge.

**Sanity check.** In case (iii) the optimal policy is exactly the policy of part (b), so $V^*(H)=\tfrac{100}{13}$ agrees with (b). In case (ii) $V^*(H)=9.925$ lies between $V^*(L)=9.9$ and the bound $\tfrac{R_{clean}}{1-\gamma}=10$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- By (c) the robot cleans in $H$; compare the three choices in $L$ with one-step lookaheads.
- Case (i) clean: the $-10$ fee is negligible next to $R_{clean}=1000$; $Q(L,\text{clean})\approx8154$ is the largest.
- Case (ii) wait: $R_{wait}$ is almost $R_{clean}$ and waiting is riskless; $Q(L,\text{wait})=9.9$ is the largest.
- Case (iii) charge: waiting pays almost nothing and cleaning risks the fee; $Q(L,\text{charge})=\tfrac{90}{13}$ is the largest.

*Verified: for each pair all nine deterministic policies were evaluated exactly with sympy; only the stated policy satisfies Bellman optimality, and the table values come from that run.*

**Common mistakes.**

- In (b), adding the $-10$ fee or a $V^\pi(E)$ equation: under $\pi$ the robot never reaches $E$, and charging from $L$ pays $0$.
- In (c), comparing only clean with wait and forgetting charge, or asserting $V^*(L)\le V^*(H)$ without proof; the proof needs the opposite bound, obtained from waiting forever in $L$.
- In (d), deciding by comparing $R_{clean}$ with $R_{wait}$ alone, ignoring the risk of the fee and the discounted future values.

**💡 Trick.** For (c), argue by contradiction: if wait were optimal at high then $V^*(\text{high})=\frac{R_{wait}}{1-\gamma}$, but waiting forever from low forces $V^*(\text{low})\ge V^*(\text{high})$, so the advantage of clean over wait at high is at least $R_{clean}-R_{wait}$, which is positive — so clean wins.

**⚠️ Watch out.** In (d) the action in **low** is not monotone in a single parameter: across cases (i)–(iii) it goes clean, wait, charge, while the ratio $R_{clean}/R_{wait}$ goes $1000$, about $1.01$, $100$, against the $-10$ empty penalty. You must weigh the risk of reaching empty, not just compare $R_{clean}$ vs $R_{wait}$.

## Q2 (30 pts) — Value functions as a function of the reward: linearity vs. optimality
**Topics:** value function linearity in reward, optimal value monotonicity, subadditivity of $V^*$, policy stability | **Pillar:** Planning | **Difficulty:** 4
**Maps to:** lecture_02, lecture_03
**Statement (English translation):**
Fix $S,A,s_0,p$. For a reward $r$, let $M_r=(S,A,p,s_0,r)$; $V_r^\pi(s)$ is the discounted return of $\pi$ from $s$ in $M_r$, and $V_r^*(s)$ that of the optimal policy. All rewards lie in $[0,1]$; $\gamma=0.9$. Given two different rewards $r_1,r_2$ and $r_3=r_1+r_2$. For each part determine the relation between the two expressions, from: **= (always equal)**, **≥ (first always ≥, and sometimes strictly >)**, **≤ (first always ≤, and sometimes strictly <)**, or **cannot be determined (sometimes >, sometimes <)**.

(a) $V_{r_3}^\pi$ vs $V_{r_1}^\pi+V_{r_2}^\pi$, for every policy $\pi$.

(b) $V_{r_3}^*$ vs $V_{r_1}^*+V_{r_2}^*$.

(c) $V_{r_3}^*$ vs $V_{r_1}^*$ (Hint: rewards are non-negative).

(d) Let $\pi_i^*$ be optimal for $r_i$. If $\pi_1^*(s)=\pi_2^*(s)=a$, must $\pi_3^*(s)=a$? Yes/No.

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) A fixed policy: $=$**

**What is asked.** The relation between $V_{r_3}^\pi$ and $V_{r_1}^\pi+V_{r_2}^\pi$ for a fixed policy $\pi$, chosen from the four options. The answer is a verdict with a proof.

**Tool and why.** Definition of $V^\pi$ as an expected discounted sum (cheat sheet §1), plus linearity of expectation. A fixed policy fixes the law of the trajectory, and that law does not depend on the reward, so the reward enters linearly.

**Definitions.** For a trajectory $s_0,a_0,s_1,a_1,\dots$ let $G_r=\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^t r(s_t,a_t)$ be its discounted return under reward $r$. We introduce it because $V_r^\pi(s)=\mathbb{E}^\pi[G_r]$, where the expectation is over trajectories of $\pi$ from $s$ under $p$.

**Solution.**

Verdict: **$=$ (always equal).**

Step 1, one trajectory: since $r_3=r_1+r_2$, every term of the sum splits, and both series converge because rewards are bounded and $\gamma<1$:

$$G_{r_3}=\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^t\big(r_1+r_2\big)(s_t,a_t)=G_{r_1}+G_{r_2}.$$

Step 2, take expectations: the trajectory law depends on $p$ and $\pi$ only, so it is the same for all three rewards. By linearity of expectation,

$$\begin{aligned}
V_{r_3}^\pi(s)&=\mathbb{E}^\pi\big[G_{r_1}+G_{r_2}\big]\\
&=V_{r_1}^\pi(s)+V_{r_2}^\pi(s).
\end{aligned}$$

$$\boxed{V_{r_3}^\pi=V_{r_1}^\pi+V_{r_2}^\pi}$$

**In words.** The policy decides where the agent goes; the reward only decides how much each visit pays. Paying $r_1$ and $r_2$ at every visit is the same as paying their sum.

**Sanity check.** In matrix form $V_r^\pi=(I-\gamma P^\pi)^{-1}r^\pi$, which is linear in the reward vector $r^\pi$. With $r_2\equiv0$ the identity is trivial.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Verdict: $=$.
- Reason: $V_r^\pi=\mathbb{E}^\pi[G_r]$, and the trajectory law does not depend on $r$.
- Linearity of expectation gives $V_{r_1+r_2}^\pi=V_{r_1}^\pi+V_{r_2}^\pi$.

*Verified: on 2000 random MDPs (2–4 states, 2–3 actions) with random policies, $(I-\gamma P^\pi)^{-1}r_3^\pi$ matched the sum of the two evaluations to $10^{-9}$.*

**(b) Optimal values: $\le$**

**What is asked.** The relation between $V_{r_3}^*$ and $V_{r_1}^*+V_{r_2}^*$. The answer is a verdict, with a proof of the inequality and examples showing both equality and strict inequality occur.

**Tool and why.** $V^*$ is the value of an optimal policy and is at least the value of every policy (cheat sheet §1). Combined with part (a), this is the fact that a maximum of a sum is at most the sum of the maxima.

**Definitions.** Let $\pi_3^*$ be an optimal policy for $r_3$; a stationary deterministic one that is optimal from every state exists. We need it to write $V_{r_3}^*$ as the value of one concrete policy.

**Solution.**

Verdict: **$\le$ (always at most, sometimes strictly less).**

Step 1, apply part (a) to $\pi_3^*$: $V_{r_3}^*(s)=V_{r_3}^{\pi_3^*}(s)$, and by (a) this splits into the $r_1$-value plus the $r_2$-value of $\pi_3^*$.

Step 2, bound each term by its optimum: $V_{r_1}^{\pi_3^*}(s)\le V_{r_1}^*(s)$ and $V_{r_2}^{\pi_3^*}(s)\le V_{r_2}^*(s)$, by the definition of $V^*$. Adding,

$$\begin{aligned}
V_{r_3}^*(s)&=V_{r_1}^{\pi_3^*}(s)+V_{r_2}^{\pi_3^*}(s)\\
&\le V_{r_1}^*(s)+V_{r_2}^*(s).
\end{aligned}$$

Step 3, strict inequality happens: one state $s$ with a self-loop and two actions $a$, $b$. Let $r_1(s,a)=1$, $r_1(s,b)=0$, and $r_2$ the reverse. Each reward alone is best served by repeating its action forever, so $V_{r_1}^*(s)=10$ and $V_{r_2}^*(s)=10$. But $r_3\equiv1$, so every policy gets $V_{r_3}^*(s)=10$, which is less than $20$.

Step 4, equality also happens: take any $r_1$ that is not identically $0$ and $r_2=\tfrac12r_1$. Multiplying a reward by a positive constant multiplies every policy's value by it, so an $r_1$-optimal policy is optimal for $r_2$ and $r_3$ as well, and $V_{r_3}^*=\tfrac32V_{r_1}^*$, which equals $V_{r_1}^*+V_{r_2}^*$.

Step 5, exactly when it is strict: equality holds at $s$ exactly when one policy is optimal from $s$ for both $r_1$ and $r_2$. If such a $\pi$ exists, then $V_{r_3}^*(s)\ge V_{r_3}^\pi(s)$, and by (a) the right side is $V_{r_1}^*(s)+V_{r_2}^*(s)$; with Step 2 this is equality. Conversely, equality in Step 2 means $\pi_3^*$ is optimal for both.

$$\boxed{V_{r_3}^*\le V_{r_1}^*+V_{r_2}^*}$$

**In words.** With $r_3$ one policy must serve both rewards at once, while separately each reward gets its own best policy. A compromise can only lose, and it loses nothing only when the two rewards agree on what to do.

**Sanity check.** In Step 3 the two rewards pull toward different actions and half of the total is lost; in Step 4 they agree and nothing is lost, as Step 5 predicts.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Verdict: $\le$.
- Proof: $V_{r_3}^*=V_{r_1}^{\pi_3^*}+V_{r_2}^{\pi_3^*}$ by (a), and each term is at most the optimum for its own reward.
- Strict example: one state, $r_1$ pays action $a$ and $r_2$ pays action $b$; then $10$ against $20$.

*Verified: value iteration on 2000 random MDPs gave $V_{r_3}^*\le V_{r_1}^*+V_{r_2}^*$ in every state; the one-state examples give $10$ against $20$ (strict) and $15$ against $15$ (equal).*

**(c) Adding a non-negative reward: $\ge$**

**What is asked.** The relation between $V_{r_3}^*$ and $V_{r_1}^*$, using that rewards are non-negative. The answer is a verdict with a proof and examples.

**Tool and why.** Monotonicity of the optimal value in the reward (cheat sheet §1), which follows from part (a): adding a non-negative reward adds a non-negative value to every policy.

**Definitions.** Let $\pi_1^*$ be an optimal policy for $r_1$. We use it as a policy that is still available under $r_3$.

**Solution.**

Verdict: **$\ge$ (always at least, sometimes strictly more).**

Step 1, $r_2$-values are non-negative: since $r_2\ge0$, every $G_{r_2}$ is a sum of non-negative terms, so $V_{r_2}^\pi(s)\ge0$ for every policy $\pi$.

Step 2, every policy gains: by (a) and Step 1, $V_{r_3}^\pi(s)$ equals $V_{r_1}^\pi(s)+V_{r_2}^\pi(s)$, which is at least $V_{r_1}^\pi(s)$.

Step 3, apply this to $\pi_1^*$; the first row is the definition of $V^*$:

$$\begin{aligned}
V_{r_3}^*(s)&\ge V_{r_3}^{\pi_1^*}(s)\\
&\ge V_{r_1}^{\pi_1^*}(s)\\
&=V_{r_1}^*(s).
\end{aligned}$$

Step 4, strict inequality happens: take $r_1\equiv0$ and $r_2\equiv\tfrac12$. Then $V_{r_1}^*=0$, while $V_{r_3}^*=\tfrac{1/2}{1-\gamma}$, which is $5$.

Step 5, equality also happens: one state with a self-loop and actions $a$, $b$; $r_1(s,a)=1$, $r_1(s,b)=0$, $r_2(s,a)=0$, $r_2(s,b)=\tfrac12$. Then $V_{r_1}^*(s)=10$. Under $r_3$ action $a$ pays $1$ and $b$ pays $\tfrac12$, so still $V_{r_3}^*(s)=10$. Equality holds although $r_2$ is positive at the reachable state $s$: the extra reward sits on an action the optimal policy does not take. A sufficient condition for strictness is that some $r_1$-optimal policy collects positive $r_2$ with positive probability from $s$, because then Step 3's second row is strict.

$$\boxed{V_{r_3}^*\ge V_{r_1}^*}$$

**In words.** Adding a non-negative reward cannot hurt: the old optimal policy is still available and earns at least as much as before.

**Sanity check.** The sign assumption is exactly what is used: with $r_1\equiv0$ and a negative $r_2\equiv-\tfrac12$ we would get $V_{r_3}^*=-5$, below $V_{r_1}^*=0$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Verdict: $\ge$.
- Proof: $r_2\ge0$ gives $V_{r_2}^\pi\ge0$, so $V_{r_3}^\pi\ge V_{r_1}^\pi$ for every $\pi$ by (a).
- Take $\pi=\pi_1^*$: then $V_{r_3}^*\ge V_{r_3}^{\pi_1^*}$, and this is at least $V_{r_1}^*$.
- Strict example: $r_1\equiv0$, $r_2\equiv\tfrac12$ gives $0$ against $5$.

*Verified: value iteration on 2000 random MDPs gave $V_{r_3}^*\ge V_{r_1}^*$ everywhere; the examples give $0$ against $5$ (strict) and $10$ against $10$ (equal).*

**(d) A shared optimal action need not survive: No**

**What is asked.** Whether $\pi_1^*(s)=a$ together with $\pi_2^*(s)=a$ forces $\pi_3^*(s)=a$. The answer is yes or no; for no, a counterexample with all numbers.

**Tool and why.** A counterexample built from Q-values (cheat sheet §1 and §2). By (b) $V^*$ is not additive, so $Q^*_{r_3}$ is not the sum of $Q^*_{r_1}$ and $Q^*_{r_2}$, and the argmax can move. For that we need a later choice that $r_1$ and $r_2$ resolve differently.

**Definitions.** States $s$, $X$, $Y$, $A_1$, $A_2$, $B$, all transitions deterministic and $\gamma=0.9$. From $s$, action $a$ goes to $X$ and action $b$ goes to $Y$. From $X$ one action goes to $A_1$ and the other to $A_2$. From $Y$ every action goes to $B$. These moves pay $0$. The states $A_1$, $A_2$, $B$ are absorbing and pay on every step: $A_1$ pays $r_1=1$ and $r_2=0$; $A_2$ pays $r_1=0$ and $r_2=1$; $B$ pays $0.9$ under both $r_1$ and $r_2$. An absorbing state paying $c$ per step is worth $\tfrac{c}{1-\gamma}$, which is $10c$.

**Solution.**

Verdict: **No.**

Step 1, under $r_1$: $V(A_1)=10$, so $V(X)=0.9\cdot10$, which is $9$, and $Q(s,a)=0.9\cdot9$, which is $8.1$. On the other side $V(B)=9$, $V(Y)=8.1$ and $Q(s,b)=7.29$. So $a$ is the unique optimal action: $\pi_1^*(s)=a$.

Step 2, under $r_2$: the same numbers, with $X$ now choosing $A_2$. So $\pi_2^*(s)=a$.

Step 3, under $r_3$: $A_1$ and $A_2$ each pay $1$, but $X$ can enter only one of them, so $V(X)=9$ and $Q(s,a)=8.1$ again. State $B$ now pays $1.8$, so $V(B)=18$, $V(Y)=16.2$ and $Q(s,b)=14.58$. So $\pi_3^*(s)=b$.

| reward | $Q(s,a)$ | $Q(s,b)$ | best |
|---|---|---|---|
| $r_1$ | $8.1$ | $7.29$ | $a$ |
| $r_2$ | $8.1$ | $7.29$ | $a$ |
| $r_3$ | $8.1$ | $14.58$ | $b$ |

$$\boxed{\text{No: }\pi_3^*(s)=b\ne a}$$

Step 4, the range of rewards: $r_1$ and $r_2$ lie in $[0,1]$. If $r_3$ must lie in $[0,1]$ as well, halve every reward: all Q-values halve ($4.05$, $3.645$, $7.29$) and every argmax stays the same.

**In words.** At $X$ the agent must commit to $A_1$ or $A_2$, so it can never collect both rewards there; $B$ pays both at once. Under $r_1$ or $r_2$ alone the better branch of $X$ beats $B$, but under the sum $B$ doubles while $X$ does not.

**Sanity check.** The numbers agree with (b) and (c): $V_{r_3}^*(s)=14.58$ is at most $V_{r_1}^*(s)+V_{r_2}^*(s)=16.2$ and at least $V_{r_1}^*(s)=8.1$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Verdict: no, because $V^*$ is not additive in the reward.
- Example: $s$ chooses between $X$ (then one of $A_1$, $A_2$) and $Y$ (then $B$); $A_1$ pays $r_1=1$, $A_2$ pays $r_2=1$, $B$ pays $0.9$ under both.
- Under $r_1$ or $r_2$: $Q(s,a)=8.1$ beats $Q(s,b)=7.29$.
- Under $r_3$: $Q(s,b)=14.58$ beats $Q(s,a)=8.1$.

*Verified: value iteration on the 6-state MDP reproduces the table, and halving all rewards keeps every argmax.*

**Common mistakes.**

- Treating $V^*$ as linear in the reward, like $V^\pi$, and answering $=$ in (b).
- In (c), forgetting that the proof needs $r_2\ge0$; with negative rewards the inequality fails.
- In (d), answering yes because "$Q_{r_3}=Q_{r_1}+Q_{r_2}$": that holds for a fixed policy's $Q^\pi$, not for $Q^*$.

**💡 Trick.** Everything follows from one distinction: for a *fixed* $\pi$ the return is linear in $r$ (so (a) is exact equality), but $V^*=\max_\pi$ is only sub-additive (b, gives $\le$) and monotone in non-negative reward (c, gives $\ge$).

**⚠️ Watch out.** Don't assume shared optimal actions survive addition (d): because $V^*$ is not additive, $\pi_1^*(s)=a$ together with $\pi_2^*(s)=a$ does **not** force $\pi_3^*(s)=a$ — a single reachable state paying under $r_3$ can flip the argmax.

## Q3 (20 pts) — Logistic (sigmoid) two-action policy: score function and REINFORCE
**Topics:** parametric policy, sigmoid policy, score function, REINFORCE | **Pillar:** Approximation | **Difficulty:** 3
**Maps to:** lecture_09, lecture_10
**Statement (English translation):**
An MDP with two actions $A=\{0,1\}$. Each state $s$ has a feature vector $\phi(s)\in\mathbb{R}^d$ ($S$ finite); a policy is parameterized by $\theta\in\mathbb{R}^d$ with
$$\pi(a=1\mid s;\theta)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\phi(s)\cdot\theta)}.$$

(a) Can this parameterization represent the policy that in every state picks each action w.p. $1/2$? (yes/no; give $\theta$ or a counterexample).

(b) Compute $\nabla_\theta\log\pi(a=1\mid s;\theta)$ and $\nabla_\theta\log\pi(a=0\mid s;\theta)$ (explain).

(c) Write the REINFORCE update at step $t$ as a function of a given trajectory $s_0,a_0,r_0,\dots,s_T,a_T,r_T$ (auxiliary variables allowed).

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) The uniform policy: Yes**

**What is asked.** Whether some $\theta$ makes the policy pick each action with probability $\tfrac12$ in every state. The answer is yes with a $\theta$, or no with a counterexample.

**Tool and why.** Direct substitution into the sigmoid parameterization (cheat sheet §9). The policy depends on $\theta$ only through the logit $\phi(s)\cdot\theta$, so we look for a $\theta$ that makes every logit equal to the value that gives $\tfrac12$.

**Definitions.** Let $\sigma(z)=\tfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$ be the sigmoid, so $\pi(1\mid s;\theta)=\sigma(z(s))$ with the logit $z(s)=\phi(s)\cdot\theta$.

**Solution.**

Verdict: **Yes.**

Step 1, which logit gives $\tfrac12$: $\sigma(z)=\tfrac12$ means $1+e^{-z}=2$, so $e^{-z}=1$ and $z=0$.

Step 2, choose $\theta=\mathbf{0}$: then $z(s)=\phi(s)\cdot\mathbf{0}$, which is $0$ for every state, whatever the features are. So

$$\pi(1\mid s;\mathbf{0})=\frac{1}{1+e^{0}}=\frac12,$$

and $\pi(0\mid s;\mathbf{0})=\tfrac12$ as well.

$$\boxed{\text{Yes: }\theta=\mathbf{0}}$$

**In words.** A zero logit means no preference between the two actions, and $\theta=\mathbf{0}$ makes every logit zero at once.

**Sanity check.** The construction does not use the features at all, so it works even if all states share the same $\phi(s)$ or if the features are linearly dependent.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Yes: take $\theta=\mathbf{0}$.
- Then $\phi(s)\cdot\theta=0$ for every $s$, so $\pi(1\mid s)=\tfrac{1}{1+e^0}$, which is $\tfrac12$.

*Verified: numpy with random features in $\mathbb{R}^3$ gives $\sigma(\phi(s)\cdot\mathbf{0})=0.5$ in every state.*

**(b) The score function**

**What is asked.** The gradients $\nabla_\theta\log\pi(1\mid s;\theta)$ and $\nabla_\theta\log\pi(0\mid s;\theta)$, with an explanation. The answer is two formulas.

**Tool and why.** Chain rule plus the sigmoid identity $\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)$ (cheat sheet §9, score table). The policy is a function of the scalar logit, and the logit is linear in $\theta$, so the gradient is a scalar derivative times $\phi(s)$.

**Definitions.** Let $z=\phi(s)\cdot\theta$ and $p=\sigma(z)$, the probability of action $1$. The gradient of the logit is $\nabla_\theta z=\phi(s)$.

**Solution.**

Step 1, the derivative of the sigmoid: write $\sigma(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}$ and differentiate:

$$\begin{aligned}
\sigma'(z)&=\frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2}\\
&=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\cdot\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}\\
&=\sigma(z)\big(1-\sigma(z)\big).
\end{aligned}$$

The last row uses $\tfrac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}=1-\sigma(z)$.

Step 2, action $1$: $\log\pi(1\mid s;\theta)=\log\sigma(z)$. By the chain rule and Step 1,

$$\nabla_\theta\log\sigma(z)=\frac{\sigma'(z)}{\sigma(z)}\,\nabla_\theta z=(1-p)\,\phi(s).$$

Step 3, action $0$: $\log\pi(0\mid s;\theta)=\log\big(1-\sigma(z)\big)$. By the chain rule and Step 1,

$$\nabla_\theta\log\big(1-\sigma(z)\big)=\frac{-\sigma'(z)}{1-\sigma(z)}\,\nabla_\theta z=-p\,\phi(s).$$

Step 4, one formula for both: for $a=1$ the factor $a-p$ is $1-p$, and for $a=0$ it is $-p$. So

$$\boxed{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(a\mid s;\theta)=(a-p)\,\phi(s)}$$

**In words.** The score pushes the logit toward the action that was taken, scaled by how surprising that action was: a likely action ($p$ near $1$ for $a=1$) gets only a small push.

**Sanity check.** Every score function has mean zero under the policy. Here the mean is $p(1-p)\phi(s)+(1-p)(-p)\phi(s)$, which is $0$.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Let $p=\sigma(\phi(s)\cdot\theta)$; use $\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)$ and $\nabla_\theta(\phi(s)\cdot\theta)=\phi(s)$.
- Action $1$: $\nabla_\theta\log\pi=(1-p)\phi(s)$.
- Action $0$: $\nabla_\theta\log\pi=-p\,\phi(s)$.
- Together: $(a-p)\phi(s)$.

*Verified: central finite differences at a random $\theta$ and $\phi(s)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ match $(a-p)\phi(s)$ for both actions to within $10^{-10}$.*

**(c) The REINFORCE update**

**What is asked.** The REINFORCE update of $\theta$ at step $t$, given a sampled trajectory $s_0,a_0,r_0,\dots,s_T,a_T,r_T$. The answer is an update formula, with auxiliary variables allowed.

**Tool and why.** REINFORCE (cheat sheet §9): by the policy-gradient theorem, the gradient of the discounted objective is

$$\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_t\gamma^tG_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi(a_t\mid s_t;\theta)\Big],$$

so one sampled trajectory gives an unbiased estimate. We plug in the score from part (b).

**Definitions.** The return-to-go $G_t=\sum_{k=t}^{T}\gamma^{k-t}r_k$ is the discounted reward collected from step $t$ on. Let $p_t=\sigma(\phi(s_t)\cdot\theta)$, the probability of action $1$ at step $t$, and let $\eta_t$ be the step size.

**Solution.**

Step 1, the auxiliary returns: compute them backward, $G_T=r_T$ and then $G_t=r_t+\gamma G_{t+1}$ for $t$ from $T-1$ down to $0$.

Step 2, the score at step $t$: by part (b) it is $(a_t-p_t)\phi(s_t)$.

Step 3, the update at step $t$: the step-$t$ term of the gradient estimate is $\gamma^tG_t$ times the score, so

$$\boxed{\theta\leftarrow\theta+\eta_t\,\gamma^{t}\,G_t\,(a_t-p_t)\,\phi(s_t)}$$

The factor $\gamma^t$ is there because in the discounted objective the decision at time $t$ counts with weight $\gamma^t$.

Step 4, the whole-trajectory form: compute every $p_t$ with the same $\theta$ (the one that generated the trajectory) and apply the sum at once,

$$\theta\leftarrow\theta+\eta\sum_{t}\gamma^t G_t(a_t-p_t)\phi(s_t).$$

**In words.** If the return after taking $a_t$ is large, the update makes $a_t$ more likely in $s_t$: for $a_t=1$ it moves $\theta$ along $+\phi(s_t)$, raising the logit, and for $a_t=0$ along $-\phi(s_t)$, lowering it.

**Sanity check.** If every reward is $0$, then every $G_t$ is $0$ and $\theta$ does not move, as it should. On average the update points along the true gradient (see the verification).

**What to write in the exam.**

- Auxiliary variables: $G_t=\sum_{k=t}^{T}\gamma^{k-t}r_k$ and $p_t=\sigma(\phi(s_t)\cdot\theta)$.
- Score from (b): $(a_t-p_t)\phi(s_t)$.
- Update at step $t$:

$$\theta\leftarrow\theta+\eta_t\gamma^tG_t(a_t-p_t)\phi(s_t).$$

*Verified: on a 2-state MDP with 3 steps, the average of $\sum_t\gamma^tG_t(a_t-p_t)\phi(s_t)$ over $4\cdot10^5$ simulated episodes matched the finite-difference gradient of the exact objective within 2 standard errors ($0.8471$ vs $0.8463$ and $-0.3778$ vs $-0.3788$).*

**Common mistakes.**

- Writing $(1-p_t)$ for both actions in the update; for $a_t=0$ the score is $-p_t\phi(s_t)$.
- Dropping the chain-rule factor $\phi(s)$, or writing $\sigma'$ as $\sigma^2$.
- Using the full return $G_0$ at every step instead of the return-to-go $G_t$ (higher variance, and not the requested form), or dropping $\gamma^t$.

**💡 Trick.** Using $\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)$, the score collapses to the single compact form $\nabla_\theta\log\pi(a\mid s;\theta)=(a-p)\,\phi(s)$ with $p=\sigma(\phi(s)\cdot\theta)$ — the "observed minus expected action" times the feature.

**⚠️ Watch out.** The sign of $(a-p)$ flips between $a=1$ and $a=0$. Write $(a_t-p_t)$, not $(1-p_t)$, in the REINFORCE update, and don't drop the $\gamma^t$ discount weighting each step.

## Q4 (20 pts) — "Max-outside-expectation" operator: contraction, and does its fixed point equal Q*?
**Topics:** contraction mapping, sup-norm, Bellman operator, Q*, max–expectation interchange | **Pillar:** Planning | **Difficulty:** 4
**Maps to:** lecture_03, lecture_02
**Statement (English translation):**
MDP $M=(S,A,p,s_0,r)$. For $U\in\mathbb{R}^{|S\times A|}$, $0<\gamma<1$, define
$$(T_M U)(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\max_{a'}\ \mathbb{E}_{s'\sim p(\cdot\mid s,a)}\big[U(s',a')\big].$$

(a) State the definition of $H$ being $\gamma$-contracting in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$.

(b) Prove $T_M$ is $\gamma$-contracting in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$.

(c) Let $U^*$ be the fixed point of $T_M$. Does $U^*=Q^*$ hold for every MDP $M$? (yes/no; explain or give a counterexample).

**Solution sketch:**

**(a) Definition of a γ-contraction**

**What is asked.** The definition of an operator $H$ being a $\gamma$-contraction in the sup norm. The answer is a definition.

**Tool and why.** Contractions and fixed points (cheat sheet §6): the definition is what Banach's fixed-point theorem needs to give a unique fixed point and convergence of iteration.

**Definitions.** For $U\in\mathbb{R}^{|S\times A|}$ the sup norm is $\|U\|_\infty=\max_{s,a}|U(s,a)|$, the largest entry in absolute value.

**Solution.**

Step 1, the setting: $H$ maps $\mathbb{R}^{|S\times A|}$ to itself and $\gamma\in[0,1)$; here $\gamma$ is the discount in the statement.

Step 2, the condition: $H$ is a $\gamma$-contraction in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$ if, for all $U$ and $V$,

$$\boxed{\|HU-HV\|_\infty\le\gamma\|U-V\|_\infty}$$

**In words.** Applying $H$ shrinks the largest entrywise gap between any two functions by at least the factor $\gamma$, so repeated application pulls everything toward one fixed point.

**Sanity check.** The standard Bellman optimality operator on Q-functions satisfies this with the discount $\gamma$; part (b) shows the operator of this question does too.

**What to write in the exam.**

- For all $U,V$: $\|HU-HV\|_\infty\le\gamma\|U-V\|_\infty$, with $\gamma\in[0,1)$.
- Here $\|U\|_\infty=\max_{s,a}|U(s,a)|$.

*Verified: the definition is the ratio test used in part (b), computed on random pairs.*

**(b) $T_M$ is a γ-contraction**

**What is asked.** A proof that $T_M$ satisfies the definition of part (a). The answer is a proof.

**Tool and why.** Two non-expansive operations (cheat sheet §6): a maximum moves by at most the largest change of its arguments, and an expectation moves by at most the largest change of the variable. The only shrinking comes from the factor $\gamma$.

**Definitions.** Fix $(s,a)$ and let $s'\sim p(\cdot\mid s,a)$. For each action $a'$ define $f(a')=\mathbb{E}_{s'}[U(s',a')]$ and $g(a')=\mathbb{E}_{s'}[V(s',a')]$. Then

$$(T_MU)(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\max_{a'}f(a').$$

We introduce $f$ and $g$ to isolate the maximum.

**Solution.**

Step 1, the reward cancels:

$$(T_MU-T_MV)(s,a)=\gamma\big(\max_{a'}f-\max_{a'}g\big).$$

Step 2, **Claim.** The two maxima differ by at most the largest gap:

$$\big|\max_{a'}f(a')-\max_{a'}g(a')\big|\le\max_{a'}|f(a')-g(a')|.$$

Let $a_1$ be an action attaining $\max f$. Then

$$\begin{aligned}
\max_{a'}f(a')&=g(a_1)+\big(f(a_1)-g(a_1)\big)\\
&\le\max_{a'}g(a')+\max_{a'}|f(a')-g(a')|.
\end{aligned}$$

So $\max f-\max g$ is at most $\max|f-g|$. Swapping the roles of $f$ and $g$ gives the same bound for $\max g-\max f$, and the two together are the claim.

Step 3, each difference: by linearity of expectation,

$$f(a')-g(a')=\mathbb{E}_{s'}[U(s',a')-V(s',a')].$$

The absolute value of an expectation is at most the expectation of the absolute value, and every value of $|U-V|$ is at most $\|U-V\|_\infty$.

Step 4, combine Steps 1–3:

$$\begin{aligned}
|(T_MU-T_MV)(s,a)|&=\gamma\,\big|\max_{a'}f(a')-\max_{a'}g(a')\big|\\
&\le\gamma\max_{a'}|f(a')-g(a')|\\
&\le\gamma\max_{a'}\mathbb{E}_{s'}|U(s',a')-V(s',a')|\\
&\le\gamma\|U-V\|_\infty.
\end{aligned}$$

Step 5, the bound holds for every $(s,a)$, so it holds for the maximum over $(s,a)$:

$$\boxed{\|T_MU-T_MV\|_\infty\le\gamma\|U-V\|_\infty}$$

**In words.** Maximizing and averaging never stretch distances; only the discount shrinks them. The order of the maximum and the expectation plays no role in this proof.

**Sanity check.** The same four lines with the expectation outside the maximum prove the usual contraction of the Bellman optimality operator, which is a good sign that the order does not matter here.

**What to write in the exam.**

- The reward cancels, leaving $\gamma$ times a difference of two maxima.
- Use $|\max f-\max g|\le\max|f-g|$.
- Use $|\mathbb{E}X|\le\mathbb{E}|X|$ and bound $|U-V|$ by $\|U-V\|_\infty$.
- Take the maximum over $(s,a)$.

*Verified: on 3000 random MDPs with random $U$, $V$, the largest ratio of $\|T_MU-T_MV\|_\infty$ to $\|U-V\|_\infty$ was $0.8995$, below $\gamma=0.9$.*

**(c) Is the fixed point $Q^*$? No**

**What is asked.** Whether the fixed point $U^*$ of $T_M$ equals $Q^*$ for every MDP. The answer is yes or no; for no, a counterexample with numbers.

**Tool and why.** Compare the two fixed-point equations (cheat sheet §2 and §6). They differ only in the order of the maximum and the expectation, and the interchange inequality says which side is larger. A small stochastic example makes the gap strict.

**Definitions.** Let $T^*$ be the Bellman optimality operator on Q-functions,

$$(T^*Q)(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'}[\max_{a'}Q(s',a')];$$

its fixed point is $Q^*$. The fixed point $U^*$ exists and is unique by part (b) and Banach's theorem.

**Solution.**

Verdict: **No.**

Step 1, the two fixed-point equations:

$$\begin{aligned}U^*(s,a)&=r(s,a)+\gamma\max_{a'}\mathbb{E}_{s'}[U^*(s',a')],\\ Q^*(s,a)&=r(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'}[\max_{a'}Q^*(s',a')].\end{aligned}$$

Step 2, the interchange inequality: for any function $h(s',a')$,

$$\max_{a'}\mathbb{E}_{s'}[h(s',a')]\le\mathbb{E}_{s'}[\max_{a'}h(s',a')],$$

because for each fixed $a'$ we have $h(s',a')\le\max_bh(s',b)$ for every $s'$; taking expectations keeps the inequality, and then we take the maximum over $a'$ on the left.

Step 3, **Claim.** $U^*\le Q^*$ in every entry. Let $U_k$ and $Q_k$ be $T_M$ and $T^*$ applied $k$ times to the zero function; the proof is by induction on $k$, upward from $0$.

**Base.** $U_0$ and $Q_0$ are both $0$.

**Hypothesis.** $U_k\le Q_k$ in every entry.

**Step.** $T_M$ is monotone (a larger $U$ gives larger expectations and larger maxima), so by the hypothesis, and then by Step 2 in the third row,

$$\begin{aligned}
U_{k+1}&=T_MU_k\\
&\le T_MQ_k\\
&\le T^*Q_k\\
&=Q_{k+1}.
\end{aligned}$$

Both operators are contractions, so $U_k$ tends to $U^*$ and $Q_k$ tends to $Q^*$, and the inequality survives the limit.

Step 4, the counterexample: states $s_0,s_1,s_2$, actions $\{1,2\}$, $\gamma=0.9$. From $s_0$ either action pays $0$ and moves to $s_1$ or $s_2$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each. States $s_1$ and $s_2$ are absorbing for both actions. In $s_1$ action $1$ pays $1$ and action $2$ pays $0$; in $s_2$ it is the reverse.

Step 5, $Q^*$: in $s_1$ the best plan is to play action $1$ forever, worth $\tfrac{1}{1-\gamma}$, which is $10$. So $Q^*(s_1,1)=10$, and $Q^*(s_1,2)=0+0.9\cdot10$, which is $9$; symmetrically in $s_2$. At $s_0$, whichever state comes next, the agent then plays the right action:

$$Q^*(s_0,\cdot)=\gamma\cdot\tfrac{1}{1-\gamma}=9.$$

Step 6, $U^*$ on $s_1$ and $s_2$: there the next state is certain, so the maximum outside the expectation is the same as inside, and $U^*$ equals $Q^*$: the values are $10$ and $9$ in $s_1$, and $9$ and $10$ in $s_2$.

Step 7, $U^*$ at $s_0$: $T_M$ must pick one $a'$ for both possible next states:

$$\begin{aligned}
U^*(s_0,\cdot)&=\gamma\max\Big\{\tfrac{10+9}{2},\ \tfrac{9+10}{2}\Big\}\\
&=0.9\cdot9.5\\
&=8.55.
\end{aligned}$$

In closed form this is

$$U^*(s_0,a)=\gamma\cdot\tfrac{1+\gamma}{2(1-\gamma)}=8.55<9$$

so $U^*(s_0,\cdot)$ is strictly below $Q^*(s_0,\cdot)$.

$$\boxed{\text{No: }U^*(s_0,\cdot)=8.55,\ Q^*(s_0,\cdot)=9}$$

Step 8, when they agree: if every transition is deterministic, the expectation is over a single next state and the two equations coincide, so $U^*=Q^*$. More generally they agree exactly when, for every $(s,a)$, one action $a'$ is optimal at every reachable next state.

**In words.** $T_M$ makes the agent choose its next action before seeing where it lands; $Q^*$ lets it choose after. Knowing $s'$ before acting is worth something, so the committed value is lower.

**Sanity check.** The gap $9-8.55$ is $0.45$, which is $\gamma$ times $10-9.5$: the discounted loss from committing to an action that is wrong half the time for one step. It agrees with the claim of Step 3.

**What to write in the exam.**

- Verdict: no; $T_M$ puts the maximum outside the expectation, and $\max_{a'}\mathbb{E}\le\mathbb{E}\max_{a'}$.
- Example: $s_0$ moves to $s_1$ or $s_2$ with probability $\tfrac12$; action $1$ pays in $s_1$, action $2$ pays in $s_2$, both absorbing.
- $Q^*(s_0,\cdot)=9$, but $U^*(s_0,\cdot)=0.9\cdot9.5$, which is $8.55$.

*Verified: value iteration of both operators on the 3-state example gives $8.55$ and $9$ at $s_0$; on 3000 random MDPs $U^*\le Q^*$ held in every entry; on a random deterministic MDP $U^*=Q^*$.*

**Common mistakes.**

- In (b), proving only $\max f-\max g\le\max|f-g|$ and forgetting the other direction of the absolute value.
- In (c), answering yes because $T_M$ is a contraction: a contraction has a unique fixed point, but nothing says that point is $Q^*$.
- Building the counterexample with deterministic transitions, where the two operators coincide and no gap can appear.

**💡 Trick.** The whole question turns on the interchange of $\max_{a'}$ and $\mathbb{E}_{s'}$: the maximum of an expectation is at most the expectation of the maximum. $T_M$ must commit to one $a'$ before seeing $s'$, whereas $Q^*$ picks $a'$ after, so $U^*\le Q^*$ with equality only when the best $a'$ is the same at every reachable $s'$.

**⚠️ Watch out.** The contraction proof still goes through (both use $|\max f-\max g|\le\max|f-g|$ plus $|\mathbb{E}|\le\mathbb{E}|\cdot|$), so being a valid contraction does **not** mean $U^*=Q^*$ — a stochastic transition where the best next action depends on $s'$ makes the inequality strict.
